DAY 129

How to Read, Reproduce & Extend an RL Paper

40m video90m practice

A paper is a compressed claim plus an incomplete recipe. Reading it well means separating the claim from its evidence; reproducing it means recovering the missing parts of the recipe, starting with the baseline, before you change anything at all.

Reading strategy: three passes

Keshav's (2007) three-pass method works well for RL, with a few domain-specific questions added.

  1. Pass one, about ten minutes. Read the title, abstract, introduction, section headings, every figure and its caption, and the conclusion. Write one sentence: what is claimed, compared with what, measured how? Most papers you read should stop here.
  2. Pass two, an hour. Read the method and the experiments, skipping proofs. Which environments, how many seeds, which metric, what kind of error bars? Which baselines, and where did their numbers come from: rerun by the authors, or copied from older papers with different protocols? Is there an ablation that isolates the contribution?
  3. Pass three, as long as it takes. Re-derive the key equations and write pseudocode from memory. List every quantity you would need to reimplement the method, and mark each one as stated, found in the code, or missing.

The hyperparameter appendix (it's always the appendix)

The main text describes the idea; the appendix describes the algorithm that produced the numbers. Look there for network sizes and activations, the optimizer and its \(\epsilon\) (Day 5), learning rates and their schedules, batch and minibatch sizes, replay-buffer size and update-to-data ratio, target-network update rule, \(n\)-step length, \(\gamma\), GAE \(\lambda\), clip ranges, entropy coefficients, the number of parallel environments, frame skip and sticky actions, observation and reward normalization, and the evaluation protocol. When something is missing from the appendix, the next places to look are the released code and its configuration files, the repository's issue tracker (other people have usually asked), and the OpenReview discussion, where reviewers often extract details the paper omits.

Write everything into one configuration file, recording for every value where it came from. This is the single most useful habit in reproduction work, because it turns "I think I matched the paper" into a list you can check. Here it is for PPO on MuJoCo, with the values from Table 3 of Schulman et al. (2017) and the details that live only in the reference code (Huang et al. 2022; Day 92):

Identifying unstated implementation details

Some details are absent from both the paper and the appendix, because the authors never thought of them as choices. They are where most failed reproductions go wrong.

  • Environment version and wrappers. MuJoCo v2, v4 and v5 environments differ; Atari results depend on sticky actions, frame skip, life-loss handling and no-op starts (Day 74).
  • Time limits. Whether truncation is bootstrapped through or treated as termination (Day 8).
  • Action handling. Clipping versus tanh squashing of continuous actions, and the log-probability correction that squashing needs (Day 95).
  • The x-axis. Environment steps or frames (Atari frames are four times the agent's steps at frame skip 4), and whether steps from all parallel environments are counted.
  • Evaluation. A separate evaluation environment or the training episodes; the deterministic mean action or a sample; how many episodes; and which checkpoint: the final one or the best one.
  • Curve processing. Smoothing windows, interpolation onto a common grid, and what the shaded region means.
  • The "±". A standard deviation, a standard error or a confidence interval, over how many seeds? The difference is a factor of \(\sqrt n\) or more.

To find them, run the official code, log everything, and read it alongside the paper: grep for numeric constants, diff its defaults against the appendix, and follow every wrapper the environment passes through.

Reproduce the baseline before claiming an improvement

Algorithm · reproduction protocol
  1. Run the official code, if there is any, unchanged, on one or two of the paper's environments with the paper's budget. Do you get the paper's curve within its reported uncertainty? If not, stop and find out why. The discrepancy is information about the paper or about your setup.
  2. Reimplement (or adopt a trusted reference implementation such as CleanRL, Stable-Baselines3 or the tuned configurations in RL Baselines3 Zoo) and match the official code on the same environments, budget and number of seeds. Compare whole curves, not just final numbers.
  3. Unit-test the parts that are easy to get wrong: returns and GAE on hand-computed examples, the terminated/truncated logic, and the log-probabilities of squashed actions. Check that the agent solves a trivial environment.
  4. Only then change one thing, and compare the change against your own matched baseline: same code, same seeds, same tuning budget.

"Reproduced" should mean something specific. Your mean over seeds falls within the paper's reported uncertainty on most environments, the qualitative ordering of methods is the same, and you report the environments where it isn't. A statistical test is a crude but useful first check, provided you know what the paper's "±" means:

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

def compare_to_reported(mine, reported_mean, reported_pm, reported_n, pm_is="std"):
    """Welch's t-test between your final returns and a paper's 'mean ± pm over n seeds'."""
    x = np.asarray(mine, dtype=float)
    if pm_is == "std":
        sd = reported_pm
    elif pm_is == "sem":                                   # standard error -> std
        sd = reported_pm * np.sqrt(reported_n)
    elif pm_is == "ci95":                                  # t-interval half-width -> std
        sd = reported_pm / stats.t.ppf(0.975, reported_n - 1) * np.sqrt(reported_n)
    else:
        raise ValueError(pm_is)
    res = stats.ttest_ind_from_stats(x.mean(), x.std(ddof=1), len(x),
                                     reported_mean, sd, reported_n, equal_var=False)
    return res.pvalue

mine = [2650.0, 2720.0, 2580.0, 2700.0, 2610.0]           # your 5 seeds, final return
for pm_is in ("std", "sem"):                               # the paper says "3000 ± 150, 5 seeds"
    print(pm_is, round(compare_to_reported(mine, 3000.0, 150.0, 5, pm_is), 3))
# std 0.004: you clearly failed to reproduce · sem 0.08: you are within noise

The same five runs either clearly fail to reproduce the paper or sit within its noise, depending on a detail most papers never state. With five seeds a test like this has little power, so look at the curves as well, and never read "not significant" as "reproduced" on its own.

What breaks · traps in your own reproductions
  • "It matched on one seed." One seed matching is an anecdote; so is one seed failing.
  • Matching the wrong curve. The paper smoothed with a 100-episode window and you didn't, or it reported evaluation returns and you are plotting training returns.
  • Silent version drift. Unpinned dependencies changed the environment or a library default between your baseline runs and your method runs.
  • Fixing the baseline less carefully than your method. Every bug you fix in your method but not in the baseline is a fake improvement.
  • Stopping when the result looks good. Deciding the number of seeds or the training length after seeing the curves biases the result, just like tuning on test seeds.

Extending a paper

Once the baseline is matched, extensions come from questioning the paper rather than adding to it. Vary an assumption it relies on: a different observation space, sparse instead of dense rewards, a non-stationary task. Ablate a component it bundles with others, as Rainbow's ablation did (Day 80). Test a claim on a harder or different benchmark. Measure scaling with model size, data or compute. Characterise where the method fails: a clear account of a failure mode, with the experiments that isolate it, is a contribution. Negative results are worth writing up if the experiments are sound, and with a matched baseline in hand you are in a position to make them sound.

Building a reading list and staying current

  • Seed the list with canonical sources: the papers cited in this course's lessons, Spinning Up's "Key Papers in Deep RL" list, and the syllabi of CS285 and CS234.
  • Expand along citations in both directions: backwards through the related-work section, forwards through "cited by" on Semantic Scholar or Google Scholar. Forward citations find the critiques and the follow-ups that fixed things.
  • Follow the venues: NeurIPS, ICML and ICLR proceedings, the Reinforcement Learning Conference (RLC, held since 2024), CoRL for robot learning, and the arXiv cs.LG listings. Read the OpenReview discussions of papers you care about.
  • Keep a log. For every paper you read past the first pass, write a short structured entry (template below). After a year it is a personal survey of the field.
  • Budget it. A sustainable rhythm is roughly one paper a week read deeply, plus a handful skimmed at the first pass.

DAY 130

Capstone — Scope, Build, Evaluate, Publish

50m video600m+ practiceCapstone

The capstone is one honest experiment, done completely: a question small enough to answer with the compute you have, a reproduced baseline, a plan fixed before the runs, statistics that survive scrutiny, and a repository that lets someone else get your numbers.

Choosing a project

Start from a question, not a method: "does X matter for Y under conditions Z?" A project that compares against a matched baseline is worth more than one that introduces a new method without one. Three shapes work well.

Scoping to the compute you actually have

Measure before you plan. Run a short pilot of each configuration, around 5% of its budget, record environment steps per second, and only then do the arithmetic. Leave a factor of two for debugging and reruns, because you will need it.

def budget(n_envs, n_variants, n_seeds, steps_per_run, steps_per_sec, parallel_runs):
    """Total runs and wall-clock hours for a sweep; steps_per_sec comes from a pilot run."""
    runs = n_envs * n_variants * n_seeds
    hours = runs * steps_per_run / steps_per_sec / 3600 / parallel_runs
    return runs, hours

runs, hours = budget(n_envs=3, n_variants=6, n_seeds=10, steps_per_run=1_000_000,
                     steps_per_sec=1_500, parallel_runs=8)
print(f"{runs} runs, about {hours:.1f} h of wall-clock")   # 180 runs, about 4.2 h

1,500 steps per second is a plausible figure for CPU PPO on a small MuJoCo task, but it varies severalfold with hardware, network size and environment, so replace it with your own measurement.

If the number is too large, cut in this order: fewer environments (three well-chosen ones beat ten shallow ones), shorter runs (stop where the curves have separated), and cheaper simulation. Accelerator-native environments (Brax, MJX, gymnax) let entire training runs, many seeds at once, execute on a GPU, as in PureJaxRL. For language-model projects, use models of at most about 1B parameters, and LoRA if memory is tight. Do not cut seeds below five; a result you cannot distinguish from noise is not cheaper, only worse.

The experiment plan and ablation design

Write the plan down and commit it to the repository before the main runs. This is pre-registration in miniature, and it protects you from the most common self-deceptions of Day 7: choosing seeds, metrics or training lengths after seeing the curves.

# EXPERIMENT PLAN · committed before the main runs on YYYY-MM-DD
Question:        Does <component or method> improve <metric> on <tasks> at <budget>?
Hypothesis:      <direction and rough size>, because <mechanism>
Falsified if:    the 95% CI of the IQM difference contains 0 on every task
Variants:        full | -A | -B | -C | baseline            (exactly one change each)
Environments:    <3-5, with exact versions>
Budget:          <env steps per run>; evaluate every <k> steps, <m> episodes, deterministic policy
Seeds:           tuning seeds 100-104; reporting seeds 0-9 (never used for tuning)
Tuning:          the same budget for every variant, or none for any
Primary metric:  final return = mean of the last 5 evaluations
Secondary:       area under the learning curve; wall-clock time
Statistics:      IQM with 95% stratified bootstrap CIs; probability of improvement vs full
Figures:         learning curves vs env steps (95% bands); IQM with CIs per variant

Ablations. Leave-one-out (remove each component from the full method) asks whether a component is necessary given the rest; add-one-in (add each to a bare baseline) asks whether it suffices on its own. Components interact, so the two designs can disagree, and you should say which one you ran. Rainbow's ablation (Day 80) is leave-one-out. When two or three components are cheap to toggle, a full \(2^k\) factorial design measures their interactions directly. Every variant gets the same tuning budget, or none gets any.

Evaluation protocol with proper statistics

The protocol is Day 7's experiment hygiene (Day 7) plus Day 128's checklist (Day 128). Evaluate at fixed step intervals in a separate environment, for a fixed number of episodes, with the policy mode stated. To aggregate across tasks, first normalize each task's scores, for example \((\text{score} - \text{random}) / (\text{reference} - \text{random})\). Then report the interquartile mean, which is robust to a few outlier runs yet uses far more of the data than the median; stratified bootstrap confidence intervals, which resample seeds within each task; and, for head-to-head claims, the probability of improvement \(P(X > Y)\) that a random run of one method beats a random run of the other (Agarwal et al. 2021). Colas et al. (2018) show how to choose the number of seeds by power analysis; for differences of modest size the answer is often more than the five most papers use.

import numpy as np

def iqm(x):
    """Interquartile mean: drop the lowest and highest 25% of scores, average the rest."""
    x = np.sort(np.ravel(x))
    k = int(0.25 * len(x))
    return x[k:len(x) - k].mean()

def stratified_bootstrap_ci(scores, stat=iqm, n_boot=5000, alpha=0.05, seed=0):
    """scores: [n_tasks, n_seeds] normalized final scores; resample seeds within each task."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    scores = np.asarray(scores, dtype=float)
    n_tasks, n_seeds = scores.shape
    boots = np.empty(n_boot)
    for b in range(n_boot):
        idx = rng.integers(0, n_seeds, size=(n_tasks, n_seeds))
        boots[b] = stat(np.take_along_axis(scores, idx, axis=1))
    lo, hi = np.quantile(boots, [alpha / 2, 1 - alpha / 2])
    return stat(scores), lo, hi

def prob_improvement(x, y):
    """P(X > Y) on one task from the final scores of two sets of runs; ties count half."""
    x, y = np.asarray(x)[:, None], np.asarray(y)[None, :]
    return (x > y).mean() + 0.5 * (x == y).mean()

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
A = rng.normal(0.80, 0.15, size=(3, 10))      # method A: 3 tasks x 10 seeds, normalized scores
B = rng.normal(0.70, 0.15, size=(3, 10))      # method B
for name, s in (("A", A), ("B", B)):
    est, lo, hi = stratified_bootstrap_ci(s)
    print(f"{name}: IQM {est:.2f}  95% CI [{lo:.2f}, {hi:.2f}]")
print("P(A > B) per task:", np.round([prob_improvement(a, b) for a, b in zip(A, B)], 2))
# A: IQM 0.81 [0.77, 0.84] · B: IQM 0.69 [0.64, 0.74] · P(A > B): 0.71, 0.80, 0.66

The trimming matches scipy.stats.trim_mean(x, 0.25), and the rliable library implements the same estimators with more options. Note that a clear aggregate difference still leaves a one-in-three chance that a random run of B beats a random run of A on some tasks. That is what "the improvement is real but modest" looks like.

A default capstone: what matters in PPO?

If you can't choose, do this one. The goal is to measure which of PPO's code-level details (Day 92) matter on continuous control, with enough seeds to be sure.

  • Implementation. Your Project 7 PPO, or CleanRL's ppo_continuous_action.py, as the full configuration, with the hyperparameters of Day 129's configuration file.
  • Environments. Hopper-v5, Walker2d-v5 and HalfCheetah-v5. Without MuJoCo, use Pendulum-v1, LunarLanderContinuous-v3 and BipedalWalker-v3.
  • Variants (6). Full; no observation normalization; no advantage normalization; no value-loss clipping; no learning-rate annealing; PyTorch's default initialization instead of orthogonal.
  • Budget and evaluation. 1M environment steps per run. Every 20k steps, run 10 episodes with the deterministic mean action in a separate environment that uses the training run's frozen observation-normalization statistics.
  • Seeds. 10 reporting seeds per environment and variant, 180 runs in all. No variant is retuned; state this as a limitation, since a detail that hurts at the full configuration's hyperparameters might help at others.
  • Plots. (1) Learning curves per environment, full against each ablation, with 95% bootstrap bands. (2) IQM of normalized final score per variant across environments, with stratified bootstrap CIs. (3) Probability of improvement of the full configuration over each ablation.

Expected results, honestly. First check that your full configuration matches the reference implementation's published curves for the same environment versions, within seed noise; if it doesn't, everything after is suspect. Then expect observation normalization to be among the details that matter most on MuJoCo (Andrychowicz et al. 2021 recommend always using it), value-loss clipping to make little difference or to hurt slightly (as reported by Engstrom et al. 2020 and Andrychowicz et al. 2021), and at least one ablation whose interval overlaps the full configuration on every task. Effects that differ between environments are common, so report them per environment as well as in aggregate. If 10 seeds cannot separate an effect, say so: that is a finding about effect size, not a failed project.

What breaks · capstone failure modes
  • Scope creep. A fourth environment or a seventh variant halves your seeds. Finish the plan you committed, then extend it.
  • No matched baseline. Comparing your method against numbers from a paper run on different versions and hardware.
  • Asymmetric effort. Bugs fixed and hyperparameters tuned for your variant only.
  • Moving the goalposts. Changing the metric, the training length or the seed count after seeing results. If you must, report both the planned and the changed analysis.
  • An unrunnable repository. It works only on your machine, with your paths and your unpinned library versions.

Practice · 600+ min

  2. Baseline (120 min). Reproduce the reference implementation's result, write unit tests for returns, advantages and wrappers, and keep the comparison plot.
  3. Plan (30 min). Fill in the experiment plan and commit it.
  4. Run (180+ min, mostly compute). Launch the sweep. While it runs, write the setup section, the hyperparameter table and the figure scripts.
  5. Analyse (90 min). Make the figures, the IQM table with CIs, the probability-of-improvement comparisons and the ablation chart.
  6. Write up (90 min). Follow the template, including limitations and negative results.
  7. Publish (30 min). Write the README, do the fresh-clone test, and tag a release.
