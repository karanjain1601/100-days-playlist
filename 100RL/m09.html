DAY 82

Why Learn a Policy Directly

40m video75m practice

Value-based methods learn \(q\) and derive a policy by maximizing over actions. Policy-based methods parameterize \(\pi(a \mid s, \theta)\) and climb the expected return \(J(\theta)\) directly. That buys continuous actions, stochastic optimal policies and smooth improvement, at the price of noisy gradient estimates that the rest of this module works to tame.

What value-based methods cannot do

Modules 5–8 all follow one recipe: estimate action values, then act (ε-)greedily on them. The policy is never represented explicitly; it is whatever \(\argmax_a Q(s, a)\) happens to be. That recipe has structural limits that no amount of tuning removes:

  • The argmax is an optimization problem. With a few discrete actions it is a lookup. With continuous actions it is an inner optimization over \(a\), solved at every step and inside every target.
  • Greedy policies are deterministic. ε-greedy adds uniform noise, but it cannot represent “go left with probability 0.59”. Some problems need exactly that.
  • The policy jumps. An arbitrarily small change in \(Q\) can flip the argmax, so the policy, and with it the data distribution, changes discontinuously. With function approximation this is one source of the oscillation and divergence of Day 69.
  • The value function can be harder than the policy. In many control tasks “push towards the target” is simple while the exact cost-to-go surface is intricate. Learning the policy directly lets you spend capacity on what you need, and lets you build prior knowledge into its form.

Continuous action spaces

A robot arm's torques, a car's steering angle and a portfolio's weights are vectors in \(\R^d\). DQN needs one output per action, so you would have to discretize: \(k\) levels in each of \(d\) dimensions gives \(k^d\) outputs. A 7-joint arm with 10 levels per joint needs \(10^7\) heads, and the resolution is still coarse. A policy network instead outputs the parameters of a distribution over \(\R^d\), for example a mean and a standard deviation per dimension: \(2d\) numbers, whatever the resolution. Days 93–95 return to the alternative of keeping a critic and making the argmax differentiable.

Stochastic optimal policies

In a fully observed MDP there is always a deterministic optimal policy (Days 24–25). Two things break that guarantee. In games, the optimal strategy can be mixed: in rock–paper–scissors any deterministic policy is exploited, and the equilibrium is uniform. Under state aliasing, where different states look identical to the agent, a deterministic policy must take the same action in all of them.

Example · the aliased gridworld

David Silver's lecture example: a row of five cells with gold below the middle one and skulls below the two ends. The agent only observes which walls surround it, so the second and fourth cells look identical. A deterministic policy must move the same way in both grey cells; from one side it then walks back and forth forever. A stochastic policy that moves east or west with probability ½ in the grey cells reaches the gold in a few steps on average. The best value-based policy is the deterministic one; the best policy is not.

Sutton & Barto's Example 13.1 makes this quantitative. Three states and a goal, reward \(-1\) per step. In the second state the effect of the actions is reversed. All states share the same features, so the agent cannot tell them apart and its policy is a single number, the probability \(p\) of choosing “right”.

import numpy as np

def corridor_value(p):
    """S&B Example 13.1: start-state value when 'right' is chosen with probability p everywhere."""
    # states 0, 1, 2 (state 1 has its actions reversed); reward -1 per step; the goal is absorbing
    M = np.array([[1 - p, p,     0.0  ],     # M[s, s'] = Pr(s -> s') among non-terminal states
                  [p,     0.0,   1 - p],
                  [0.0,   1 - p, 0.0  ]])
    return np.linalg.solve(np.eye(3) - M, -np.ones(3))[0]

for name, p in [("eps-greedy left", 0.05), ("uniform", 0.5), ("eps-greedy right", 0.95)]:
    print(f"{name:17s} p={p:.2f}  v={corridor_value(p):7.2f}")

sigmoid = lambda z: 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))         # two actions: softmax = sigmoid of the preference gap
theta = -3.0                                     # start near "almost always left"
for step in range(300):                          # gradient ascent on J(theta) = v(start)
    h = 1e-5
    grad = (corridor_value(sigmoid(theta + h)) - corridor_value(sigmoid(theta - h))) / (2 * h)
    theta += 0.02 * grad
print(f"learned p(right) = {sigmoid(theta):.3f}, v = {corridor_value(sigmoid(theta)):.2f}")  # 0.586, -11.66

The gradient here is computed by finite differences on the exact value, which needs the model. Day 83 shows how to get the same gradient from sampled episodes alone.

Smoother convergence

With a continuous parameterization, action probabilities change smoothly with \(\theta\). With ε-greedy they can change completely after an arbitrarily small change in the value estimates. Largely because of this, policy-gradient methods have stronger convergence guarantees: with suitable step sizes, stochastic gradient ascent on \(J\) converges to a local optimum even with function approximation (Sutton et al. 2000), whereas control with approximate action values can chatter or diverge.

A second, subtler advantage (S&B §13.1): with a softmax over learned preferences, the preferences are not pushed towards any particular values, so the policy can approach a deterministic one when that is optimal or settle at a stochastic one when that is optimal. A softmax over action values at a fixed temperature can do neither: the values converge to their true values, which fixes the action probabilities at some arbitrary intermediate level.

Softmax policies for discrete actions

Definition · softmax in action preferences $$\pi(a \mid s, \theta) = \frac{e^{h(s, a, \theta)}}{\sum_b e^{h(s, b, \theta)}},$$

where \(h\) is any differentiable preference function: linear, \(h = \theta^\top \mathbf x(s, a)\), or the logits output by a network. Every action keeps positive probability, which is what lets the score function below exist.

Derivation · the softmax score function $$\ln \pi(a \mid s, \theta) = h(s, a, \theta) - \ln \sum_b e^{h(s, b, \theta)} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s, \theta) = \nabla h(s, a, \theta) - \sum_b \pi(b \mid s, \theta)\, \nabla h(s, b, \theta).$$

For linear preferences this is \(\mathbf x(s, a) - \sum_b \pi(b \mid s, \theta)\, \mathbf x(s, b)\): the chosen action's features minus their policy average (S&B Exercise 13.3). For a network, with respect to the logits it is \(\mathbf e_a - \pi(\cdot \mid s)\): push the chosen logit up and every logit down in proportion to its probability.

Gaussian policies for continuous actions

Definition · Gaussian policy $$\pi(a \mid s, \theta) = \frac{1}{\sigma(s, \theta)\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(a - \mu(s, \theta))^2}{2\sigma(s, \theta)^2}\Big),$$

with the mean \(\mu\) from a network or linear function, and \(\sigma\) kept positive by parameterizing \(\log \sigma\) (or with a softplus). For \(d\)-dimensional actions the standard choice is a diagonal Gaussian: \(d\) independent components, whose log-densities add.

Derivation · the Gaussian score function $$\ln \pi = -\ln \sigma - \tfrac12 \ln 2\pi - \frac{(a - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial \ln \pi}{\partial \mu} = \frac{a - \mu}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial \ln \pi}{\partial \log \sigma} = \frac{(a - \mu)^2}{\sigma^2} - 1.$$

Chain these through the network. The \(\mu\) term pulls the mean towards actions that did well; the \(\log\sigma\) term widens the policy when good actions were far from the mean and narrows it when they were close. Note the \(1/\sigma^2\): as the policy sharpens, its score grows without bound. That is one reason step sizes in parameter space are a poor idea (Day 89).

Two common designs: a state-dependent \(\sigma(s)\) as a second network head (SAC, Day 95), or a state-independent learned vector \(\log\sigma\) (PPO's usual choice, Day 92). Real actuators have bounds, and a Gaussian has unbounded support, so you either clip the sample before sending it to the environment (PPO) or squash it through \(\tanh\) and correct the density (SAC).

The objective J(θ)

Definition · performance measures
  • Episodic: \(J(\theta) = v_{\pi_\theta}(s_0)\), or \(\E_{S_0 \sim p_0}\big[v_{\pi_\theta}(S_0)\big]\) with a start distribution. Equivalently \(J(\theta) = \E_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}[G_0]\), the expected return of a whole trajectory \(\tau = (S_0, A_0, R_1, S_1, \dots)\).
  • Continuing: the average reward \(r(\pi) = \lim_{h \to \infty} \frac1h \sum_{t=1}^{h} \E[R_t] = \sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a)\, r\), with \(\mu\) the stationary state distribution (S&B §13.6, Day 68).

Policy-gradient methods do stochastic gradient ascent: \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t + \alpha\, \widehat{\nabla J(\theta_t)}\), where \(\widehat{\nabla J}\) is a stochastic estimate whose expectation approximates the true gradient (S&B eq. 13.1).

The difficulty is visible in the definition: \(J\) depends on \(\theta\) through the actions chosen and through the states those actions lead to, and the second dependence runs through the unknown dynamics. Day 83 shows that the gradient can nonetheless be estimated from experience alone. Note also that \(J\) is not concave in \(\theta\) for any interesting parameterization, so “converges” always means “to a local optimum”.

What breaks · the costs of learning a policy
  • Variance. Gradient estimates built from sampled returns are very noisy. Days 84–88 are mostly about reducing that noise.
  • Sample efficiency. Vanilla policy gradients are on-policy: every update needs fresh data from the current policy, and old data is thrown away. Days 90–95 recover some reuse.
  • Premature determinism. A softmax or Gaussian can collapse onto one action before exploring enough, after which its gradients vanish. Entropy bonuses (Day 86) and entropy-regularized objectives (Day 95) push back.
  • Local optima and plateaus. Gradient ascent on a non-concave \(J\) finds whatever basin it starts in.

DAY 83

The Policy Gradient Theorem

50m video90m practice

The gradient of expected return can be written as an expectation over the policy's own experience, \(\nabla J(\theta) = \E\big[\sum_t \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\, G_t\big]\), with no derivative of the dynamics and no derivative of the state distribution. That one fact is what makes model-free policy optimization possible.

The problem

Changing \(\theta\) changes which actions are taken, which changes which states are visited, which changes which actions are taken there, and so on. The first effect is easy to differentiate, since we wrote \(\pi\) down. The second runs through \(p(s' \mid s, a)\), which we don't know. A naive chain rule would need \(\partial \mu(s) / \partial \theta\), the sensitivity of the state distribution to the parameters, which is a property of the environment. The policy gradient theorem says we never need it.

Derivation 1: the log-derivative trick on trajectories

Treat a whole episode \(\tau = (S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_T)\) as one random object. Its probability under \(\pi_\theta\) factorizes:

$$p_\theta(\tau) = p_0(S_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\; p(S_{t+1}, R_{t+1} \mid S_t, A_t).$$
Derivation · score function on trajectories

Let \(R(\tau) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} R_{t+1}\) (take \(\gamma = 1\) for now). Using \(\nabla p = p\, \nabla \ln p\) as on Day 4:

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \nabla \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) = \sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)\, \nabla \ln p_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) = \E_\tau\big[R(\tau)\, \nabla \ln p_\theta(\tau)\big].$$

Now take the log of the factorization. The start distribution and the dynamics do not depend on \(\theta\), so their gradients are zero:

$$\nabla \ln p_\theta(\tau) = \underbrace{\nabla \ln p_0(S_0)}_{0} + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta) + \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \underbrace{\nabla \ln p(S_{t+1}, R_{t+1} \mid S_t, A_t)}_{0}.$$ $$\boxed{\;\nabla J(\theta) = \E_\tau\Big[R(\tau) \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\Big]\;}$$

The assumptions are mild but real: \(\pi\) is differentiable in \(\theta\), its support doesn't depend on \(\theta\) (so you can move \(\nabla\) inside the sum or integral), and \(R(\tau)\) is bounded. Nothing is assumed about the reward or the dynamics, which can be discontinuous black boxes. Day 85 shows that each \(\nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\) only needs the rewards that come after \(A_t\), which turns \(R(\tau)\) into \(G_t\). The second derivation reaches that form directly.

Derivation 2: Sutton & Barto's proof from the Bellman equation

The episodic proof of S&B §13.2, with \(\gamma = 1\) and \(J(\theta) = v_\pi(s_0)\). All gradients are with respect to \(\theta\), and \(\pi\) depends on \(\theta\) throughout.

Proof · policy gradient theorem (episodic case)

Step 1: a recursion for the gradient. Start from \(v_\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s, a)\) and apply the product rule, then the Bellman equation for \(q_\pi\):

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla v_\pi(s) &= \sum_a \Big[\nabla \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s, a) + \pi(a \mid s)\, \nabla q_\pi(s, a)\Big] \\ &= \sum_a \Big[\nabla \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s, a) + \pi(a \mid s)\, \nabla \sum_{s', r} p(s', r \mid s, a)\big(r + v_\pi(s')\big)\Big] \\ &= \underbrace{\sum_a \nabla \pi(a \mid s)\, q_\pi(s, a)}_{\phi(s)} \;+\; \sum_{s'} \underbrace{\sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, p(s' \mid s, a)}_{P_\pi(s' \mid s)}\, \nabla v_\pi(s'). \end{aligned}$$

The third line uses the fact that \(p\) and \(r\) do not depend on \(\theta\), so \(\nabla\) passes straight through to \(v_\pi(s')\).

Step 2: unroll. The result has the shape of a Bellman equation, \(\nabla v_\pi = \phi + P_\pi \nabla v_\pi\), with the vector-valued “reward” \(\phi\). Substitute it into itself repeatedly:

$$\nabla v_\pi(s) = \phi(s) + \sum_{s'} P_\pi(s' \mid s)\, \phi(s') + \sum_{s''} P^2_\pi(s'' \mid s)\, \phi(s'') + \cdots = \sum_{x \in \mathcal S} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \Pr(s \to x, k, \pi)\, \phi(x),$$

where \(\Pr(s \to x, k, \pi)\) is the probability of being in \(x\) after \(k\) steps from \(s\). The series converges because episodes terminate.

Step 3: normalize. Let \(\eta(x) = \sum_k \Pr(s_0 \to x, k, \pi)\), the expected number of visits to \(x\) per episode, and \(\mu(x) = \eta(x) / \sum_{x'} \eta(x')\), the on-policy distribution:

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \nabla v_\pi(s_0) = \sum_x \eta(x)\, \phi(x) = \Big(\sum_{x'} \eta(x')\Big) \sum_x \mu(x)\, \phi(x) \;\propto\; \sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a q_\pi(s, a)\, \nabla \pi(a \mid s, \theta). \qquad \blacksquare$$

Why the state distribution's gradient never appears

In Derivation 1 the reason is mechanical: the state distribution is generated by \(p_0\) and \(p(s' \mid s, a)\), which are \(\theta\)-free factors of \(p_\theta(\tau)\), so their logs have zero gradient. Every way \(\theta\) affects which states you visit goes through earlier action probabilities, and those scores are already in the sum.

Derivation 2 says the same thing in value-function language. Changing the policy at \(s\) does change where you go next, but the value of going there is exactly what \(q_\pi(s, a)\) measures. The term \(\nabla q_\pi(s, a)\) in Step 1, which is where a \(\nabla\mu\) would have to hide, turns into \(\nabla v_\pi\) at the successor states, and unrolling converts it into local terms at those states. The effect of \(\theta\) on the state distribution is fully accounted for by crediting downstream returns to upstream actions. That is why the theorem only needs samples from \(\mu\), never its derivative.

The score function

Lemma · the score has zero mean

For any state \(s\), \(\E_{A \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)}\big[\nabla \ln \pi(A \mid s, \theta)\big] = \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\, \frac{\nabla \pi(a \mid s)}{\pi(a \mid s)} = \nabla \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = \nabla 1 = 0.\)

\(\nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s, \theta)\), the score function (S&B call it the eligibility vector), is the direction in parameter space that most increases the log-probability of taking \(a\) in \(s\). Its zero mean is behind the baseline result of Day 85, and its covariance \(\E[\nabla \ln \pi\, \nabla \ln \pi^\top]\) is the Fisher information matrix of Day 89. For softmax it is \(\mathbf e_a - \pi\) in logit space, for a Gaussian \((a - \mu)/\sigma^2\) in mean space (Day 82).

The final form

The state-weighted sum is not something you can sample directly, so S&B §13.3 rewrites it as an expectation over actions using \(\nabla \pi = \pi\, \nabla \ln \pi\), then replaces \(q_\pi(S_t, A_t)\) by its unbiased sample \(G_t\) (because \(\E_\pi[G_t \mid S_t, A_t] = q_\pi(S_t, A_t)\)):

$$\nabla J(\theta) \propto \E_\pi\Big[\sum_a q_\pi(S_t, a)\, \nabla \pi(a \mid S_t, \theta)\Big] = \E_\pi\big[q_\pi(S_t, A_t)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\big] = \E_\pi\big[G_t\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\big].$$

Summing over an episode instead of normalizing by \(\eta\) removes the proportionality constant. Since \(\sum_x \eta(x)\, \phi(x) = \E\big[\sum_t \phi(S_t)\big]\), the exact trajectory form is

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \E_\tau\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\, G_t\Big] \quad (\gamma = 1), \qquad \nabla J(\theta) = \E_\tau\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\, G_t\Big] \quad (\gamma < 1).$$

With discounting, Step 1 picks up a factor \(\gamma\) on the recursion, so the state weights become the discounted visitation \(d_\gamma(x) = \sum_k \gamma^k \Pr(s_0 \to x, k, \pi)\), that is \(\nabla v = (I - \gamma P_\pi)^{-1}\phi\), the Neumann series of Day 4. The \(\gamma^t\) is easy to forget and almost always dropped in practice; Day 84 says what that costs.

Policy gradient as weighted maximum likelihood

Supervised learning of a policy from labelled pairs \((s_i, a_i)\) (behaviour cloning, Day 106) ascends \(\sum_i \nabla \ln \pi(a_i \mid s_i, \theta)\). The sample policy gradient is the same expression with each term weighted by the return that followed:

$$\widehat{\nabla J} = \frac1N \sum_{i} G_i\, \nabla \ln \pi(a_i \mid s_i, \theta).$$

So policy-gradient learning is supervised learning on the agent's own actions, with labels it generated itself, weighted by how well each action turned out: actions followed by high return become more likely, actions followed by low return less so. In code you build the pseudo-loss \(L(\theta) = -\frac1N \sum_i G_i \ln \pi(a_i \mid s_i, \theta)\), a return-weighted cross-entropy whose autograd gradient is \(-\widehat{\nabla J}\). Its value is not the performance and need not go down as the agent improves; never use it as a learning curve.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
S, A, gamma = 5, 3, 0.9
P = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(S), size=(S, A))    # P[s, a, s']
R = rng.normal(size=(S, A))                    # r(s, a)
p0 = np.full(S, 1 / S)                         # start-state distribution

def evaluate(theta):
    """Exact J, v, q and discounted visitation d for a tabular softmax policy."""
    pi = np.exp(theta - theta.max(1, keepdims=True))
    pi /= pi.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    P_pi = np.einsum("sa,sat->st", pi, P)
    M = np.eye(S) - gamma * P_pi
    v = np.linalg.solve(M, (pi * R).sum(1))
    q = R + gamma * P @ v                      # [S, A]
    d = np.linalg.solve(M.T, p0)               # d(s) = sum_k gamma^k Pr(S_k = s)
    return pi, v, q, d, p0 @ v

theta = rng.normal(size=(S, A))
pi, v, q, d, J = evaluate(theta)

# The theorem for tabular softmax: dJ/dtheta[s,a] = d(s) * pi(a|s) * (q(s,a) - v(s))
grad_pgt = d[:, None] * pi * (q - v[:, None])

grad_fd = np.zeros_like(theta)                 # central finite differences on the exact J
for s in range(S):
    for a in range(A):
        e = np.zeros_like(theta); e[s, a] = 1e-5
        grad_fd[s, a] = (evaluate(theta + e)[-1] - evaluate(theta - e)[-1]) / 2e-5

print(np.abs(grad_pgt - grad_fd).max())       # about 2e-10: the theorem is exact

For tabular softmax, \(\sum_a \nabla_{\theta_{s,b}} \pi(a \mid s)\, q(s, a) = \pi(b \mid s)\big(q(s, b) - v(s)\big)\), so the theorem predicts \(d(s)\,\pi(b \mid s)\,A_\pi(s, b)\). Notice the advantage appearing on its own: that is Day 85's baseline, for free.

What breaks
  • Wrong state distribution. The theorem holds for states drawn from the current policy's \(\mu\) (or \(d_\gamma\)). Gradients computed on a replay buffer or on data from an older policy estimate something else. Off-policy policy gradients need importance weights (Day 39) or a trust region (Days 90–91).
  • Deterministic policies. The score function needs a density with \(\theta\)-independent support. A deterministic \(\mu_\theta(s)\) has neither, which is why it gets its own theorem (Day 93).
  • The proportionality constant. The “∝” hides the average episode length. It is absorbed into the step size, but it means the scale of \(\widehat{\nabla J}\) changes as episodes get longer.
  • Reading the pseudo-loss. It changes with the batch's returns, not with how good the policy is.

REINFORCE

40m video105m practice

REINFORCE is the policy gradient theorem with every expectation replaced by one sampled episode: run the policy, compute each step's return, and nudge each action's log-probability in proportion to it. It is unbiased and simple, and its estimates are so noisy that most of this module exists to fix them.

Monte Carlo policy gradient

Day 83 gave \(\nabla J(\theta) = \E_\tau\big[\sum_t \gamma^t G_t\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\big]\). Every quantity inside the expectation is observable after an episode ends: the actions, the states, the rewards that make up \(G_t\), and the score, which we can differentiate because we wrote \(\pi\) down. So the Monte Carlo estimate from \(N\) episodes,

$$\widehat{\nabla J} = \frac1N \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{t=0}^{T_i - 1} \gamma^t\, G^{(i)}_t\, \nabla \ln \pi\big(A^{(i)}_t \mid S^{(i)}_t, \theta\big),$$

is unbiased, and stochastic gradient ascent with it converges to a local optimum under the usual Robbins–Monro step sizes (Day 5). Williams (1992) introduced the family and the name: REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor × Offset Reinforcement × Characteristic Eligibility, that is, step size × (return − baseline) × score.

The algorithm, step by step

Algorithm · REINFORCE (S&B §13.3)
  1. Input: a differentiable policy \(\pi(a \mid s, \theta)\); step size \(\alpha > 0\). Initialize \(\theta\).
  2. Loop for each episode:
    1. Generate \(S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T\) by following \(\pi(\cdot \mid \cdot, \theta)\).
    2. Loop for \(t = 0, 1, \dots, T-1\):
      1. \(G \leftarrow \sum_{k=t+1}^{T} \gamma^{k-t-1} R_k\) (computed for all t in one backward pass)
      2. \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, \gamma^t\, G\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\)

Read the update as three factors. The score says which direction in parameter space makes \(A_t\) more likely in \(S_t\). The return says how far to go: actions followed by large returns are reinforced more. Dividing by \(\pi\) (inside the log) stops frequently chosen actions from winning merely because they are updated more often. Without it, an action's total push would scale with its probability rather than with its value.

Sample-based gradient estimation in code

Autograd does the differentiation: build the pseudo-loss of Day 83, \(L = -\sum_t \gamma^t G_t \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\), and call backward(). Three choices change the effective step size even though they don't change the direction: summing over time steps or averaging over them (the sum scales with episode length, which grows as CartPole improves); one episode per update or a batch of several; and whether the return is a tensor that requires grad (it must not, or you are differentiating the data).

import gymnasium as gym
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.distributions import Categorical

torch.manual_seed(0)
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
policy = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64, 2))     # outputs logits
opt = torch.optim.Adam(policy.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
gamma = 0.99

for episode in range(1000):
    obs, _ = env.reset(seed=episode)
    logps, rewards, done = [], [], False
    while not done:
        dist = Categorical(logits=policy(torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32)))
        action = dist.sample()
        logps.append(dist.log_prob(action))
        obs, reward, terminated, truncated, _ = env.step(action.item())
        rewards.append(reward)
        done = terminated or truncated      # pure MC cannot bootstrap, so truncation also ends it

    G, returns = 0.0, []
    for r in reversed(rewards):             # G_t = R_{t+1} + gamma * G_{t+1}
        G = r + gamma * G
        returns.append(G)
    returns = torch.tensor(returns[::-1])   # plain data: no gradient flows through returns
    discounts = gamma ** torch.arange(len(rewards))       # S&B's gamma^t; most code drops it
    loss = -(discounts * returns * torch.stack(logps)).sum()   # gradient of loss = -grad J estimate

    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    if episode % 50 == 0:
        print(f"episode {episode:4d}  return {sum(rewards):5.0f}")

A complete REINFORCE agent. On a good seed the return climbs towards the 500-step cap over several hundred episodes, then falls back, recovers and falls again; some seeds barely learn at all. That wobble is the subject of the next section and of Day 85.

The high-variance problem, quantified

The estimate is unbiased, so its error is all variance: \(\Var[\widehat{\nabla J}] = \frac1N \Var[g(\tau)]\) for a per-episode estimate \(g(\tau)\). Three sources make \(\Var[g(\tau)]\) large.

Derivation · where the variance comes from

1. Score terms add up. Write \(s_t = \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\). Conditioned on the history up to \(S_t\), \(\E[s_t \mid H_t] = 0\) (Day 83's lemma), so \(s_0, s_1, \dots\) are martingale differences and are uncorrelated. Hence \(\E\big\|\sum_t s_t\big\|^2 = \sum_t \E\|s_t\|^2\), which grows linearly with the horizon.

2. Returns are large. Each term is multiplied by a return of size up to \(T R_{\max}\). Crudely, the second moment of \(R(\tau)\sum_t s_t\) grows like \(T^2 \cdot T = T^3\).

3. Offsets cost variance but buy nothing. Add a constant \(c\) to every return (CartPole's +1 per step makes every return positive). Since \(\E[c\, s] = 0\), the mean is unchanged, but for a single step

$$\E\big[(G + c)^2 \|s\|^2\big] - \|\nabla J\|^2 = \Var[G s] + 2c\, \E\big[G \|s\|^2\big] + c^2\, \E\|s\|^2,$$

so the variance grows like \(c^2 \operatorname{tr} F\), with \(F = \E[s s^\top]\) the Fisher matrix. With all-positive returns every sampled action gets pushed up, and the useful signal is only the small difference between how hard each one is pushed.

A useful summary is the signal-to-noise ratio \(\|\E g\|^2 / \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}[g]\). To get the gradient's direction right to relative error \(\epsilon\) you need roughly \(N \approx \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}[g] / (\epsilon^2 \|\nabla J\|^2)\) episodes. Ilyas et al. (2020) measured this in deep policy-gradient agents and found the gradient estimates at standard batch sizes are poorly correlated with the true gradient. On CartPole you can measure it directly (Day 85's snippet does exactly that).

Discounting subtleties

Three separate issues hide in the \(\gamma\)s.

  • The \(\gamma^t\) factor. It is part of the exact gradient of \(J(\theta) = \E[G_0]\) for the discounted objective. Almost every implementation drops it, weighting a decision at step 400 as much as one at step 0. The resulting update is a policy gradient for a different weighting of states, and Nota & Thomas (2020) show it is not the gradient of any objective; Thomas (2014) showed the same bias in natural actor-critic algorithms. It works in practice because \(\gamma\) is being used as a variance-reduction device (ignore rewards far in the future) rather than as part of the objective; Schulman et al. (2016) adopt exactly that interpretation.
  • Discounted versus undiscounted performance. Train with \(\gamma = 0.99\), report undiscounted episode return. They are different objectives, and \(\gamma\) is a hyperparameter you are tuning, not a property of the task.
  • Truncation. A Monte Carlo return cannot bootstrap, so on a time-limited episode \(G_t\) near the limit undercounts the true return. With CartPole's 500-step cap, late-episode actions look worse than they are. Fixes: bootstrap from a critic (Day 86), use a limit much longer than \(1/(1-\gamma)\), or add the remaining time to the observation (Pardo et al. 2018).
What breaks · watching REINFORCE wobble
  • Collapse after success. Once the policy reaches 500, every return is near-maximal and similar, so the gradient is dominated by noise. A few unlucky large steps knock the policy off its peak, and recovery takes hundreds of episodes.
  • Learning-rate sensitivity. The gradient's scale changes with episode length and return magnitude, so a step size that works early is too large late. Adam softens but doesn't remove this.
  • Seed variance. Runs that differ only in seed diverge wildly; never conclude anything from one (Day 7).
  • Returns that require grad. If returns were computed from network outputs without detaching, autograd would differentiate them too. Here they are plain floats, so this can't happen; with a critic it can (Day 86).

DAY 85

Baselines & Variance Reduction

40m video90m practice

Two facts about the score function cut the variance of the policy gradient without touching its mean: an action cannot be credited with rewards that came before it, and any function of the state alone can be subtracted from the return. Reward-to-go and a learned value baseline together turn “was this episode good?” into “was this action better than usual here?”.

Subtracting a state-dependent baseline

Generalize the theorem by subtracting any function \(b(s)\) that does not depend on the action (S&B eq. 13.10):

$$\nabla J(\theta) \propto \sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \big(q_\pi(s, a) - b(s)\big)\, \nabla \pi(a \mid s, \theta), \qquad \widehat{\nabla J} = \sum_t \gamma^t \big(G_t - b(S_t)\big)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta).$$

Proof that the baseline leaves the gradient unbiased

Theorem · baselines are unbiased

If \(b\) depends on the state (or on anything determined before \(A_t\) is drawn) but not on \(A_t\), then \(\E\big[b(S_t)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\big] = 0\).

Proof

In the state-sum form: \(\sum_a b(s)\, \nabla \pi(a \mid s) = b(s)\, \nabla \sum_a \pi(a \mid s) = b(s)\, \nabla 1 = 0\). In the sample form, condition on the state with the tower rule (Day 3):

$$\E\big[b(S_t)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\big] = \E\Big[b(S_t)\; \underbrace{\E\big[\nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t) \,\big|\, S_t\big]}_{=\,0 \text{ (score lemma)}}\Big] = 0.$$

The argument needs \(b\) to be constant once you condition on \(S_t\). A baseline that depends on \(A_t\) breaks it: \(\E[b(S_t, A_t)\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t) \mid S_t] \ne 0\) in general, and action-dependent baselines need an explicit correction term (Tucker et al. 2018 discuss when they help at all).

Why it reduces variance, and the best baseline

The mean is fixed, so minimizing variance means minimizing the second moment. For one state, with \(s = \nabla \ln \pi(A \mid S)\):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial b}\, \E\big[(G - b)^2 \|s\|^2\big] = -2\,\E\big[(G - b)\|s\|^2\big] = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; b^* = \frac{\E\big[G\, \|s\|^2\big]}{\E\big[\|s\|^2\big]}.$$

This is a score-weighted average return (Greensmith et al. 2004; Weaver & Tao 2001), and a per-coordinate version is better still. If \(\|s\|^2\) is roughly uncorrelated with \(G\), then \(b^* \approx \E[G \mid S] = v_\pi(S)\). In practice everyone uses \(b(s) = \hat v(s, \mathbf w)\): the weight becomes an estimate of the advantage \(A_\pi(s, a) = q_\pi(s, a) - v_\pi(s)\), positive when the action did better than this state's average and negative otherwise. That is the offset problem of Day 84 solved: returns are no longer all positive.

Reward-to-go, from causality

Day 83's trajectory form multiplies every score term by the whole return \(R(\tau)\), including rewards received before the action was taken. Those rewards cannot have been caused by it.

Proof · past rewards contribute zero in expectation

Let \(H_t = (S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, R_t, S_t)\), the history up to the decision at \(t\). For \(k < t\), the reward \(R_{k+1}\) is a function of \(H_t\). The policy is Markov, so \(A_t\) depends on \(H_t\) only through \(S_t\) and \(\E[\nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t) \mid H_t] = 0\). Hence

$$\E\big[R_{k+1}\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\big] = \E\Big[R_{k+1}\, \E\big[\nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t) \,\big|\, H_t\big]\Big] = 0 \qquad (k < t).$$

Drop those terms from \(\big(\sum_k \gamma^k R_{k+1}\big)\sum_t \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\) and what multiplies the score at time \(t\) is \(\sum_{k \ge t} \gamma^k R_{k+1} = \gamma^t G_t\):

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \E_\tau\Big[\sum_t \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\, R(\tau)\Big] = \E_\tau\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)\, G_t\Big].$$

A learned value-function baseline

Fit \(\hat v(s, \mathbf w)\) to the observed returns by Monte Carlo regression, \(\min_{\mathbf w} \E\big[(G_t - \hat v(S_t, \mathbf w))^2\big]\) (Day 62's gradient MC). The baseline does not have to be accurate to keep the gradient unbiased; it only has to be accurate to reduce variance. It does have to stay out of the policy's gradient: the advantage weight is data for the actor and must be detached.

Algorithm · REINFORCE with baseline (S&B §13.4)
  1. Input: policy \(\pi(a \mid s, \theta)\), state-value function \(\hat v(s, \mathbf w)\); step sizes \(\alpha^\theta > 0\), \(\alpha^{\mathbf w} > 0\).
  2. Loop for each episode:
    1. Generate \(S_0, A_0, R_1, \dots, S_{T-1}, A_{T-1}, R_T\) following \(\pi(\cdot \mid \cdot, \theta)\).
    2. Loop for \(t = 0, \dots, T-1\):
      1. \(G \leftarrow \sum_{k=t+1}^{T} \gamma^{k-t-1} R_k\)
      2. \(\delta \leftarrow G - \hat v(S_t, \mathbf w)\)
      3. \(\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha^{\mathbf w}\, \delta\, \nabla \hat v(S_t, \mathbf w)\) (critic: regress towards G)
      4. \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha^\theta\, \gamma^t\, \delta\, \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t, \theta)\) (actor: same δ, used as a weight)

This is still Monte Carlo: \(\hat v\) is used only as a baseline, never to bootstrap, so the estimate stays unbiased. The moment you use \(\hat v(S_{t+1})\) in place of the rest of the return, you have an actor-critic (Day 86).

Advantage normalization

Most deep implementations standardize the advantages within each batch: \(\hat A \leftarrow (\hat A - \operatorname{mean}) / (\operatorname{std} + 10^{-8})\). The centering is a batch-mean baseline (slightly biased because the mean includes the sample itself; the effect is \(O(1/N)\) and is usually ignored). The scaling is not a variance reduction at all but an adaptive step size: the update's magnitude stops depending on the reward scale and on how spread out this batch's returns happen to be. PPO does it per minibatch (Day 92), and GRPO (Day 126) takes the idea to its end, replacing the critic entirely with returns normalized within a group of samples for the same prompt.

Variance before and after

import numpy as np
import gymnasium as gym

env, rng = gym.make("CartPole-v1"), np.random.default_rng(0)
W = rng.normal(0, 0.5, size=(2, 4))              # a fixed, mediocre linear-softmax policy

def probs(S):
    z = S @ W.T
    z = np.exp(z - z.max(-1, keepdims=True))
    return z / z.sum(-1, keepdims=True)

def rollout(seed):
    s, _ = env.reset(seed=seed)
    S, A, R = [], [], []
    while True:
        a = rng.choice(2, p=probs(s))
        S.append(s); A.append(a)
        s, r, terminated, truncated, _ = env.step(a)
        R.append(r)
        if terminated or truncated:
            return np.array(S), np.array(A), np.array(R)

rtg = lambda R: np.cumsum(R[::-1])[::-1]         # reward-to-go G_t (gamma = 1)
feats = lambda S: np.hstack([S, S ** 2, np.ones((len(S), 1))])

# fit a linear value baseline on 200 *separate* episodes, so it is independent of the test data
fit = [rollout(10_000 + i) for i in range(200)]
w = np.linalg.lstsq(np.vstack([feats(S) for S, _, _ in fit]),
                    np.concatenate([rtg(R) for _, _, R in fit]), rcond=None)[0]

def estimates(S, A, R):
    score = (np.eye(2)[A] - probs(S))[:, :, None] * S[:, None, :]   # grad_W log pi(a|s), [T, 2, 4]
    G = rtg(R)
    return np.stack([score.sum(0) * G[0],                            # total return R(tau)
                     np.einsum("t,tij->ij", G, score),               # reward-to-go
                     np.einsum("t,tij->ij", G - feats(S) @ w, score)]).reshape(3, -1)

g = np.stack([estimates(*rollout(i)) for i in range(2000)])          # [episodes, 3, 8]
for name, k in zip(["total return", "reward-to-go", "rtg - v(s)"], range(3)):
    print(f"{name:13s} mean[:3]={g[:, k].mean(0)[:3].round(2)}  total variance={g[:, k].var(0).sum():9.1f}")

Three estimators of the same gradient from the same 2000 episodes. In one run the summed per-coordinate variance fell from about 30,000 (total return) to about 6,000 (reward-to-go) to about 3,300 (with the baseline), while the means agreed within sampling error. The ratios depend on the policy; measure them for yours.

What breaks
  • Fitting the baseline on the batch it is applied to. A flexible \(\hat v\) fitted to the same returns can absorb the very signal you need (in the limit, \(G_t - \hat v(S_t) \to 0\)). Fit on the previous batch, or fit before the policy update with limited capacity.
  • Gradients leaking through the baseline. If the advantage is not detached, the actor loss trains the critic to make advantages small in whatever direction lowers the pseudo-loss.
  • Normalizing tiny batches. With a handful of samples the standard deviation is noisy or zero; with one sample, std() is NaN.
  • Action-dependent “baselines”. Subtracting \(\hat q(s, a)\) instead of \(\hat v(s)\) is biased unless you add the correction term back.

DAY 86

Actor–Critic Fundamentals

45m video105m practice

Replace the Monte Carlo return with a bootstrapped estimate from a learned value function and REINFORCE becomes actor–critic: updates every step, far lower variance, and a bias exactly as large as the critic's error. Every algorithm from here to Day 95 is an actor–critic.

Replacing the Monte Carlo return with a bootstrapped critic

REINFORCE with baseline weights the score by \(G_t - \hat v(S_t)\), and \(G_t\) is only available when the episode ends. Bootstrap instead, as TD did for prediction (Day 42): replace \(G_t\) by the one-step return \(R_{t+1} + \gamma \hat v(S_{t+1}, \mathbf w)\). The weight becomes the TD error

$$\delta_t = R_{t+1} + \gamma\, \hat v(S_{t+1}, \mathbf w) - \hat v(S_t, \mathbf w).$$
Derivation · the TD error estimates the advantage

If the critic is exact, \(\hat v = v_\pi\), then

$$\E\big[\delta_t \mid S_t = s, A_t = a\big] = \E\big[R_{t+1} + \gamma v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid s, a\big] - v_\pi(s) = q_\pi(s, a) - v_\pi(s) = A_\pi(s, a).$$

So \(\delta_t\) is an unbiased, single-sample estimate of the advantage, and \(\E[\delta_t \nabla \ln \pi(A_t \mid S_t)]\) is the true policy gradient. With an approximate critic,

$$\E[\delta_t \mid s, a] = A_\pi(s, a) + \gamma\, \E\big[\hat v(S_{t+1}) - v_\pi(S_{t+1}) \mid s, a\big] - \big(\hat v(s) - v_\pi(s)\big).$$

The last term depends only on \(s\), so Day 85's baseline argument removes it from the gradient. The middle term depends on the action through \(S_{t+1}\), and that is the bias.

The “critic” is the learned value function, the “actor” the learned policy. S&B reserve the name actor–critic for methods whose critic bootstraps; a value function used only as a baseline (Day 85) does not count.

One-step actor–critic

Algorithm · one-step actor–critic (episodic, S&B §13.5)
  1. Input: policy \(\pi(a \mid s, \theta)\), state-value function \(\hat v(s, \mathbf w)\); step sizes \(\alpha^\theta, \alpha^{\mathbf w} > 0\).
  2. Loop for each episode:
    1. Initialize \(S\); \(I \leftarrow 1\).
    2. Loop while \(S\) is not terminal:
      1. \(A \sim \pi(\cdot \mid S, \theta)\); take \(A\), observe \(S', R\).
      2. \(\delta \leftarrow R + \gamma\, \hat v(S', \mathbf w) - \hat v(S, \mathbf w)\) (\(\hat v(S') = 0\) if \(S'\) is terminal)
      3. \(\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w + \alpha^{\mathbf w}\, \delta\, \nabla \hat v(S, \mathbf w)\)
      4. \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha^\theta\, I\, \delta\, \nabla \ln \pi(A \mid S, \theta)\)
      5. \(I \leftarrow \gamma I\); \(S \leftarrow S'\) (\(I\) carries Day 84's \(\gamma^t\); practice drops it)

The two loss terms, and shared vs separate networks

In autograd form the two updates are two losses, with the TD target detached in both and the advantage detached in the actor's:

$$L_{\text{critic}}(\mathbf w) = \tfrac12\big(\underbrace{R + \gamma \hat v(S')}_{\text{no grad}} - \hat v(S, \mathbf w)\big)^2, \qquad L_{\text{actor}}(\theta) = -\underbrace{\delta}_{\text{no grad}}\, \ln \pi(A \mid S, \theta) - \beta\, \mathcal H\big(\pi(\cdot \mid S, \theta)\big).$$

Separate networks keep the two problems apart: different learning rates, no interference, and the critic can be larger. The standard choice for low-dimensional state inputs (PPO's MuJoCo default, Day 92). A shared trunk with two heads halves the compute and shares a representation, which matters for pixels (A3C and PPO on Atari both share a CNN). The price is one combined loss, \(L = L_{\text{actor}} + c_v L_{\text{critic}}\), where \(c_v\) (commonly 0.5) must balance gradients of very different scales: value losses scale with the square of the returns, policy losses don't. Theory adds one more constraint: convergence proofs for actor–critic need the critic to learn on a faster timescale than the actor (Konda & Tsitsiklis 2000), which in practice means a larger critic step size or several critic updates per actor update.

Entropy regularization for exploration

Add the policy's entropy \(\mathcal H(\pi(\cdot \mid s)) = -\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) \ln \pi(a \mid s)\), weighted by \(\beta\), to the objective (Williams & Peng 1991). For softmax with logits \(h\):

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal H}{\partial h_b} = -\pi(b \mid s)\,\big(\ln \pi(b \mid s) + \mathcal H\big),$$

which raises the logits of actions rarer than average (\(\ln \pi_b < -\mathcal H\)) and lowers the common ones. It stops the policy collapsing onto one action before the critic is good enough to justify it. A3C used \(\beta = 0.01\); PPO on Atari uses 0.01 and on MuJoCo usually 0. The bonus changes the objective: with \(\beta > 0\) the optimum is slightly stochastic even when a deterministic policy is best. Day 95 embraces this and makes entropy part of the return itself.

import gymnasium as gym
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.distributions import Categorical

torch.manual_seed(0)
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
actor = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
critic = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64, 1))
opt_actor = torch.optim.Adam(actor.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
opt_critic = torch.optim.Adam(critic.parameters(), lr=5e-3)    # faster critic: two timescales
gamma, beta = 0.99, 0.01

obs, _ = env.reset(seed=0)
episode, ep_return = 0, 0.0

Compatible function approximation

When is a learned critic good enough that substituting it into the policy gradient introduces no bias? Sutton et al. (2000) gave a sufficient condition.

Theorem · compatible function approximation (Sutton et al. 2000)

Let \(f_{\mathbf w}(s, a)\) approximate \(q_\pi\). If

  1. compatibility: \(\nabla_{\mathbf w} f_{\mathbf w}(s, a) = \nabla_\theta \ln \pi(a \mid s, \theta)\), and
  2. the critic is at a minimum of its error: \(\mathbf w\) minimizes \(\sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\big(q_\pi(s, a) - f_{\mathbf w}(s, a)\big)^2\),

then \(\nabla J(\theta) \propto \sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \nabla \pi(a \mid s, \theta)\, f_{\mathbf w}(s, a)\) exactly.

Proof

At the minimum the gradient of the error with respect to \(\mathbf w\) vanishes. Using condition 1 to replace \(\nabla_{\mathbf w} f_{\mathbf w}\):

$$\sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \pi(a \mid s)\big(q_\pi(s, a) - f_{\mathbf w}(s, a)\big)\, \nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_s \mu(s) \sum_a \nabla \pi(a \mid s)\big(q_\pi(s, a) - f_{\mathbf w}(s, a)\big) = 0,$$

using \(\pi\, \nabla \ln \pi = \nabla \pi\). So the error \(q_\pi - f_{\mathbf w}\) is orthogonal to every score direction, and swapping \(q_\pi\) for \(f_{\mathbf w}\) in the theorem changes nothing. \(\blacksquare\)

Condition 1 forces \(f_{\mathbf w}(s, a) = \mathbf w^\top \nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s, \theta)\) (plus anything independent of \(\mathbf w\)): linear in the score features. Since the score has zero mean over actions (Day 83), \(\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) f_{\mathbf w}(s, a) = 0\): a compatible approximator is really an advantage approximator, not a \(q\) approximator. Add a state-value baseline to represent the rest. Two further consequences: the theory says the critic's job is to get the advantage right in the directions the policy can move, not to fit \(q\) everywhere; and the least-squares \(\mathbf w\) turns out to be the natural gradient \(F^{-1}\nabla J\) (Kakade 2001; Day 89), which is the basis of natural actor–critic (Peters & Schaal 2008). Deep RL critics are almost never compatible, so the guarantee is a guide to what matters rather than a property of PPO or SAC.

What breaks
  • A bad critic steers the actor. Unlike REINFORCE, whose errors average out, a systematically wrong critic gives a consistently wrong gradient. Watch the critic's explained variance (Day 92).
  • Forgetting to detach δ. The actor loss then also trains the critic, to make \(\delta\) small where \(\ln \pi\) is large.
  • Bootstrapping through termination, or not through truncation. Both bias the critic (Day 8).
  • Shared-trunk scale mismatch. With large returns the value loss dominates the trunk's gradient and the policy barely learns; normalize returns or tune \(c_v\).
  • Entropy coefficient too high or too low. Too high and the policy stays near uniform; too low and it collapses early. Log the entropy every update.

DAY 87

A2C & A3C

45m video105m practice

Run many copies of the environment, collect a short rollout from each, and average their actor–critic gradients: parallel streams decorrelate on-policy data the way a replay buffer decorrelated DQN's. A3C did this asynchronously across CPU threads; A2C does it synchronously as one batch, and turned out to be at least as good and much simpler.

Why parallel environments

An on-policy method cannot store old data and sample it later, since that data came from a different policy. But consecutive transitions from a single environment are highly correlated, so an update built from the last few steps of one episode is a poor, biased-looking sample of the state distribution. Mnih et al. (2016) observed that running many environment instances in parallel, each in a different part of its episode, gives a batch that is diverse at every update, without replay. That was the key idea of A3C, and it made on-policy deep RL competitive on Atari.

A3C: asynchronous advantage actor–critic

Algorithm · A3C, one actor-learner thread (after Mnih et al. 2016, Algorithm S3)
  1. Shared: global parameters \(\theta\) (policy) and \(\theta_v\) (value), global step counter. Thread-local copies \(\theta', \theta_v'\).
  2. Repeat until the global counter exceeds the budget:
    1. Reset gradients \(d\theta, d\theta_v \leftarrow 0\); synchronize \(\theta' \leftarrow \theta\), \(\theta_v' \leftarrow \theta_v\).
    2. Act with \(\pi(\cdot \mid \cdot\,; \theta')\) for up to \(t_{\max}\) steps (5 on Atari) or until the episode terminates.
    3. \(R \leftarrow 0\) if the last state is terminal, else \(R \leftarrow V(s_t; \theta_v')\).
    4. For \(i = t-1\) down to \(t_{\text{start}}\): \(R \leftarrow r_i + \gamma R\); accumulate \(d\theta \mathrel{+}= \nabla_{\theta'} \ln \pi(a_i \mid s_i; \theta')\,(R - V(s_i; \theta_v')) + \beta \nabla_{\theta'} \mathcal H\) and \(d\theta_v \mathrel{+}= \partial (R - V(s_i; \theta_v'))^2 / \partial \theta_v'\).
    5. Apply \(d\theta, d\theta_v\) to the global parameters, without locking. (Hogwild!-style)

Each step in a rollout uses the longest \(n\)-step return available to it: the first step gets a 5-step return, the last a 1-step return. The paper ran 16 threads on one multi-core CPU, used RMSProp with statistics shared across threads (more robust than per-thread statistics in their comparison), \(\beta = 0.01\), and a CNN trunk shared by policy and value heads. It reported surpassing the previous state of the art on Atari while training for half the time on a CPU instead of a GPU, and the same algorithm with Gaussian policies worked on MuJoCo and on 3D Labyrinth navigation.

Hogwild!: lock-free asynchronous updates

Recht et al. (2011) showed that SGD can run in parallel threads writing to shared memory with no locks at all, and still converge, when gradients are sparse so that threads rarely touch the same coordinates. A3C borrows the mechanism but not the assumption: its gradients are dense, so concurrent writes do overwrite each other, and each thread's gradient is computed from parameters that are several updates stale by the time it is applied. Staleness means each thread's data comes from a slightly older policy than the one being updated, a mild form of off-policy-ness that nothing in A3C corrects. IMPALA (Day 121) later made that correction explicit with V-trace.

A2C, and why it matched A3C

A2C is the synchronous version: step all \(N\) environments together, wait for every rollout to finish, and make one update from the whole \(N \times n\) batch. OpenAI's Baselines release (Wu et al. 2017) reported that their synchronous A2C performed better than their asynchronous implementations, with no evidence that the noise from asynchrony helped. The reasons are now fairly clear:

  • A3C's gains came from parallel, decorrelated data, which A2C keeps, not from asynchrony.
  • Synchronous updates are exactly on-policy: every sample in the batch comes from the parameters being updated.
  • One large batch makes efficient use of a GPU; A3C's many tiny per-thread updates do not.
  • A2C is deterministic given a seed, which makes it debuggable and reproducible (Day 7).

Batch construction and rollout length

A2C's batch is \(N\) environments × \(n\) steps. Each row \(t\) of the rollout gets an \(n\)-step-style advantage that bootstraps from \(\hat v\) of the observation after the last step, with the recursion cut at episode boundaries:

$$\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\,(1 - d_t)\,\hat A_{t+1}, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma\,(1 - \text{term}_t)\,\hat v(s_{t+1}) - \hat v(s_t),$$

where \(d_t\) marks the end of an episode for any reason and \(\text{term}_t\) only a true termination. This is GAE with \(\lambda = 1\); Day 88 adds the \(\lambda\). The trade-offs: larger \(N\) gives smoother gradients but fewer updates per environment step; longer \(n\) means less bootstrapping bias, more variance, and fewer, larger updates. Common settings: on Atari, 16 environments × 5 steps (Baselines' A2C); Stable-Baselines3's A2C defaults to 5 steps per environment, RMSProp with learning rate \(7\times10^{-4}\), value coefficient 0.5 and gradient clipping at 0.5.

A vectorized-environment implementation

Gymnasium 1.x vector environments reset automatically on the step after an episode ends: that step ignores your action, returns the new episode's first observation with reward 0, and is not a real transition. So the observation returned on the terminal step is the true final observation (good: we can bootstrap from it on truncation), and the following row must be masked out of the loss (Day 8).

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import gymnasium as gym
from torch.distributions import Categorical

N, T, gamma, n_updates = 16, 5, 0.99, 10_000            # 16 envs x 5 steps = 80 samples per update
envs = gym.make_vec("CartPole-v1", num_envs=N, vectorization_mode="sync")
actor = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64, 2))
critic = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(4, 64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64, 1))
params = list(actor.parameters()) + list(critic.parameters())
opt = torch.optim.RMSprop(params, lr=7e-4, alpha=0.99, eps=1e-5)
f32 = lambda x: torch.as_tensor(x, dtype=torch.float32)

obs, _ = envs.reset(seed=0)
prev_done = np.zeros(N, dtype=bool)        # the step after an episode ends is an autoreset step
ep_ret, finished = np.zeros(N), []
for update in range(n_updates):
    logp, ent, val, rew, term, done, valid = ([] for _ in range(7))
    for t in range(T):
        x = f32(obs)
        dist = Categorical(logits=actor(x))
        a = dist.sample()
        obs, r, terminated, truncated, _ = envs.step(a.numpy())
        logp.append(dist.log_prob(a)); ent.append(dist.entropy()); val.append(critic(x).squeeze(-1))
        rew.append(f32(r)); term.append(f32(terminated)); done.append(f32(terminated | truncated))
        valid.append(f32(~prev_done))      # autoreset rows ignored the action: mask them out
        prev_done = terminated | truncated
        ep_ret += r; finished += list(ep_ret[prev_done]); ep_ret[prev_done] = 0.0
    logp, ent, val, rew, term, done, valid = map(torch.stack, (logp, ent, val, rew, term, done, valid))

    with torch.no_grad():                  # n-step advantages, bootstrapped from V(s_T)
        v = torch.cat([val, critic(f32(obs)).squeeze(-1)[None]])     # [T+1, N]
        adv, running = torch.zeros(T, N), torch.zeros(N)
        for t in reversed(range(T)):
            delta = rew[t] + gamma * (1 - term[t]) * v[t + 1] - v[t]
            running = delta + gamma * (1 - done[t]) * running
            adv[t] = running
        returns = adv + val

    m = valid.sum()
    actor_loss = -(valid * adv * logp).sum() / m
    critic_loss = 0.5 * (valid * (returns - val) ** 2).sum() / m
    loss = actor_loss + 0.5 * critic_loss - 0.01 * (valid * ent).sum() / m
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
    nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(params, 0.5)
    opt.step()
    if update % 500 == 0 and finished:
        print(f"update {update:5d}  env steps {update * N * T:7d}  return {np.mean(finished[-20:]):5.1f}")

A complete A2C. The value of row \(t+1\) doubles as the bootstrap for row \(t\), because under next-step autoreset row \(t+1\)'s observation is the real successor of row \(t\), including the final observation of a truncated episode.

What breaks
  • Autoreset boundaries. Bootstrapping across an episode boundary, or training on the fake reset transition, is the single most common A2C/PPO bug. Each Gymnasium version handles this differently; read the docs for yours.
  • Truncation treated as termination. Zeroing the bootstrap at a time limit teaches the critic that late states are worthless. Many reference implementations do this; know whether yours does.
  • Environments in lockstep. If every copy is seeded identically and resets together, the batch is \(N\) copies of nearly the same data. Seed them differently (Gymnasium's vector reset(seed=s) seeds env \(i\) with \(s + i\)).
  • Asynchrony bugs in A3C. Races, irreproducibility and stale gradients that hurt with large learning rates. Unless you need CPU-only scaling, use A2C.

Generalized Advantage Estimation

45m video90m practice

GAE applies the λ-return to advantages: an exponentially weighted average of \(k\)-step advantage estimates, which collapses to a discounted sum of TD errors with weights \((\gamma\lambda)^l\). It turns the discrete \(n\)-step dial into a continuous one, and costs a single backward pass over the rollout.

The λ-return, applied to advantages

Day 50 averaged all \(n\)-step returns with geometric weights, \(G^\lambda_t = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{n \ge 1} \lambda^{n-1} G_{t:t+n}\), to get a target between TD(0) and Monte Carlo. Schulman et al. (2016) do the same for the actor's weight. Following their notation, write \(r_t\) for the reward that follows action \(a_t\) (S&B's \(R_{t+1}\)), \(V\) for the critic, and

$$\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).$$

Deriving GAE(γ, λ)

Derivation · from k-step advantages to a sum of TD errors

Step 1: k-step advantages telescope. Define \(\hat A^{(k)}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{k-1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}\). Writing out the deltas, every intermediate \(V\) appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign:

$$\hat A^{(k)}_t = -V(s_t) + r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{k-1} r_{t+k-1} + \gamma^k V(s_{t+k}),$$

the \(k\)-step return minus the baseline. \(\hat A^{(1)}_t = \delta_t\); \(\hat A^{(\infty)}_t = \sum_l \gamma^l r_{t+l} - V(s_t)\), the Monte Carlo advantage.

Step 2: average them geometrically.

$$\hat A^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)}_t = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \lambda^{k-1} \hat A^{(k)}_t = (1 - \lambda)\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \lambda^{k-1} \sum_{l=0}^{k-1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}.$$

Step 3: swap the sums. The term \(\gamma^l \delta_{t+l}\) appears in every \(\hat A^{(k)}\) with \(k > l\), so its total weight is \((1 - \lambda)\sum_{k=l+1}^{\infty} \lambda^{k-1} = \lambda^l\):

$$\boxed{\;\hat A^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}\;}$$

An exponentially weighted sum of TD errors

The two ends of the dial are the estimators of Day 86:

  • \(\lambda = 0\): \(\hat A_t = \delta_t\), the one-step TD error. Lowest variance; biased whenever \(V \ne V^{\pi}\).
  • \(\lambda = 1\): \(\hat A_t = \sum_l \gamma^l \delta_{t+l} = \sum_l \gamma^l r_{t+l} - V(s_t)\), the Monte Carlo advantage with \(V\) as a baseline. Unbiased for any \(V\); highest variance.

Adding \(V(s_t)\) back gives \(\hat A_t + V(s_t) = G^\lambda_t\), the λ-return computed with the current critic. That is the natural regression target for the critic, and it is exactly what PPO implementations use: returns = advantages + values (Day 92).

The bias–variance trade-off via λ

Schulman et al. make “bias” precise. Call an estimator \(\hat A_t\) \(\gamma\)-just if \(\E\big[\hat A_t \nabla \ln \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\big] = \E\big[A^{\pi, \gamma}(s_t, a_t)\, \nabla \ln \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\big]\), that is, if using it gives the exact gradient of the discounted objective. Then:

Result · when GAE is unbiased (Schulman et al. 2016)

\(\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, 1)\) is \(\gamma\)-just for any value function \(V\), because \(V\) only enters as a baseline. \(\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)\) with \(\lambda < 1\) is \(\gamma\)-just when \(V = V^{\pi, \gamma}\), and biased otherwise, with the bias controlled by how much weight falls on bootstrapped terms.

So there are two parameters with two different jobs. \(\gamma\) changes the objective: even with a perfect critic, \(\gamma < 1\) biases the gradient relative to the undiscounted return, in exchange for ignoring the noisy far future. \(\lambda\) changes the estimator: it is only biased through critic error. Their empirical finding was that \(\lambda\) can be set much lower than \(\gamma\) at little cost, because a reasonably accurate critic contributes far less bias than an aggressive discount.

The efficient backward-pass implementation

The infinite sum obeys a one-line recursion, \(\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\, \hat A_{t+1}\), so a rollout of length \(T\) is processed in one reverse sweep. Two flags handle episode boundaries inside the rollout: a true termination removes the bootstrap \(V(s_{t+1})\) from \(\delta_t\); any episode end (termination or truncation) cuts the recursion so advantages don't leak across episodes. At the end of the rollout, \(V\) of the next observation bootstraps the tail.

import numpy as np

def gae(rewards, values, last_value, terminated, done, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95):
    """GAE for one environment's rollout of length T (arrays of shape [T]).
    values[t] = V(s_t); last_value = V(s_T), the state after the final step.
    terminated[t]: s_{t+1} is terminal, so no bootstrap.
    done[t] = terminated[t] or truncated[t]: the episode ended, so cut the recursion."""
    T = len(rewards)
    adv, next_adv = np.zeros(T), 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        next_value = last_value if t == T - 1 else values[t + 1]
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * (1.0 - terminated[t]) * next_value - values[t]
        next_adv = delta + gamma * lam * (1.0 - done[t]) * next_adv
        adv[t] = next_adv
    return adv, adv + values       # advantages for the actor, lambda-returns for the critic

# check against the forward definition sum_l (gamma*lam)^l delta_{t+l}
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, gamma, lam = 50, 0.99, 0.95
r, V, vT, z = rng.normal(size=T), rng.normal(size=T), rng.normal(), np.zeros(T)
adv, _ = gae(r, V, vT, z, z, gamma, lam)
delta = r + gamma * np.append(V[1:], vT) - V
forward = [sum((gamma * lam) ** l * delta[t + l] for l in range(T - t)) for t in range(T)]
print(np.abs(adv - forward).max())                     # ~1e-15

The same loop, vectorized over environments with arrays of shape [T, N], is the heart of every PPO implementation. One subtlety: values[t + 1] is the bootstrap for row t only if row t + 1's observation is the true successor of row t. With Gymnasium 1.x next-step autoreset it is (Day 87); with older same-step autoreset you need info["final_observation"] instead.

Typical λ = 0.95, and why

In the GAE paper's experiments (TRPO on cart-pole and 3D locomotion), the best results came from intermediate values, roughly \(\lambda \in [0.9, 0.99]\) with \(\gamma\) between 0.99 and 0.995. PPO adopted \(\gamma = 0.99, \lambda = 0.95\) for both Atari and MuJoCo, and most libraries inherited it. With those values \(\gamma\lambda \approx 0.94\), an effective horizon of about 17 steps: long enough to look past the critic's local errors, short enough to suppress most of the variance from the distant future. Andrychowicz et al. (2021), in a large study of on-policy design choices, found \(\lambda = 0.9\) a good starting point. Neither number is sacred: environments whose rewards arrive long after the decisive action want a larger \(\lambda\) (and \(\gamma\)).

A GAE ablation

The experiment that makes \(\lambda\) concrete: hold everything else fixed and sweep \(\lambda \in \{0, 0.5, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99, 1\}\) over several seeds. Expect an inverted U in final return. At \(\lambda = 0\), learning is slow early on, when the critic is poor and its bias dominates. At \(\lambda = 1\) the curves are noisy and sensitive to batch size. The flat top, usually somewhere between 0.9 and 0.99, is wide, which is why 0.95 is rarely tuned.

What breaks
  • Using done where terminated belongs. Cutting the bootstrap at truncations makes every time-limited episode look like a failure; on Pendulum, where every episode is truncated, it is badly wrong.
  • Forgetting to cut the recursion. Without the (1 - done) on the recursion, the next episode's TD errors leak into this episode's last advantages.
  • Stale values. The values used in GAE must be those recorded during the rollout (the old critic), not recomputed after the critic has been updated; otherwise advantages and value targets move during the update.
  • Gradients through the advantages. Compute GAE under torch.no_grad(); it is data.
  • Normalizing value targets as if they were advantages. Advantage normalization (Day 92) applies to the actor's weights only; the critic must regress onto the unnormalized λ-returns.

Natural Policy Gradients

45m video75m practice

A gradient step of fixed Euclidean length can change the policy enormously or hardly at all, depending on how it happens to be parameterized. Measuring steps by the KL divergence between policies gives the natural gradient \(F^{-1}\nabla J\), which is invariant to parameterization and can be computed with conjugate gradient without ever forming \(F\).

The problem with Euclidean steps

“Steepest ascent” only means something relative to a notion of step length. The ordinary gradient is the steepest direction under the Euclidean norm of the parameters:

$$\frac{\nabla J}{\|\nabla J\|} = \argmax_{\|\Delta\|_2 \le 1}\, \nabla J^\top \Delta.$$

But parameter distances are arbitrary. Multiply one parameter by 10 inside the network and divide its initialization by 10: the policy is unchanged, the gradient with respect to that coordinate shrinks tenfold, and gradient ascent now behaves differently. Worse, the effect on the policy of a fixed-size step varies across parameter space. For a Gaussian policy the score with respect to the mean is \((a - \mu)/\sigma^2\): the same step in \(\mu\) that nudges a wide policy can shift a narrow one by many standard deviations. In the snippet below, one step of \(0.1\,\nabla J\) changes the policy by about 0.2 nats of KL when \(\sigma = 1\) and by about 18 nats when \(\sigma = 0.1\). Conversely, near-deterministic softmax policies have tiny gradients, which produces the long plateaus Kakade (2001) used to motivate the natural gradient.

KL divergence as the right metric

What we care about is how much the policy's behaviour changes, and the natural measure of distance between two distributions is the KL divergence. For policies, average it over the states the policy visits:

$$\bar D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta, \theta + \Delta) = \E_{s \sim d^{\pi_\theta}}\Big[\KL\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\, \big\|\, \pi_{\theta + \Delta}(\cdot \mid s)\big)\Big].$$

It depends only on the two policies, not on how they are parameterized. So a step rule stated in terms of KL is automatically parameterization-free. To use it, expand it for small \(\Delta\).

The KL's second-order expansion and the Fisher matrix

Derivation · KL ≈ ½ Δᵀ F Δ

Fix a state and write \(p_\theta = \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\). By definition \(\KL(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\Delta}) = \E_{a \sim p_\theta}\big[\ln p_\theta(a) - \ln p_{\theta+\Delta}(a)\big]\). Taylor-expand the second log around \(\theta\):

$$\ln p_{\theta+\Delta}(a) = \ln p_\theta(a) + \Delta^\top \nabla \ln p_\theta(a) + \tfrac12 \Delta^\top \nabla^2 \ln p_\theta(a)\, \Delta + O(\|\Delta\|^3).$$

The zeroth-order terms cancel. The first-order term vanishes in expectation because the score has zero mean (Day 83). What remains is

$$\KL(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\Delta}) = -\tfrac12 \Delta^\top \E\big[\nabla^2 \ln p_\theta(a)\big] \Delta + O(\|\Delta\|^3).$$

Finally, \(\nabla^2 \ln p = \nabla^2 p / p - \nabla \ln p\, \nabla \ln p^\top\), and \(\E_{a \sim p}[\nabla^2 p(a) / p(a)] = \nabla^2 \sum_a p(a) = 0\). Hence \(-\E[\nabla^2 \ln p] = \E[\nabla \ln p\, \nabla \ln p^\top]\), and

$$\KL(p_\theta \| p_{\theta+\Delta}) \approx \tfrac12\, \Delta^\top F(\theta)\, \Delta, \qquad F(\theta) = \E_{s \sim d^{\pi}, a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\nabla \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla \ln \pi_\theta(a \mid s)^\top\big].$$

\(F\) is the Fisher information matrix: the covariance of the score, and the Hessian of the KL at \(\Delta = 0\). It is positive semidefinite, it can be estimated from the same on-policy samples as the gradient, and it acts as a local metric on the space of policies: it says which parameter directions change behaviour a lot (high curvature) and which barely matter.

The natural gradient F⁻¹∇J

Derivation · steepest ascent under a KL budget

Maximize the linearized objective subject to the quadratic KL budget: \(\max_\Delta\; g^\top \Delta\) subject to \(\tfrac12 \Delta^\top F \Delta \le \epsilon\), with \(g = \nabla J\). The Lagrangian \(g^\top \Delta - \nu(\tfrac12 \Delta^\top F \Delta - \epsilon)\) is stationary at \(g = \nu F \Delta\), so \(\Delta \propto F^{-1} g\). Choosing the scale so the constraint is tight:

$$\Delta^* = \sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon}{g^\top F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g.$$

\(\tilde\nabla J = F^{-1}\nabla J\) is the natural gradient (Amari 1998; applied to policies by Kakade 2001). Natural policy gradient ascent is \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha F^{-1}\nabla J\), or with the step length set by the KL budget as above, which is the form TRPO uses. There is a beautiful link back to Day 86: with compatible features \(f_{\mathbf w}(s, a) = \mathbf w^\top \nabla \ln \pi(a \mid s)\), the least-squares critic satisfies the normal equations \(\E[\nabla \ln \pi\, \nabla \ln \pi^\top]\,\mathbf w = \E[\nabla \ln \pi\; q_\pi]\), that is \(F\mathbf w = \nabla J\). The compatible critic's weights are the natural gradient.

Covariance: invariance to reparameterization

Theorem · the natural gradient is covariant

Let \(\theta = \phi(\psi)\) be a smooth invertible reparameterization with Jacobian \(J = \partial\theta / \partial\psi\). Then the natural-gradient step computed in \(\psi\), mapped to \(\theta\), equals the natural-gradient step computed in \(\theta\) (to first order).

Proof

By the chain rule, \(\nabla_\psi J = J^\top \nabla_\theta J\), and since the score transforms the same way, \(F_\psi = \E[J^\top \nabla_\theta \ln\pi\, \nabla_\theta \ln\pi^\top J] = J^\top F_\theta J\). The natural step in \(\psi\) is

$$\Delta\psi = F_\psi^{-1} \nabla_\psi J = J^{-1} F_\theta^{-1} J^{-\top} J^\top \nabla_\theta J = J^{-1} F_\theta^{-1} \nabla_\theta J,$$

and it moves \(\theta\) by \(J\,\Delta\psi = F_\theta^{-1}\nabla_\theta J\), the natural step in \(\theta\). The ordinary gradient fails this test: \(J\,\nabla_\psi J = J J^\top \nabla_\theta J\), which depends on \(J\). \(\blacksquare\)

For a Gaussian with parameters \((\mu, \log\sigma)\), \(F = \operatorname{diag}(1/\sigma^2, 2)\). The natural gradient rescales the mean's gradient by \(\sigma^2\): it moves the mean in units of the policy's own width. Parameterize with \(\sigma\) instead of \(\log\sigma\) and the ordinary gradient changes; the natural one, as a change in the distribution, does not.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
reward = lambda a: -(a - 3.0) ** 2              # one-step continuous bandit, best action 3

def pg_and_fisher(mu, log_sigma, n=200_000):
    """Score-function gradient and Fisher matrix for N(mu, sigma^2), params (mu, log sigma)."""
    sigma = np.exp(log_sigma)
    a = rng.normal(mu, sigma, n)
    score = np.stack([(a - mu) / sigma ** 2,              # d log pi / d mu
                      (a - mu) ** 2 / sigma ** 2 - 1])    # d log pi / d log sigma
    adv = reward(a) - reward(a).mean()                    # constant baseline (Day 85)
    g = score @ adv / n                                   # vanilla policy gradient
    F = score @ score.T / n                               # Fisher = E[score score^T]
    return g, F

def conjugate_gradient(Avp, b, iters=10, tol=1e-10):
    """Solve A x = b using only products A @ v (works for NumPy arrays and torch tensors)."""
    x, r = b * 0.0, b * 1.0
    p, rr = r * 1.0, r @ r
    for _ in range(iters):
        Ap = Avp(p)
        alpha = rr / (p @ Ap)
        x, r = x + alpha * p, r - alpha * Ap
        rr_new = r @ r
        if rr_new < tol:
            break
        p, rr = r + (rr_new / rr) * p, rr_new
    return x

for sigma in [1.0, 0.1]:
    g, F = pg_and_fisher(0.0, np.log(sigma))
    nat = conjugate_gradient(lambda v: F @ v, g)          # F^-1 g without inverting F
    step = 0.1 * g                                        # the same Euclidean learning rate both times
    print(f"sigma={sigma}: grad={g.round(3)}  natural={nat.round(3)}  "
          f"KL of a 0.1*grad step ~ {0.5 * step @ F @ step:.3f}")
# sigma=1.0: grad=[ 6.02 -2.04]  natural=[ 6. -1.]    KL ~ 0.22
# sigma=0.1: grad=[ 6.02 -0.02]  natural=[ 0.06 -0.01]  KL ~ 18.2

At \(\sigma = 0.1\) the ordinary gradient still says “move the mean by 6 per unit step”, sixty policy widths; the natural gradient says 0.06, six-tenths of a width. The same learning rate gives a KL change about 80 times larger in the narrow case.

Conjugate gradient, to avoid inverting F

A policy network has \(n = 10^4\) to \(10^7\) parameters. Forming \(F\) costs \(O(n^2)\) memory and inverting it \(O(n^3)\) time, both out of the question. But the natural gradient is the solution of the linear system \(Fx = g\), and conjugate gradient solves symmetric positive-definite systems using only matrix–vector products \(v \mapsto Fv\). In exact arithmetic it converges in at most \(n\) iterations, and in practice about 10 iterations give a good enough direction. A product \(Fv\) never needs \(F\) itself: it is either the sample average \(\frac1N\sum_i s_i (s_i^\top v)\) of the scores, or, as TRPO does, the Hessian-vector product of the average KL computed by differentiating twice (Day 90). Either way it costs about as much as one or two gradient evaluations.

Algorithm · conjugate gradient for F x = g
  1. \(x \leftarrow 0\), \(r \leftarrow g\), \(p \leftarrow g\).
  2. For \(k = 1, \dots, K\) (typically \(K = 10\)):
    1. \(\alpha \leftarrow r^\top r / (p^\top F p)\) (one Fisher-vector product)
    2. \(x \leftarrow x + \alpha p\), \(r \leftarrow r - \alpha F p\)
    3. If \(r^\top r\) is below tolerance, stop.
    4. \(p \leftarrow r + (r^\top_{\text{new}} r_{\text{new}} / r^\top_{\text{old}} r_{\text{old}})\, p\)
What breaks
  • Singular or ill-conditioned F. Overparameterized networks have many directions that don't change the policy at all, so \(F\) is singular. Add damping, \(F + \lambda I\) with \(\lambda \approx 0.1\) (or smaller), before running CG.
  • A noisy F. Estimated from one batch, \(F\) is as noisy as the gradient. The quadratic model of the KL can then be badly wrong, so a step that “should” have KL \(\epsilon\) can have much more. Day 90's line search exists for this.
  • Finite steps. Invariance holds to first order; the second-order expansion is only trustworthy for small \(\epsilon\).
  • Cost and complexity. Each update needs on-policy samples, several Fisher-vector products and careful numerics. K-FAC approximations (ACKTR, Wu et al. 2017) and PPO's first-order clipping (Day 91) are two ways around it.

DAY 90

TRPO

45m video90m practice

TRPO maximizes a local surrogate of the return subject to a limit on how far the policy moves in KL. A theorem says that a penalized form of the surrogate can never make the policy worse; the practical algorithm approximates it with a natural-gradient step computed by conjugate gradient and Fisher-vector products, followed by a line search that checks the step really is acceptable.

The performance difference lemma

Follow Schulman et al. (2015): write \(\eta(\pi) = \E_\pi\big[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\big]\) for the expected discounted return (our \(J\)), and \(\rho_\pi(s) = \sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s \mid \pi)\) for the unnormalized discounted visitation.

Lemma · performance difference (Kakade & Langford 2002) $$\eta(\tilde\pi) = \eta(\pi) + \E_{\tau \sim \tilde\pi}\Big[\sum_{t \ge 0} \gamma^t A_\pi(s_t, a_t)\Big] = \eta(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_{\tilde\pi}(s) \sum_a \tilde\pi(a \mid s)\, A_\pi(s, a).$$
Proof

Write \(A_\pi(s_t, a_t) = \E\big[r_t + \gamma V_\pi(s_{t+1}) - V_\pi(s_t) \mid s_t, a_t\big]\) and sum along a trajectory of the new policy:

$$\E_{\tau \sim \tilde\pi}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t \big(r_t + \gamma V_\pi(s_{t+1}) - V_\pi(s_t)\big)\Big] = \E_{\tau \sim \tilde\pi}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t\Big] - \E_{s_0}\big[V_\pi(s_0)\big] = \eta(\tilde\pi) - \eta(\pi).$$

The value terms telescope, leaving only \(-V_\pi(s_0)\), and \(s_0\) has the same distribution under both policies. Grouping the sum by state gives the second form. \(\blacksquare\)

This is exact, and it says a new policy is better if it puts positive probability on positive-advantage actions in the states it visits. The trouble is \(\rho_{\tilde\pi}\): we have samples from the old policy, not the new one.

The surrogate objective

Replace the new policy's visitation by the old one's:

$$L_{\pi}(\tilde\pi) = \eta(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_{\pi}(s) \sum_a \tilde\pi(a \mid s)\, A_\pi(s, a).$$

It agrees with \(\eta\) to first order at \(\tilde\pi = \pi\): \(L_{\pi_{\theta_0}}(\pi_{\theta_0}) = \eta(\pi_{\theta_0})\), because \(\sum_a \pi(a \mid s) A_\pi(s, a) = 0\), and \(\nabla_\theta L_{\pi_{\theta_0}}(\pi_\theta)\big|_{\theta_0} = \nabla_\theta \eta(\pi_\theta)\big|_{\theta_0}\), because the derivative of the surrogate is exactly the policy gradient theorem with an advantage baseline. So a small enough step that improves \(L\) improves \(\eta\). The inner sum is an importance-sampled expectation over the old policy's actions, which is how it is estimated in practice:

$$\sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, A(s, a) = \E_{a \sim \pi_{\text{old}}}\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)}\, A(s, a)\Big], \qquad \hat L(\theta) = \hat\E_t\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t \mid s_t)}\, \hat A_t\Big].$$

The monotonic improvement theorem

How small is small enough? Kakade & Langford (2002) answered this for mixture policies \(\pi_{\text{new}} = (1 - \alpha)\pi_{\text{old}} + \alpha\pi'\) in conservative policy iteration, with a bound of \(\frac{2\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\alpha^2\). Schulman et al. (2015) extended it to arbitrary stochastic policies by measuring the change with total variation.

Theorem · monotonic improvement bound (Schulman et al. 2015, Theorem 1)

Let \(\alpha = D^{\max}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{\text{old}}, \pi_{\text{new}}) = \max_s D_{\mathrm{TV}}\big(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s), \pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot \mid s)\big)\) and \(\epsilon = \max_{s, a} |A_{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s, a)|\). Then

$$\eta(\pi_{\text{new}}) \ge L_{\pi_{\text{old}}}(\pi_{\text{new}}) - \frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\,\alpha^2.$$

Since \(D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p, q)^2 \le D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q)\) (Pinsker), it follows that \(\eta(\tilde\pi) \ge L_\pi(\tilde\pi) - C\, D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi, \tilde\pi)\) with \(C = \frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\) and \(D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi, \tilde\pi) = \max_s \KL\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \tilde\pi(\cdot \mid s)\big)\).

Corollary · monotone improvement by minorization–maximization

Let \(M_i(\pi) = L_{\pi_i}(\pi) - C\, D^{\max}_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_i, \pi)\). The theorem says \(\eta(\pi) \ge M_i(\pi)\) for every \(\pi\), and \(M_i(\pi_i) = \eta(\pi_i)\) because the KL is zero and \(L_{\pi_i}(\pi_i) = \eta(\pi_i)\). So if \(\pi_{i+1}\) does at least as well as \(\pi_i\) on \(M_i\),

$$\eta(\pi_{i+1}) \;\ge\; M_i(\pi_{i+1}) \;\ge\; M_i(\pi_i) \;=\; \eta(\pi_i).$$

Read the fine print. The guarantee needs exact advantages, the maximum KL over all states, and the penalty coefficient \(C\), which is enormous: with \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(4\gamma/(1-\gamma)^2 \approx 4 \times 10^4\), times the largest advantage. Steps that respect it are tiny. Achiam et al. (2017) later proved a tighter bound in terms of the TV distance averaged over the old policy's states, which is closer to what TRPO actually constrains, but still conservative. The theorem is the justification for the shape of the algorithm, not a guarantee about the algorithm as run.

The KL constraint

The practical algorithm makes two substitutions. The penalty becomes a hard constraint, because a fixed penalty coefficient that is safe gives tiny steps and one that is useful is problem-dependent. And the maximum over states becomes an average over sampled states, which can be estimated:

$$\max_\theta\; \hat\E_t\Big[\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}\, \hat A_t\Big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \hat\E_t\Big[\KL\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\big\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big] \le \delta,$$

with \(\delta = 0.01\) as the usual choice. Advantages come from GAE (Day 88) with a separately trained critic.

Conjugate gradient and the line search

Approximate the objective linearly (gradient \(g\)) and the constraint quadratically (Hessian of the mean KL, which is the Fisher matrix \(F\), Day 89). The solution is the natural gradient step scaled to the boundary: \(\theta = \theta_{\text{old}} + \sqrt{2\delta / (x^\top F x)}\; x\) with \(x = F^{-1}g\). Because both approximations can be wrong for a finite step, TRPO then checks the real thing.

Algorithm · TRPO (one iteration)
  1. Collect trajectories with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); compute advantages \(\hat A_t\) with GAE.
  2. Compute \(g = \nabla_\theta \hat L(\theta)\big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
  3. Run \(K \approx 10\) iterations of conjugate gradient on \((F + \lambda I)\,x = g\), using Fisher-vector products. (damping λ ≈ 0.1)
  4. Full step \(s = \sqrt{2\delta / (x^\top F x)}\, x\).
  5. Backtracking line search: for \(j = 0, 1, \dots, 9\), try \(\theta = \theta_{\text{old}} + 0.5^j s\); accept the first that satisfies the KL constraint and improves \(\hat L\). If none does, keep \(\theta_{\text{old}}\).
  6. Update the critic by regression on the λ-returns (several epochs of Adam, or its own trust region).

Fisher-vector products

At \(\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}\) the mean KL is zero, and so is its gradient, but its Hessian is \(F\). So \(Fv\) is a Hessian-vector product, and autograd gives it by differentiating twice: first the KL's gradient with create_graph=True, then the gradient of its dot product with \(v\). Cost: about two backward passes per product, never an \(n \times n\) matrix.

import torch
from torch.distributions import Categorical, kl_divergence
from torch.nn.utils import parameters_to_vector, vector_to_parameters

def flat_grad(y, params, **kw):
    return torch.cat([g.reshape(-1) for g in torch.autograd.grad(y, params, **kw)])

def trpo_step(policy, obs, act, adv, max_kl=0.01, damping=0.1, cg_iters=10, backtracks=10):
    """One TRPO policy update for a discrete policy network mapping obs -> logits."""
    params = list(policy.parameters())
    with torch.no_grad():
        old_logits = policy(obs)
        old_logp = Categorical(logits=old_logits).log_prob(act)

    def surrogate():                      # L(theta) = E[ pi_theta / pi_old * A ]
        logp = Categorical(logits=policy(obs)).log_prob(act)
        return (torch.exp(logp - old_logp) * adv).mean()

    def mean_kl():                        # E_s KL(pi_old(.|s) || pi_theta(.|s))
        return kl_divergence(Categorical(logits=old_logits), Categorical(logits=policy(obs))).mean()

    def fvp(v):                           # F v = d/dtheta [ (d KL / dtheta) . v ]  at theta_old
        g_kl = flat_grad(mean_kl(), params, create_graph=True)
        return flat_grad(g_kl @ v, params) + damping * v

    g = flat_grad(surrogate(), params)
    x = conjugate_gradient(fvp, g, cg_iters)              # x ~ F^-1 g, the CG of Day 89
    step = torch.sqrt(2 * max_kl / (x @ fvp(x))) * x       # largest step with 1/2 s^T F s = max_kl
    theta_old = parameters_to_vector(params).detach()
    with torch.no_grad():
        L_old = surrogate()
        for j in range(backtracks):                       # backtracking line search
            vector_to_parameters(theta_old + 0.5 ** j * step, params)
            if mean_kl() <= max_kl and surrogate() > L_old:
                return True
        vector_to_parameters(theta_old, params)           # nothing acceptable: keep the old policy
    return False

Why TRPO is powerful but painful

TRPO's results were striking for 2015: one set of hyperparameters learned swimming, hopping and walking gaits in MuJoCo from scratch, and the same algorithm played Atari from pixels. Updates rarely collapse, and \(\delta\) transfers well across tasks. The pain is in the machinery, and the PPO paper lists it: TRPO is complicated, and it is not compatible with architectures that include noise (such as dropout) or that share parameters between the policy and value function or with auxiliary tasks. Concretely:

  • Every update needs a CG solve (about ten Fisher-vector products, each roughly two backward passes) plus several forward passes for the line search.
  • The constraint is defined for the policy alone, so a shared policy–value trunk has no clean place in it.
  • It takes one step per batch. Reusing the batch for several epochs of minibatch SGD, the cheap way to gain sample efficiency, does not fit the formulation.
  • Getting the numerics right (damping, flattening parameters, the line-search conditions) is fiddly, and bugs are silent.

PPO (Day 91) keeps the trust-region idea and drops the second-order machinery.

What breaks
  • No damping. CG on a singular \(F\) can return enormous directions; the step size formula then divides by a tiny \(x^\top Fx\).
  • Line search on the wrong criterion. Accepting steps that satisfy the KL constraint without checking that the surrogate improved, or the reverse, loses the method's robustness.
  • Stale or recomputed old policy. old_logits must be fixed before any parameter changes; recomputing them inside the line search makes the ratio identically 1.
  • Mean KL hides outliers. The average can be within \(\delta\) while a few states change drastically. Log the maximum KL over the batch as well.

DAY 91

PPO I — The Clipped Objective

45m video105m practice

PPO keeps TRPO's idea, a surrogate objective that is only trusted near the old policy, but enforces it with a first-order trick: clip the probability ratio so that each sample stops pushing once the policy has moved far enough in its favour. That makes several epochs of minibatch SGD on each batch safe, and it is simple enough to have become the default on-policy algorithm.

The probability ratio

$$r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}, \qquad r_t(\theta_{\text{old}}) = 1.$$

The ratio measures how much more (or less) likely the new policy makes the action actually taken. TRPO's surrogate, which Schulman et al. (2017) call \(L^{\mathrm{CPI}}\) after conservative policy iteration, is \(L^{\mathrm{CPI}}(\theta) = \hat\E_t\big[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t\big]\). Since \(\nabla r_t = r_t \nabla \ln \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\), its gradient at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\) is the ordinary policy gradient. Maximized without a constraint, it is unbounded: the optimizer can push \(r_t \to \infty\) for every positive-advantage sample, and a few large steps destroy the policy. In code the ratio is always computed as exp(logp - old_logp), with old_logp stored at collection time.

The clipped surrogate objective

Definition · PPO-Clip (Schulman et al. 2017) $$L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat\E_t\Big[\min\Big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\; \clip\big(r_t(\theta), 1 - \varepsilon, 1 + \varepsilon\big)\hat A_t\Big)\Big], \qquad \varepsilon \approx 0.1\text{–}0.2.$$
Derivation · the geometry, case by case

\(\hat A_t > 0\) (the action was better than expected). Dividing by \(\hat A_t\) preserves the min, so the term is \(\hat A_t \min\big(r, \clip(r, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\big) = \hat A_t \min(r, 1 + \varepsilon)\). It rises with \(r\) up to \(1 + \varepsilon\) and is flat beyond: no incentive to make the action more than \(1+\varepsilon\) times as likely. Below \(1 - \varepsilon\) it is unclipped, so if the policy has moved the wrong way, the full gradient pulls it back.

\(\hat A_t < 0\) (worse than expected). Dividing by a negative number flips the min to a max: the term is \(\hat A_t \max\big(r, \clip(r, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\big) = \hat A_t \max(r, 1 - \varepsilon)\). It is flat for \(r < 1 - \varepsilon\): no incentive to suppress the action below \(1-\varepsilon\) of its old probability. Above \(1 + \varepsilon\) it is unclipped.

In both cases \(L^{\mathrm{CLIP}} \le L^{\mathrm{CPI}}\), with equality inside \([1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon]\) and in the “wrong-way” region. The objective is a pessimistic lower bound: clipping only ever removes an incentive to move further, never hides a loss. The gradient of a sample is exactly zero in its flat region.

Why clipping approximates a trust region

As SGD runs over the same batch, samples whose ratio has left the interval in their favourable direction stop contributing. The batch gradient shrinks as more samples hit their clip, so updates limit themselves without any second-order computation. But it is a soft, per-sample mechanism, not a constraint:

  • Parameters are shared across states, so a sample's ratio can be dragged outside the interval by updates driven by other samples, and nothing then pulls it back unless it is in the wrong-way region.
  • The KL divergence is not bounded at all. Engstrom et al. (2020) found PPO's ratios routinely exceed the clipping range and its KL can grow large, and that much of PPO's stability comes from implementation details rather than the clip itself (Day 92). Wang et al. (2019) proposed variants with tighter control.

So monitor it: log the clip fraction (share of samples with \(|r - 1| > \varepsilon\)) and an estimate of the KL every update. A common safeguard, used by Spinning Up's PPO, is early stopping: end the epochs for this batch once the approximate KL exceeds a target of around 0.01–0.02.

The KL-penalty variant

Definition · PPO with adaptive KL penalty $$L^{\mathrm{KLPEN}}(\theta) = \hat\E_t\Big[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t - \beta\, \KL\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\big\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big].$$

After each update compute \(d = \hat\E_t[\KL]\). If \(d < d_{\text{targ}}/1.5\), set \(\beta \leftarrow \beta/2\); if \(d > 1.5\, d_{\text{targ}}\), set \(\beta \leftarrow 2\beta\).

This is the Lagrangian form of TRPO's constraint with a heuristic multiplier update. In the PPO paper's continuous-control comparison, clipping with \(\varepsilon = 0.2\) scored best, and the adaptive-KL version somewhat worse; the clipped form became the default. A KL term reappears in RLHF (Day 124), but in a different role: there it is a penalty against a fixed reference policy, folded into the reward, rather than a trust region against the previous iterate.

Multiple epochs over the same batch

TRPO takes one constrained step per batch. PPO runs \(K\) epochs of minibatch SGD on the clipped objective over the same \(NT\) samples (\(N\) actors × \(T\) steps): commonly \(K = 3\)–\(4\) on Atari and \(K = 10\) on MuJoCo. That reuse is where PPO's sample-efficiency advantage over A2C comes from, and the clip is what makes it safe: without it, ten epochs of \(L^{\mathrm{CPI}}\) ascent on one batch would overfit it and move the policy far outside the region where the advantages mean anything.

The full PPO algorithm

With a shared or separate critic and an entropy bonus, the paper's combined objective (maximized) is

$$L^{\mathrm{CLIP+VF+S}}(\theta) = \hat\E_t\Big[L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}_t(\theta) - c_1 \big(V_\theta(s_t) - V^{\mathrm{targ}}_t\big)^2 + c_2\, \mathcal H\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big].$$
Algorithm · PPO, actor–critic style
  1. For iteration \(= 1, 2, \dots\):
    1. Run \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) in \(N\) parallel environments for \(T\) steps. Store observations, actions, \(\ln \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)\), values, rewards and termination flags.
    2. Compute advantages \(\hat A_t\) with GAE(\(\gamma, \lambda\)) and value targets \(V^{\mathrm{targ}}_t = \hat A_t + V(s_t)\). (Day 88)
    3. For epoch \(= 1, \dots, K\): shuffle the \(NT\) samples; for each minibatch, normalize its advantages, compute \(r_t(\theta)\), and take a gradient step on \(-L^{\mathrm{CLIP+VF+S}}\) with gradient clipping.
    4. Optionally stop the epochs early if the approximate KL exceeds a target.
    5. \(\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta\).

Here is the update half of a complete, CleanRL-style implementation. Day 92 supplies the agent, the environment wrappers and the rollout loop, and explains every default.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

def ppo_update(agent, opt, batch, epochs=10, n_minibatches=32, clip=0.2, ent_coef=0.0,
               vf_coef=0.5, max_grad_norm=0.5, clip_vloss=True, target_kl=None):
    """batch: dict of flat tensors obs, actions, logp (old), adv, returns, values (old)."""
    n = len(batch["adv"])
    for epoch in range(epochs):
        for idx in torch.randperm(n).tensor_split(n_minibatches):    # every sample once per epoch
            b = {k: v[idx] for k, v in batch.items()}
            _, logp, entropy, value = agent.act(b["obs"], b["actions"])
            log_ratio = logp - b["logp"]
            ratio = log_ratio.exp()
            adv = (b["adv"] - b["adv"].mean()) / (b["adv"].std() + 1e-8)   # per-minibatch normalization

            pg_loss = torch.max(-adv * ratio, -adv * ratio.clamp(1 - clip, 1 + clip)).mean()  # = -L^CLIP
            if clip_vloss:                                   # PPO2-style value clipping (Day 92)
                v_clipped = b["values"] + (value - b["values"]).clamp(-clip, clip)
                v_loss = 0.5 * torch.max((value - b["returns"]) ** 2,
                                         (v_clipped - b["returns"]) ** 2).mean()
            else:
                v_loss = 0.5 * ((value - b["returns"]) ** 2).mean()
            loss = pg_loss - ent_coef * entropy.mean() + vf_coef * v_loss

            opt.zero_grad()
            loss.backward()
            nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(agent.parameters(), max_grad_norm)    # global norm, all params
            opt.step()

            with torch.no_grad():                            # diagnostics
                approx_kl = ((ratio - 1) - log_ratio).mean().item()        # k3 estimator, always >= 0
                clipfrac = ((ratio - 1).abs() > clip).float().mean().item()
        if target_kl is not None and approx_kl > target_kl:  # optional early stop
            break
    return dict(pg_loss=pg_loss.item(), v_loss=v_loss.item(), entropy=entropy.mean().item(),
                approx_kl=approx_kl, clipfrac=clipfrac)

Part 1 of the PPO implementation. Note \(-\min(x, y) = \max(-x, -y)\): the policy loss is the negated clipped objective. agent.act(obs, action) must return the log-probability of the given action under the current parameters; for a discrete policy that is a Categorical log-prob, for a Gaussian the sum over action dimensions.

What breaks
  • Too many epochs or too high a learning rate. Ratios run far past the clip, the KL explodes, and returns collapse a few iterations later. Watch clipfrac (commonly 0.05–0.2 is healthy) and approx_kl.
  • Recomputing the old log-probabilities. They must be recorded when the action is sampled. Computed after any update, the ratio starts away from 1 and the clip means something else.
  • Ratio from probabilities. Dividing small probabilities underflows; always exponentiate a difference of log-probabilities.
  • Clipping in the wrong direction. Using min where the loss needs max (or forgetting the sign) gives an optimistic objective with zero gradient in the wrong-way region.
  • Believing PPO is a trust-region method. It approximates one. A large enough step still breaks it.

PPO II — Implementation Details That Decide Everything

50m video150m practice

The clipped objective is a small part of what makes PPO work. The reference implementations contain dozens of further choices, most of them absent from the paper, and a handful of them (normalization, initialization, annealing, value and gradient clipping) account for much of the gap between a textbook PPO and one that matches published results.

Why the details decide everything

Engstrom et al. (2020) took the “code-level optimizations” in OpenAI's PPO implementation (value clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal initialization, learning-rate annealing, observation normalization and clipping, tanh activations, gradient clipping) and toggled them. PPO stripped of them performed roughly like TRPO, while TRPO given them matched or beat standard PPO: much of the improvement credited to the clipped objective came from the optimizations. Andrychowicz et al. (2021) trained over 250,000 agents to map which on-policy design choices matter. Huang et al. (2022) then reproduced OpenAI Baselines' ppo2 line by line in CleanRL and wrote down every choice that affects results: 37 of them. Observation and reward normalization

Observations are standardized with a running mean and variance, \(\tilde s = (s - \hat\mu)/\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2 + 10^{-8}}\), then clipped to \([-10, 10]\). MuJoCo observations mix positions near 1 with velocities in the tens; a tanh network fed those saturates, and its gradients vanish. Andrychowicz et al. (2021) found observation normalization one of the most important choices of all. The statistics are part of the trained agent: save them, and freeze them at evaluation (in Gymnasium 1.x, set update_running_mean = False on the wrapper and copy its obs_rms).

Rewards are scaled, not standardized: each reward is divided by a running estimate of the standard deviation of the discounted return, \(\tilde r_t = r_t / \sqrt{\widehat{\Var}[\mathcal R_t] + 10^{-8}}\) where \(\mathcal R_t = \gamma \mathcal R_{t-1} + r_t\), with no mean subtracted, then clipped to \([-10, 10]\). The goal is value targets of order 1, so the critic's loss, the value clipping range and the shared-trunk loss weights all live on a sensible scale. Not centring matters: subtracting a mean from rewards would change which policies are optimal (for example, by making “survive longer” look bad), while dividing by a positive constant does not.

Algorithm · running normalization (as in Baselines' VecNormalize and Gymnasium's wrappers)
  1. Keep running estimates \((\hat\mu_s, \hat\sigma^2_s)\) of the observation mean and variance, and \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathcal R}\) of the discounted-return variance, merged batch by batch with the parallel (Chan et al.) variance update.
  2. On every step, for each environment:
    1. Update \((\hat\mu_s, \hat\sigma^2_s)\) with \(s_t\); emit \(\tilde s_t = \clip\big((s_t - \hat\mu_s)/\sqrt{\hat\sigma^2_s + 10^{-8}}, -10, 10\big)\).
    2. \(\mathcal R \leftarrow \gamma\,\mathcal R + r_t\); update \(\hat\sigma^2_{\mathcal R}\) with \(\mathcal R\); emit \(\tilde r_t = \clip\big(r_t / \sqrt{\hat\sigma^2_{\mathcal R} + 10^{-8}}, -10, 10\big)\).
    3. If the episode ended, reset \(\mathcal R \leftarrow 0\). (the statistics themselves are never reset)
  3. At evaluation, stop updating the statistics and use the saved ones.

Advantage normalization per minibatch

Within each minibatch, advantages are standardized to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (with \(10^{-8}\) in the denominator), as in Day 91's ppo_update. This is Day 85's normalization at a finer grain: a per-minibatch baseline plus an adaptive step size. It applies to the policy loss only; the critic regresses onto unnormalized λ-returns. It is one of the details that matter most when reward scales vary, and it is fragile with tiny minibatches.

Orthogonal initialization with specific gains

Every linear layer's weights are initialized orthogonally and its biases to zero, with three different gains: \(\sqrt 2\) for hidden layers, 0.01 for the policy's output layer, and 1 for the value output layer. The small policy gain makes the initial logits (or Gaussian means) nearly zero, so the first policy is close to uniform, or a zero-mean Gaussian with \(\sigma = 1\) when \(\log\sigma\) starts at 0. The agent starts at maximal exploration and with no accidental preference baked in. Andrychowicz et al. (2021) singled out the small last-layer initialization as important. Orthogonal hidden layers preserve the norms of activations and gradients through depth better than PyTorch's default uniform initialization.

Learning-rate annealing and Adam's ε

The Adam learning rate decays linearly from its initial value (\(2.5 \times 10^{-4}\) on Atari, \(3 \times 10^{-4}\) on MuJoCo) to zero over the run: at update \(k\) of \(K\), \(\alpha_k = \alpha_0 (1 - k/K)\). Late in training the policy is near a good solution and large steps mostly do damage, so shrinking them stabilizes the final performance. The cost is that results depend on the total budget: a run annealed over 1M steps is not the first 1M steps of a 10M-step run. Adam's \(\epsilon\) is set to \(10^{-5}\) rather than PyTorch's default \(10^{-8}\), which damps updates for parameters whose gradient second moments are tiny (Day 5).

Value-function clipping

$$L^V = \tfrac12 \max\Big[\big(V_\theta(s_t) - V^{\mathrm{targ}}_t\big)^2,\; \big(V_{\text{old}}(s_t) + \clip(V_\theta(s_t) - V_{\text{old}}(s_t), -\varepsilon, \varepsilon) - V^{\mathrm{targ}}_t\big)^2\Big]$$

The value analogue of the policy clip, with the same pessimistic shape you explored in Day 91's demo: within one batch, don't move a state's value prediction more than \(\varepsilon\) from what it was during collection, using the larger of the two losses. It came from ppo2, reuses the policy's \(\varepsilon\) even though values and ratios have nothing to do with each other, and only makes sense when rewards are normalized, because \(\varepsilon\) is in units of return. The evidence is unflattering: Engstrom et al. (2020) found no sign that it helps, and Andrychowicz et al. (2021) found it can hurt. CleanRL keeps it on by default for fidelity to the reference; the clip_vloss flag in Day 91's code turns it off.

Gradient clipping at 0.5

After backward(), the gradients of all parameters, actor and critic together, are rescaled so that their global L2 norm is at most 0.5. Rare minibatches with extreme advantages or value errors would otherwise produce updates far larger than the trust region intends. Because the norm is global, a huge critic gradient also shrinks the actor's step in a shared-optimizer setup; log the pre-clipping norm to see how often clipping is active.

Shared vs separate networks, and the entropy coefficient

On Atari the policy and value heads share a Nature CNN (Day 74) and the losses are combined as \(L = L^{\pi} - c_e \mathcal H + c_v L^V\) with \(c_v = 0.5\). On MuJoCo the actor and critic are separate 64–64 tanh MLPs, but still trained with one optimizer on the same combined loss. A useful consequence: with separate networks and Adam, \(c_v\) barely matters, since Adam's per-parameter normalization makes the critic's update nearly invariant to scaling its loss (only \(\epsilon\) and the global gradient clipping couple the two). With a shared trunk, \(c_v\) sets how much the representation serves the value function, and getting it wrong is a common failure. For continuous actions the policy is a diagonal Gaussian with a state-independent, learned \(\log\sigma\) initialized to 0; samples are drawn unbounded, stored unclipped (so their log-probabilities are correct), and clipped only on the way into the environment.

The entropy coefficient is 0.01 on Atari and 0 on MuJoCo. With a learned state-independent \(\log\sigma\), the Gaussian's entropy is just \(\sum_i \log\sigma_i\) plus a constant, so an entropy bonus would act directly on the exploration noise; the MuJoCo defaults leave \(\sigma\) to the policy gradient alone. For discrete actions with sparse rewards, the entropy bonus is often the difference between learning and collapsing onto one action.

The complete implementation

Part 2: environments and agent. Part 3: the rollout loop. Together with Day 91's ppo_update (Part 1) this is a complete continuous-control PPO for Gymnasium ≥ 1.0.

import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import gymnasium as gym
from torch.distributions import Normal

def make_env(env_id, gamma):
    def thunk():
        env = gym.make(env_id)
        env = gym.wrappers.RecordEpisodeStatistics(env)      # raw returns, before any rescaling
        env = gym.wrappers.ClipAction(env)                   # env sees clipped actions; we store raw ones
        env = gym.wrappers.NormalizeObservation(env)         # running mean / std
        env = gym.wrappers.TransformObservation(env, lambda o: np.clip(o, -10, 10), env.observation_space)
        env = gym.wrappers.NormalizeReward(env, gamma=gamma) # divide by std of the discounted return
        env = gym.wrappers.TransformReward(env, lambda r: np.clip(r, -10, 10))
        return env
    return thunk

def layer_init(layer, gain=np.sqrt(2), bias=0.0):
    nn.init.orthogonal_(layer.weight, gain)
    nn.init.constant_(layer.bias, bias)
    return layer

def mlp(n_in, n_out, out_gain):
    return nn.Sequential(layer_init(nn.Linear(n_in, 64)), nn.Tanh(),
                         layer_init(nn.Linear(64, 64)), nn.Tanh(),
                         layer_init(nn.Linear(64, n_out), gain=out_gain))

class Agent(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.critic = mlp(obs_dim, 1, out_gain=1.0)              # separate networks (MuJoCo default)
        self.actor_mean = mlp(obs_dim, act_dim, out_gain=0.01)   # near-zero initial mean
        self.actor_logstd = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(act_dim))   # state-independent, sigma starts at 1

    def value(self, x):
        return self.critic(x).squeeze(-1)

    def act(self, x, action=None):
        mean = self.actor_mean(x)
        dist = Normal(mean, self.actor_logstd.exp().expand_as(mean))
        if action is None:
            action = dist.sample()
        # independent action dimensions: log-probs and entropies add up
        return action, dist.log_prob(action).sum(-1), dist.entropy().sum(-1), self.value(x)

env_id, seed, num_envs, num_steps, total_steps = "Hopper-v5", 1, 1, 2048, 1_000_000
gamma, lam, lr = 0.99, 0.95, 3e-4

torch.manual_seed(seed)
envs = gym.vector.SyncVectorEnv([make_env(env_id, gamma) for _ in range(num_envs)])
obs_dim = envs.single_observation_space.shape[0]
act_dim = envs.single_action_space.shape[0]
agent = Agent(obs_dim, act_dim)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(agent.parameters(), lr=lr, eps=1e-5)        # eps 1e-5, not 1e-8

shape = (num_steps, num_envs)
obs, actions = torch.zeros(shape + (obs_dim,)), torch.zeros(shape + (act_dim,))
logps, rewards, values, terms, dones, valid = (torch.zeros(shape) for _ in range(6))
next_obs = torch.as_tensor(envs.reset(seed=seed)[0], dtype=torch.float32)
prev_done = np.zeros(num_envs, dtype=bool)          # Gymnasium 1.x next-step autoreset (Day 87)
n_updates, global_step = total_steps // (num_envs * num_steps), 0

for update in range(n_updates):
    opt.param_groups[0]["lr"] = lr * (1.0 - update / n_updates)   # linear annealing towards 0
    for t in range(num_steps):
        global_step += num_envs
        obs[t] = next_obs
        with torch.no_grad():
            actions[t], logps[t], _, values[t] = agent.act(next_obs)
        o, r, term, trunc, info = envs.step(actions[t].numpy())
        rewards[t] = torch.as_tensor(r, dtype=torch.float32)
        terms[t] = torch.as_tensor(term, dtype=torch.float32)
        dones[t] = torch.as_tensor(term | trunc, dtype=torch.float32)
        valid[t] = torch.as_tensor(~prev_done, dtype=torch.float32)  # reset steps ignore the action
        prev_done = term | trunc
        next_obs = torch.as_tensor(o, dtype=torch.float32)
        if "episode" in info:
            for i in np.flatnonzero(info["_episode"]):
                print(f"step {global_step:8d}  return {info['episode']['r'][i]:8.1f}")

    with torch.no_grad():                           # GAE (Day 88), bootstrapping through truncation
        adv, last = torch.zeros(shape), torch.zeros(num_envs)
        next_value = agent.value(next_obs)
        for t in reversed(range(num_steps)):
            v_next = next_value if t == num_steps - 1 else values[t + 1]
            delta = rewards[t] + gamma * (1 - terms[t]) * v_next - values[t]
            last = delta + gamma * lam * (1 - dones[t]) * last
            adv[t] = last
        returns = adv + values

    keep = valid.flatten().bool()                   # drop autoreset rows
    batch = {k: x.flatten(0, 1)[keep] for k, x in dict(obs=obs, actions=actions, logp=logps,
             adv=adv, returns=returns, values=values).items()}
    stats = ppo_update(agent, opt, batch)           # Part 1 (Day 91): 10 epochs x 32 minibatches
    ev = 1 - (batch["returns"] - batch["values"]).var() / batch["returns"].var()
    print(f"update {update:3d}  kl {stats['approx_kl']:.4f}  clipfrac {stats['clipfrac']:.3f}  "
          f"entropy {stats['entropy']:.2f}  explained var {ev:.2f}")

Reading the debug variables

What to log, and rough healthy ranges
  • Approximate KL per update, using the estimator \(\hat\E[(r - 1) - \ln r]\), which is unbiased and never negative (Schulman 2020). Typical values are around 0.01–0.02 for the defaults above; persistent values several times larger mean the steps are too big.
  • Clip fraction. Commonly 0.05–0.2. Near zero means the clip never binds (learning rate may be too low); much higher means most samples are saturated.
  • Policy entropy, or \(\log\sigma\) for Gaussians. It should fall slowly. A cliff is premature collapse; a rise usually means the advantages carry no signal.
  • Explained variance of the critic, \(1 - \Var[V^{\mathrm{targ}} - V]/\Var[V^{\mathrm{targ}}]\). It should climb towards 1. Persistently near 0 or negative means the critic is useless and the advantages are mostly noise.
  • Value loss, pre-clipping gradient norm, steps per second, and the learning rate itself.
What breaks
  • Normalization statistics not saved. An agent evaluated with fresh observation statistics sees a different input distribution and performs badly. Save them with the checkpoint.
  • Logging the normalized reward. Curves in the wrong units, and comparisons with papers that make no sense.
  • Clipping the stored action. Storing the clipped action makes the stored log-probability wrong for the action whose log-prob you later recompute, and biases the ratio at the action bounds.
  • Comparing against a baseline with different details. “My PPO beats theirs” often means “my implementation details differ from theirs” (Engstrom et al. 2020). State them.
  • Annealing tied to the budget. Changing total_steps changes the learning-rate schedule, so short debugging runs are not small versions of long runs.

DAY 93

DPG & DDPG

45m video105m practice

For a deterministic policy \(a = \mu_\theta(s)\), the policy gradient is the critic's action-gradient chained through the actor: \(\nabla J = \E\big[\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a Q(s, a)\big|_{a = \mu_\theta(s)}\big]\). There is no integral over actions, so it can be estimated off-policy from a replay buffer. DDPG is this idea plus DQN's machinery, and it is famously brittle.

Why a deterministic policy

The stochastic policy gradient averages over both states and actions, and in high-dimensional action spaces the action average needs many samples. Silver et al. (2014) asked what happens if the policy is deterministic, \(\mu_\theta: \mathcal S \to \mathcal A \subseteq \R^d\). The score function no longer exists (there is no density), so Day 83's theorem doesn't apply, but a different gradient does, and it only averages over states. It also reconnects with value-based RL: instead of maximizing \(Q(s, \cdot)\) by search, as DQN does over a discrete set, train a network \(\mu\) to output the maximizing action, by gradient ascent on \(Q\).

The deterministic policy gradient theorem

Theorem · deterministic policy gradient (Silver et al. 2014)

Suppose \(p(s' \mid s, a)\), \(r(s, a)\), \(\mu_\theta(s)\) and their derivatives in \(a\) and \(\theta\) are continuous. With \(J(\theta) = \E_{s_0}\big[V^{\mu}(s_0)\big]\) and \(\rho^\mu\) the discounted state visitation,

$$\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \int_{\mathcal S} \rho^\mu(s)\, \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a Q^\mu(s, a)\big|_{a = \mu_\theta(s)}\, ds = \E_{s \sim \rho^\mu}\Big[\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a Q^\mu(s, a)\big|_{a = \mu_\theta(s)}\Big].$$
Proof sketch · the same recursion as Day 83

Since the policy is deterministic, \(V^\mu(s) = Q^\mu(s, \mu_\theta(s))\), and \(Q^\mu(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma \int p(s' \mid s, a)\, V^\mu(s')\, ds'\). Differentiate \(V^\mu(s)\) with respect to \(\theta\); \(\theta\) enters through the action \(\mu_\theta(s)\) and through \(V^\mu\) at the next state:

$$\begin{aligned} \nabla_\theta V^\mu(s) &= \nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a \Big[r(s, a) + \gamma \int p(s' \mid s, a)\, V^\mu(s')\, ds'\Big]_{a = \mu_\theta(s)} + \gamma \int p(s' \mid s, \mu_\theta(s))\, \nabla_\theta V^\mu(s')\, ds' \\ &= \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a Q^\mu(s, a)\big|_{a = \mu_\theta(s)}}_{\text{local term}} + \gamma \int p(s' \mid s, \mu_\theta(s))\, \nabla_\theta V^\mu(s')\, ds'. \end{aligned}$$

Again a Bellman equation for the gradient, with a local “reward”. Unrolling it, exactly as on Day 83, weights the local term at every state by \(\sum_t \gamma^t p(s \to s', t, \mu)\); integrating over \(s_0\) gives \(\rho^\mu\). Note the shape: \(\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)\) is a \(\dim\theta \times d\) Jacobian and \(\nabla_a Q\) a \(d\)-vector, and the product is exactly what backpropagating \(Q(s, \mu_\theta(s))\) into \(\theta\) computes. \(\blacksquare\)

Silver et al. also show that the deterministic gradient is the limit of the stochastic policy gradient as the policy's variance goes to zero, so the two theorems are one family. Practically, the actor's loss is just \(-Q(s, \mu_\theta(s))\), averaged over states, with gradients flowing through the critic into the action but only the actor's parameters updated.

Off-policy, and why it must be

A deterministic policy does not explore: run \(\mu_\theta\) and you only ever see one action per state, so the critic learns nothing about \(\nabla_a Q\) except through generalization. The fix is to act with a behaviour policy \(\beta\), for instance \(\mu_\theta(s)\) plus noise, and learn off-policy. Two things make that easy here:

  • The actor update has no integral over actions, so there is no action importance ratio. Evaluated on states from \(\beta\), \(\E_{s \sim \rho^\beta}\big[\nabla_\theta\mu_\theta(s)\, \nabla_a Q(s, a)|_{a=\mu_\theta(s)}\big]\) is the off-policy deterministic gradient. (Like the off-policy actor–critic of Degris et al. 2012, it drops a term involving \(\nabla_\theta Q^\mu\) and uses the behaviour state distribution; it is an approximation, but a well-behaved one.)
  • The critic is learned by Q-learning-style targets, \(y = r + \gamma Q(s', \mu_\theta(s'))\), whose next action comes from the target policy, not from \(\beta\). So transitions from any behaviour policy, including old ones in a replay buffer, are valid training data.

That is the deep insight behind DDPG: a continuous-action Q-learning in which the max is replaced by a learned maximizer.

DDPG: actor, critic and both targets

Algorithm · DDPG (Lillicrap et al. 2016)
  1. Initialize actor \(\mu\), critic \(Q\), targets \(\mu' \leftarrow \mu\), \(Q' \leftarrow Q\), replay buffer \(\mathcal D\).
  2. For each environment step:
    1. Act with \(a = \clip(\mu(s) + \mathcal N_t, a_{\text{low}}, a_{\text{high}})\); store \((s, a, r, s', \text{term})\) in \(\mathcal D\).
    2. Sample a minibatch. Critic target \(y = r + \gamma(1 - \text{term})\, Q'(s', \mu'(s'))\) (no gradient).
    3. Update the critic by minimizing \(\frac1B \sum (Q(s, a) - y)^2\).
    4. Update the actor by minimizing \(-\frac1B \sum Q(s, \mu(s))\) with respect to the actor's parameters only.
    5. Soft-update both targets: \(\theta' \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1 - \tau)\theta'\).

The paper's settings, as reported in its supplementary material: Adam with learning rates \(10^{-4}\) (actor) and \(10^{-3}\) (critic), \(L_2\) weight decay \(10^{-2}\) on the critic, \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\tau = 0.001\), hidden layers of 400 and 300 units, actions entering the critic at its second layer, final-layer weights initialized in \([-3\times10^{-3}, 3\times10^{-3}]\), batch normalization, minibatches of 64, a buffer of \(10^6\), and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise with \(\theta = 0.15, \sigma = 0.2\). Modern re-implementations usually drop batch norm and weight decay and use 256-unit layers, \(\tau = 0.005\) and Gaussian noise.

import copy
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class Actor(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim, act_high):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(obs_dim, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(256, act_dim), nn.Tanh())
        self.register_buffer("scale", torch.as_tensor(act_high, dtype=torch.float32))

    def forward(self, s):
        return self.scale * self.net(s)          # deterministic action in [-high, high]

class Critic(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(obs_dim + act_dim, 256), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 1))

    def forward(self, s, a):
        return self.net(torch.cat([s, a], -1)).squeeze(-1)

def soft_update(target, source, tau):
    with torch.no_grad():
        for tp, p in zip(target.parameters(), source.parameters()):
            tp.mul_(1 - tau).add_(tau * p)

def ddpg_update(batch, actor, critic, actor_t, critic_t, opt_a, opt_c, gamma=0.99, tau=0.005):
    s, a, r, s2, term = batch                    # float tensors; term = 1 only for true terminations
    with torch.no_grad():
        y = r + gamma * (1 - term) * critic_t(s2, actor_t(s2))
    critic_loss = F.mse_loss(critic(s, a), y)
    opt_c.zero_grad(); critic_loss.backward(); opt_c.step()

    actor_loss = -critic(s, actor(s)).mean()     # ascend Q(s, mu(s)) through the critic
    opt_a.zero_grad(); actor_loss.backward(); opt_a.step()   # only the actor's parameters move
    soft_update(actor_t, actor, tau)
    soft_update(critic_t, critic, tau)
    return critic_loss.item(), -actor_loss.item()

The DDPG update, as a fragment of a training loop with a replay buffer (Day 72). The actor's backward pass also deposits gradients in the critic's parameters; they are harmless because opt_c.zero_grad() clears them before the next critic step. Ornstein–Uhlenbeck vs Gaussian action noise

DDPG's exploration noise was an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, \(dx_t = \theta(\bar x - x_t)\,dt + \sigma\, dW_t\): Brownian motion pulled back towards a mean \(\bar x = 0\). Its samples are temporally correlated, with correlation time \(1/\theta\) and stationary standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{2\theta}\). The motivation was physical control with inertia, where independent noise at every step averages out before it moves the system anywhere, while correlated noise produces sustained pushes. Implementations discretize it with different time steps \(dt\), which changes the correlation time in steps by orders of magnitude:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def ou_noise(n, theta=0.15, sigma=0.2, dt=1e-2):
    """Euler-discretized Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process: dx = theta * (0 - x) dt + sigma dW."""
    x, out = 0.0, np.empty(n)
    for i in range(n):
        x += theta * (0.0 - x) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.normal()
        out[i] = x
    return out

acf = lambda z, k: np.corrcoef(z[:-k], z[k:])[0, 1]     # autocorrelation at lag k
noises = {"gaussian 0.1": 0.1 * rng.normal(size=200_000),
          "OU, dt=0.01": ou_noise(200_000, dt=1e-2),
          "OU, dt=1": ou_noise(200_000, dt=1.0)}
for name, z in noises.items():
    print(f"{name:13s} std={z.std():.3f}  "
          + "  ".join(f"acf[{k}]={acf(z, k):+.2f}" for k in (1, 10, 100)))
# gaussian 0.1  std=0.100  acf[1]=-0.00  acf[10]=-0.00  acf[100]=-0.00
# OU, dt=0.01   std=0.360  acf[1]=+1.00  acf[10]=+0.98  acf[100]=+0.86
# OU, dt=1      std=0.379  acf[1]=+0.85  acf[10]=+0.19  acf[100]=+0.01

Same \(\theta\) and \(\sigma\), two discretizations: one noise is nearly constant over a hundred steps, the other forgets in about ten. Both are almost four times larger than the Gaussian noise modern implementations use.

Fujimoto et al. (2018) found that plain uncorrelated Gaussian noise \(\mathcal N(0, 0.1)\) (scaled to the action range) works as well as OU on the MuJoCo benchmarks, and it has become the default. Noise in parameter space rather than action space (Plappert et al. 2018) is a third option that gives temporally consistent, state-dependent exploration.

Soft target updates

DDPG updates its targets every step by Polyak averaging, \(\theta' \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1 - \tau)\theta'\), instead of DQN's periodic hard copies (Day 73). With \(\tau = 0.001\) the target networks lag the online ones by roughly \(1/\tau = 1000\) updates. Both targets are needed: the target action \(\mu'(s')\) comes from the target actor, and without it the critic's target would move every time the actor does, which is the same moving-target instability that made naive deep Q-learning diverge.

What breaks · DDPG's notorious brittleness
  • The actor exploits the critic. Gradient ascent on \(Q\) seeks out actions where the critic is wrong in the optimistic direction, and bootstrapping propagates that optimism. Q-values climb while returns fall. Day 94 is about this.
  • Sharp peaks in Q. A deterministic actor can lock onto a narrow spike of the critic that no real action region supports.
  • Hyperparameter and seed sensitivity. Henderson et al. (2018) used DDPG to show how much results move with seeds, network sizes and reward scales. Returns often collapse mid-training and recover, or don't.
  • Reward scale. Critic targets scale with rewards, and with them the actor's gradient. Rescaling rewards by 10 can change a working run into a failing one.
  • Noise conventions. OU with \(dt = 0.01\) vs \(dt = 1\) are different algorithms; papers rarely say which they used.

DAY 94

TD3

45m video105m practice

TD3 is DDPG with three fixes aimed at one disease: approximation error in the critic that the actor learns to exploit. Take the minimum of two critics in the target, update the actor less often than the critics, and smooth the target action with clipped noise.

Overestimation in actor–critic

Q-learning overestimates because its target takes a max over noisy estimates (Days 47 and 75). An actor–critic has no explicit max, but gradient ascent on the critic is an implicit one. Fujimoto, van Hoof & Meger (2018) make the argument precise: if the critic's error is zero-mean, the policy obtained by a small gradient step on the approximate critic still has an expected approximate value at least as high as the true-gradient policy's, and under mild conditions this produces an expected overestimate of the true value. Bootstrapping then carries the overestimate into every state's target, and it compounds. Thrun & Schwartz (1993) had identified the same mechanism for function approximation in general.

They demonstrated it directly on Hopper and Walker2d: over a million steps, DDPG's value estimate climbs well above the true discounted return measured by rolling out its policy, while the clipped double-Q version below stays close to, or slightly under, the truth. The toy below reproduces the mechanism in one state.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
a = np.linspace(-1, 1, 401)                     # 1-D action space, a single state
q_true = 1 - a ** 2                             # true Q(s, a): best action 0, value 1

def critic():
    """Truth plus a smooth, zero-mean approximation error (random-phase cosines)."""
    w, phi = rng.uniform(1, 6, 8), rng.uniform(0, 2 * np.pi, 8)
    c = rng.normal(0, 0.1, 8)
    return q_true + (c[:, None] * np.cos(w[:, None] * a + phi[:, None])).sum(0)

single, clipped, lost = [], [], []
for _ in range(5000):
    q1, q2 = critic(), critic()
    i = q1.argmax()                             # a fully trained actor maximises its own critic
    single.append(q1[i] - q_true[i])            # DDPG: the target evaluates that action with Q1
    clipped.append(min(q1[i], q2[i]) - q_true[i])   # TD3: min over two independent critics
    lost.append(1 - q_true[i])                  # true value given up by trusting the critic
print(f"bias of Q1 at the actor's action: {np.mean(single):+.3f}")    # about +0.17
print(f"bias of min(Q1, Q2):              {np.mean(clipped):+.3f}")   # about -0.04
print(f"true value lost:                  {np.mean(lost):.3f}")       # about 0.07

Every critic is unbiased at every action, yet the critic's value at the action its actor picks is biased upward by about 0.17. An independent second critic is unbiased there, so the minimum of the two errs slightly low.

Why not Double DQN's fix (select with the online network, evaluate with the target)? In actor–critic the policy changes slowly, so the online and target networks are too similar to give an independent evaluation, and Fujimoto et al. found that version barely helped.

Fix 1: clipped double-Q learning

Train two critics \(Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}\) with their own target networks, and give both the same target, built from the smaller of the two target values:

$$y = r + \gamma\,(1 - \text{term})\, \min_{i = 1, 2} Q_{\phi'_i}(s', \tilde a).$$

The minimum can underestimate, and that is a deliberate trade: an underestimated action is simply avoided by the actor, so the error does not get amplified through the policy update the way an overestimate does. The actor is trained on \(Q_{\phi_1}\) only.

Fix 2: delayed policy updates

Update the critics every step but the actor (and all target networks) only every \(d = 2\) steps. A critic that has just changed has high-variance errors; letting it reduce them before the actor follows its gradient means the actor climbs a better estimate, and fewer target updates means slower-moving, lower-variance targets. It is the actor–critic version of “evaluate before you improve”.

Fix 3: target policy smoothing

Compute the target action with clipped noise:

$$\tilde a = \clip\Big(\mu_{\theta'}(s') + \clip(\epsilon, -c, c),\; a_{\text{low}}, a_{\text{high}}\Big), \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \tilde\sigma^2),\; \tilde\sigma = 0.2,\; c = 0.5.$$

This averages the target over a small neighbourhood of the target action, which encodes the prior that similar actions have similar values and stops the critic from developing narrow peaks that a deterministic actor would latch onto. It is a SARSA-like expected target over a smoothed policy, and a regularizer on the critic.

Algorithm · TD3 (Fujimoto et al. 2018)
  1. Initialize critics \(Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}\) and actor \(\mu_\theta\) independently; targets \(\phi'_i \leftarrow \phi_i\), \(\theta' \leftarrow \theta\); replay buffer \(\mathcal D\).
  2. For \(t = 1, 2, \dots\):
    1. Act with \(a = \clip(\mu_\theta(s) + \epsilon, a_{\text{low}}, a_{\text{high}})\), \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.1^2)\); store \((s, a, r, s', \text{term})\).
    2. Sample a minibatch; \(\tilde a \leftarrow \clip\big(\mu_{\theta'}(s') + \clip(\tilde\epsilon, -c, c), a_{\text{low}}, a_{\text{high}}\big)\), \(\tilde\epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \tilde\sigma^2)\). (fix 3)
    3. \(y \leftarrow r + \gamma(1 - \text{term}) \min_{i} Q_{\phi'_i}(s', \tilde a)\). (fix 1)
    4. Update both critics: \(\phi_i \leftarrow \argmin_{\phi_i} \frac1B \sum (y - Q_{\phi_i}(s, a))^2\) (one gradient step).
    5. If \(t \bmod d = 0\): (fix 2)
      1. Update the actor by the deterministic policy gradient on \(Q_{\phi_1}\): minimize \(-\frac1B \sum Q_{\phi_1}(s, \mu_\theta(s))\).
      2. Soft-update all targets: \(\phi'_i \leftarrow \tau\phi_i + (1 - \tau)\phi'_i\), \(\theta' \leftarrow \tau\theta + (1 - \tau)\theta'\).

Implementation walkthrough

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def td3_update(it, batch, actor, actor_t, q1, q2, q1_t, q2_t, opt_actor, opt_q, act_high,
               gamma=0.99, tau=0.005, policy_noise=0.2, noise_clip=0.5, policy_delay=2):
    """One TD3 step. Actor/Critic/soft_update as on Day 93; opt_q covers q1 and q2 together;
    act_high is a tensor of action bounds (symmetric)."""
    s, a, r, s2, term = batch
    with torch.no_grad():
        noise = (torch.randn_like(a) * policy_noise).clamp(-noise_clip, noise_clip) * act_high
        a2 = (actor_t(s2) + noise).clamp(-act_high, act_high)            # fix 3: smoothing
        y = r + gamma * (1 - term) * torch.min(q1_t(s2, a2), q2_t(s2, a2))  # fix 1: clipped double Q
    q_loss = F.mse_loss(q1(s, a), y) + F.mse_loss(q2(s, a), y)          # both critics, same target
    opt_q.zero_grad(); q_loss.backward(); opt_q.step()

    if it % policy_delay == 0:                                            # fix 2: delayed updates
        actor_loss = -q1(s, actor(s)).mean()                             # Q1 only
        opt_actor.zero_grad(); actor_loss.backward(); opt_actor.step()
        for target, source in ((actor_t, actor), (q1_t, q1), (q2_t, q2)):
            soft_update(target, source, tau)
    return q_loss.item()

The whole of TD3 beyond DDPG fits in a dozen lines. Two critics with independent initializations matter: initialize them separately, never as copies of each other.

What breaks
  • Critics that are copies. If \(Q_2\) starts as a deep copy of \(Q_1\) and sees the same minibatches, their errors are highly correlated and the minimum does little.
  • Noise in the wrong units. Policy and exploration noise are fractions of the action range; on an environment with bounds \(\pm 2\) (Pendulum), unscaled noise is half as large as intended.
  • Too much underestimation. On tasks with sparse or deceptive rewards the pessimistic target can stall learning. Later methods tune the degree of pessimism (for example by using more critics or a quantile of them).
  • Bootstrapping through termination. Same rule as always: term, not term or trunc.

Maximum-Entropy RL & SAC

50m video120m practice

Add the policy's entropy to every reward and the optimal policy becomes a Boltzmann distribution over soft Q-values: as random as possible while still collecting reward. Soft Actor-Critic learns it off-policy with twin critics, a reparameterized squashed-Gaussian actor and a temperature that tunes itself, and that combination made it the default baseline for continuous control.

The maximum-entropy objective

Definition · maximum-entropy RL $$J(\pi) = \sum_{t} \E_{(s_t, a_t) \sim \rho_\pi}\Big[\gamma^t\Big(r(s_t, a_t) + \alpha\, \mathcal H\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big)\Big], \qquad \mathcal H\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\big) = -\E_{a \sim \pi}\big[\log \pi(a \mid s)\big].$$

The temperature \(\alpha > 0\) sets the exchange rate between reward and randomness. As \(\alpha \to 0\) the standard objective returns.

Unlike Day 86's entropy bonus, which only regularizes the current step's action distribution, this objective rewards future entropy too: the agent prefers to reach states where it will have many good options. The consequences are practical. Exploration is built into the objective rather than bolted on. The policy keeps every near-optimal action alive instead of committing to one arbitrarily, which makes it more robust to perturbations and gives a better starting point for fine-tuning. And the optimization landscape is smoother. The framework comes from Ziebart's maximum-causal-entropy work (which Day 107 uses for inverse RL) and the view of control as probabilistic inference (Levine 2018).

Soft value functions and the soft Bellman equation

Definition · soft value functions $$Q^\pi(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma\, \E_{s'}\big[V^\pi(s')\big], \qquad V^\pi(s) = \E_{a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)}\big[Q^\pi(s, a) - \alpha \log \pi(a \mid s)\big].$$

Combined, the soft Bellman equation for policy evaluation is

$$Q^\pi(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma\, \E_{s' \sim p,\; a' \sim \pi}\big[Q^\pi(s', a') - \alpha \log \pi(a' \mid s')\big].$$

This is ordinary policy evaluation with an entropy-augmented reward, so the soft Bellman backup is a \(\gamma\)-contraction and iterating it converges (Haarnoja et al. 2018a, Lemma 1). The optimal soft values satisfy a soft version of the optimality equation, with the max replaced by a log-sum-exp:

$$V^*(s) = \alpha \log \int_{\mathcal A} \exp\big(Q^*(s, a)/\alpha\big)\, da, \qquad \pi^*(a \mid s) = \exp\Big(\frac{Q^*(s, a) - V^*(s)}{\alpha}\Big).$$

The optimal policy is a Boltzmann distribution over soft Q-values, and as \(\alpha \to 0\) the log-sum-exp becomes a hard max and \(\pi^*\) becomes greedy.

Derivation · soft policy improvement is a KL projection

Soft policy iteration improves \(\pi\) by projecting the Boltzmann policy of its own soft Q onto the policy class \(\Pi\):

$$\pi_{\text{new}}(\cdot \mid s) = \argmin_{\pi' \in \Pi} \KL\Big(\pi'(\cdot \mid s) \,\Big\|\, \frac{\exp(Q^{\pi_{\text{old}}}(s, \cdot)/\alpha)}{Z(s)}\Big).$$

Expand the KL: \(\E_{a \sim \pi'}\big[\log \pi'(a \mid s) - Q(s, a)/\alpha + \log Z(s)\big] = \frac1\alpha\, \E_{a \sim \pi'}\big[\alpha \log \pi'(a \mid s) - Q(s, a)\big] + \log Z(s)\). \(Z\) does not depend on \(\pi'\), so the projection minimizes \(\E_{a \sim \pi'}\big[\alpha \log \pi'(a \mid s) - Q(s, a)\big]\), which is exactly SAC's actor loss. Haarnoja et al. prove that this step never decreases the soft Q-function, and that alternating evaluation and improvement converges to the optimal policy in \(\Pi\) (in the tabular case).

The SAC algorithm, and twin critics

SAC approximates soft policy iteration with networks and SGD, off-policy from a replay buffer. The first version (Haarnoja et al. 2018a) trained a separate state-value network; the second (Haarnoja et al. 2018b) dropped it, borrowed TD3's clipped double-Q, and added automatic temperature tuning. That second version is what “SAC” now means.

Algorithm · Soft Actor-Critic (Haarnoja et al. 2018b)
  1. Initialize policy \(\pi_\theta\), critics \(Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}\), target critics \(\bar\phi_i \leftarrow \phi_i\), temperature \(\log\alpha\), replay buffer \(\mathcal D\).
  2. For each environment step: sample \(a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\), step, store \((s, a, r, s', \text{term})\). Then, for each gradient step (usually one):
    1. Sample a minibatch. For each \(s'\), sample a fresh \(\tilde a' \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s')\) from the current policy.
    2. Soft target: \(y = r + \gamma(1 - \text{term})\big(\min_i Q_{\bar\phi_i}(s', \tilde a') - \alpha \log \pi_\theta(\tilde a' \mid s')\big)\) (no gradient).
    3. Critics: minimize \(\sum_i (Q_{\phi_i}(s, a) - y)^2\).
    4. Actor: minimize \(\E_{s, \epsilon}\big[\alpha \log \pi_\theta(\tilde a_\theta(s, \epsilon) \mid s) - \min_i Q_{\phi_i}(s, \tilde a_\theta(s, \epsilon))\big]\).
    5. Temperature: minimize \(-\alpha\, \E\big[\log \pi_\theta(\tilde a \mid s) + \bar{\mathcal H}\big]\) over \(\log\alpha\).
    6. Polyak-average the target critics with \(\tau = 0.005\).

There is no target actor: the policy is stochastic, so the target action is sampled from the current policy, and that sampling already smooths the target the way TD3's noise does. Defaults from the 2018b paper: Adam with learning rate \(3\times10^{-4}\) for everything, batch 256, replay \(10^6\), two hidden layers of 256, \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\tau = 0.005\), one gradient step per environment step, target entropy \(-\dim\mathcal A\).

The reparameterization trick for the actor

The actor loss is an expectation over actions sampled from the policy being optimized. Day 4 offered two ways to differentiate such an expectation. The score-function route is possible but noisy. Because \(Q\) is a differentiable network, SAC uses the reparameterization route instead: write the action as a deterministic function of the state and independent noise,

$$\tilde a_\theta(s, \epsilon) = \tanh\big(\mu_\theta(s) + \sigma_\theta(s) \odot \epsilon\big), \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I),$$

so the loss becomes \(\E_{s \sim \mathcal D,\, \epsilon}\big[\alpha \log \pi_\theta(\tilde a_\theta(s, \epsilon) \mid s) - Q(s, \tilde a_\theta(s, \epsilon))\big]\), and its gradient flows through \(\tilde a\) into \(Q\) exactly as in DDPG, plus through \(\log \pi\). The variance is much lower than the score-function estimator's, and since the actions are re-sampled at replayed states, no importance weights are needed.

Squashed Gaussian policies and the tanh log-prob correction

Actions must lie in a box, so SAC squashes an unbounded Gaussian sample through \(\tanh\). That changes the density, and the entropy term needs the correct density.

Derivation · change of variables through tanh

Let \(u \sim \mu(u \mid s)\), a diagonal Gaussian (Haarnoja's notation for its density), and \(a = \tanh(u)\) elementwise. The Jacobian \(da/du\) is diagonal with entries \(1 - \tanh^2(u_i)\), so by the change-of-variables formula \(\pi(a \mid s) = \mu(u \mid s)\, \big|\det(da/du)\big|^{-1}\), and

$$\log \pi(a \mid s) = \log \mu(u \mid s) - \sum_{i=1}^{d} \log\big(1 - \tanh^2(u_i)\big).$$

Computing \(\log(1 - \tanh^2 u)\) directly loses all precision once \(|u|\) is large, because \(\tanh u\) rounds to \(\pm 1\). Use the identity \(1 - \tanh^2 u = 4/(e^{u} + e^{-u})^2\), which gives the stable form

$$\log\big(1 - \tanh^2(u)\big) = 2\big(\log 2 - u - \operatorname{softplus}(-2u)\big).$$

If the environment's actions are \(a_{\text{env}} = c \odot a\) for a scale vector \(c\), subtract a further \(\sum_i \log c_i\). It is constant, but it shifts the entropy that the temperature is tuned against.

Automatic temperature tuning

A fixed \(\alpha\) is hard to choose: the right value depends on the reward scale, varies across tasks, and changes during training. Haarnoja et al. (2018b) instead constrain the entropy and let \(\alpha\) be the Lagrange multiplier:

$$\max_\pi \E\Big[\sum_t r(s_t, a_t)\Big] \quad\text{subject to}\quad \E_{(s_t, a_t) \sim \rho_\pi}\big[-\log \pi(a_t \mid s_t)\big] \ge \bar{\mathcal H} \;\;\text{for all } t.$$

The dual problem gives a simple loss for \(\alpha\), minimized by gradient descent alongside the other networks:

$$J(\alpha) = \E_{a_t \sim \pi_t}\big[-\alpha \log \pi_t(a_t \mid s_t) - \alpha \bar{\mathcal H}\big] = -\alpha\, \E\big[\log \pi_t(a_t \mid s_t) + \bar{\mathcal H}\big].$$

If the policy's entropy \(-\E[\log\pi]\) is below the target, the bracket is positive and the gradient raises \(\alpha\), pushing for more entropy; if above, \(\alpha\) falls. The standard target is \(\bar{\mathcal H} = -\dim\mathcal A\), a heuristic: a 1-D Gaussian has entropy \(-1\) nat at a standard deviation of about 0.09, so it asks for a fairly concentrated but still stochastic policy. Optimize \(\log\alpha\) rather than \(\alpha\) to keep it positive, and use detached log-probabilities in its loss.

import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
from torch.distributions import Normal

LOG_STD_MIN, LOG_STD_MAX = -5.0, 2.0

class SquashedGaussianActor(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, obs_dim, act_dim, act_high):
        super().__init__()
        self.trunk = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(obs_dim, 256), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.ReLU())
        self.mu, self.log_std = nn.Linear(256, act_dim), nn.Linear(256, act_dim)
        self.register_buffer("scale", torch.as_tensor(act_high, dtype=torch.float32))

    def forward(self, s):
        h = self.trunk(s)
        mu, log_std = self.mu(h), self.log_std(h).clamp(LOG_STD_MIN, LOG_STD_MAX)
        std = log_std.exp()
        u = mu + std * torch.randn_like(mu)                  # reparameterized: noise is outside the graph
        logp = Normal(mu, std).log_prob(u).sum(-1)
        logp = logp - (2 * (math.log(2) - u - F.softplus(-2 * u))).sum(-1)   # - sum log(1 - tanh(u)^2)
        logp = logp - torch.log(self.scale).sum()             # a_env = scale * tanh(u)
        return self.scale * torch.tanh(u), logp

def sac_update(batch, actor, q1, q2, q1_t, q2_t, opt_actor, opt_q, log_alpha, opt_alpha,
               target_entropy, gamma=0.99, tau=0.005):
    s, a, r, s2, term = batch
    alpha = log_alpha.exp().detach()
    with torch.no_grad():                                     # soft Bellman target
        a2, logp2 = actor(s2)                                 # next action from the *current* policy
        soft_v = torch.min(q1_t(s2, a2), q2_t(s2, a2)) - alpha * logp2
        y = r + gamma * (1 - term) * soft_v
    q_loss = F.mse_loss(q1(s, a), y) + F.mse_loss(q2(s, a), y)
    opt_q.zero_grad(); q_loss.backward(); opt_q.step()

    a_new, logp = actor(s)                                    # gradients flow through a_new
    actor_loss = (alpha * logp - torch.min(q1(s, a_new), q2(s, a_new))).mean()
    opt_actor.zero_grad(); actor_loss.backward(); opt_actor.step()

    alpha_loss = -(log_alpha.exp() * (logp.detach() + target_entropy)).mean()
    opt_alpha.zero_grad(); alpha_loss.backward(); opt_alpha.step()
    soft_update(q1_t, q1, tau)                                # soft_update from Day 93
    soft_update(q2_t, q2, tau)
    return q_loss.item(), actor_loss.item(), alpha.item()

The SAC update, as a fragment of an off-policy loop like Day 93's. The critics' target uses a freshly sampled next action and its log-probability; the actor's loss uses the same reparameterized sample for both terms. Compared with TD3 there is no target actor, no delayed update and no target noise.

Why SAC is the default continuous-control baseline

  • Sample efficiency. Off-policy learning from replay, with one gradient step per environment step, typically reaches a given return on MuJoCo in far fewer environment steps than PPO.
  • Stability. Haarnoja et al. (2018b) report markedly smaller variation across seeds than DDPG, and performance competitive with or better than TD3 on the standard tasks.
  • Few knobs. With automatic temperature tuning, one set of hyperparameters works across most MuJoCo tasks; the reward-scale sensitivity of fixed-\(\alpha\) SAC largely disappears.
  • Exploration by construction, and a stochastic policy that degrades gracefully. The 2018b paper also trained real robots (a quadruped's walking gait and a dexterous hand manipulation task) directly in the real world.

It is not universal. Each environment step costs a full gradient step on three networks, so in massively parallel simulators where samples are nearly free (Brax, Isaac Gym), PPO usually wins on wall-clock (Project 7). Discrete actions need a modified version (Christodoulou 2019). And MBPO (Day 98) and offline methods such as CQL (Day 111) build on SAC rather than replace it.

What breaks
  • Missing or naive tanh correction. Without it the entropy estimate is wrong near the bounds, and the temperature tunes against the wrong number. Computing \(\log(1 - \tanh^2)\) directly produces \(-\infty\) once \(|u|\) is large.
  • Unbounded log σ. Without clamping, \(\log\sigma\) can run off to large negative values and the log-probabilities blow up. Clamp it (for example to \([-5, 2]\) or \([-20, 2]\)).
  • Temperature loss with live log-probs. Without detach() the α loss also trains the actor, in the wrong direction.
  • α collapse or explosion. A target entropy that is unreachable (too high for the action dimension) drives \(\alpha\) upwards forever; log \(\alpha\) and the entropy every few thousand steps.
  • Evaluating the stochastic policy. Report the deterministic \(\tanh(\mu)\) policy at evaluation, and say so.

A fair comparison protocol

  • Same evaluation for everyone. Every 10k environment steps (5k on Pendulum), run 10 episodes with the deterministic policy (the Gaussian mean for PPO, \(\mu(s)\) for TD3, \(\tanh\mu(s)\) for SAC) on a separate evaluation environment with frozen normalization statistics. Report undiscounted episode return.
  • Same budget axis. Environment steps, counting every step taken by every parallel environment.
  • Defaults first. Use each algorithm's standard hyperparameters (Days 92, 94, 95) on every task. If you tune, give every algorithm the same tuning budget and report it (Day 7).
  • Seeds. Five per (algorithm, environment), seeds 0–4, fixed in advance. Never drop a seed.
  • Final performance. The mean of the last five evaluations of each run; aggregate across seeds with a 95% bootstrap interval, and across tasks with the interquartile mean and stratified bootstrap intervals (rliable, Agarwal et al. 2021).

Sample efficiency versus wall-clock

The two axes answer different questions. Sample efficiency (return per environment step) matters when samples are expensive: real robots, slow simulators, anything with a human in the loop. Wall-clock efficiency (return per second of compute) matters when samples are cheap. SAC and TD3 take a gradient step for every environment step on a single environment; PPO collects thousands of steps across parallel environments per update and reuses each batch for a few epochs. On CPU MuJoCo, expect the off-policy methods to win clearly on the step axis and PPO to close much of the gap, or win, on the time axis as you add parallel environments. On GPU-batched simulators (Brax, MJX, Isaac) with thousands of environments, PPO routinely trains locomotion in minutes, which is why it dominates there.
