Why Prediction Is Not Enough

Day 92 · 85 min
The worked example that should unsettle you

In a hospital dataset, asthma is associated with lower mortality among pneumonia patients (Caruana et al. found a model had learned exactly this rule). The association is real and the predictor is well calibrated. But the mechanism is that asthmatic patients were routed directly to intensive care. The best predictor says "asthma → lower risk"; the corresponding decision rule — send asthmatics home — kills people. The best predictor is the worst decision rule, and no amount of held-out accuracy detects this.

The ladder of causation. (1) Association p(y∣x) — what is seen. (2) Intervention p(y∣do(x)) — what happens if we act. (3) Counterfactual p(Yx∣X=x′,Y=y′) — what would have happened to this unit. Each rung requires strictly more than the one below, and no amount of observational data alone climbs it: infinitely many SCMs induce the same observational distribution but different interventional ones.

Structural causal model

M=(U,V,F,P(U)): exogenous U, endogenous V, and assignments Vi:=fi(pai,Ui). The induced graph draws an arrow into Vi from each argument. Intervention do(X=x) replaces fX by the constant x, deleting all arrows into X — the mutilated graph. Everything else is unchanged: this is the modularity/autonomy assumption, and it is the substantive content of the formalism.

Simpson's paradox, live — confounding strength versus the adjusted effect

Simpson's paradox is not a statistical curiosity. The reversal is a fact about the numbers; the question "should I adjust for Z?" has no statistical answer. If Z is a confounder (Z→X, Z→Y) you must adjust; if Z is a mediator (X→Z→Y) adjusting removes part of the effect you want; if Z is a collider, adjusting creates bias. Same table, three answers, determined entirely by assumptions that the data cannot supply.

Identification

Day 93 · 85 min
The identification problem

A causal quantity Q is identifiable from (G,P(V)) if any two SCMs consistent with G that agree on P(V) also agree on Q. Identification is a question about the model, entirely prior to estimation; a non-identified quantity cannot be estimated by any amount of data.

Back-door criterion

S satisfies the back-door criterion relative to (X,Y) if no node in S is a descendant of X, and S blocks every path from X to Y with an arrow into X. Then p(y∣do(x))=∑sp(y∣x,s)p(s).

proofIn the mutilated graph GX, S d-separates X from Y given the remaining back-door paths blocked, and pdo(x)(s)=p(s) since S has no ancestors through X. Expand pdo(x)(y)=∑spdo(x)(y∣s)pdo(x)(s) and replace each factor.

Choosing among valid adjustment sets. Many S satisfy the criterion; they identify the same quantity but with different variance. Adding a strong predictor of Y that is not related to X reduces variance (like a covariate in a randomized trial); adding a strong predictor of X that is unrelated to Y — an instrument-like variable — inflates variance and amplifies any residual bias (Z-bias / bias amplification). The optimal adjustment set is characterized graphically (Henckel, Perković & Maathuis): adjust for the parents of Y on causal paths, not for everything available.

Front-door criterion

If M intercepts all directed paths X→Y, no back-door path from X to M exists, and all back-door paths from M to Y are blocked by X, then even with unmeasured confounding of X and Y: p(y∣do(x))=∑mp(m∣x)∑x′p(y∣m,x′)p(x′).

The front-door formula chains two back-door adjustments: X→M (unconfounded by assumption) and M→Y (confounding blocked by X). It is the standard demonstration that identification is possible without ignorability — and it is rare in practice, because a fully mediating, unconfounded M is a strong claim.

do-calculus and the ID algorithm

Three rules, applied to graphs with edges deleted:
R1 (insert/delete observation): p(y∣do(x),z,w)=p(y∣do(x),w) if Y⊥dZ∣X,W in GX.
R2 (action ↔ observation): p(y∣do(x),do(z),w)=p(y∣do(x),z,w) if Y⊥dZ∣X,W in GXZ.
R3 (insert/delete action): p(y∣do(x),do(z),w)=p(y∣do(x),w) if Y⊥dZ∣X,W in GXZ(W).
Completeness (Shpitser–Pearl; Huang–Valtorta): the three rules are complete — if a quantity is identifiable, do-calculus derives it, and the ID algorithm finds the expression or returns a hedge, a graphical certificate of non-identifiability.

Why “adjust for everything” is wrong
  • Colliders. Conditioning on a common effect of X and Y opens a path and creates spurious association where none existed.
  • M-bias. X←U1→Z←U2→Y: Z is a pre-treatment, non-descendant variable, and adjusting for it induces bias. "Pre-treatment" is not a sufficient licence.
  • Mediators. Adjusting for a variable on the causal path removes the effect you are trying to measure.
  • Selection bias is collider bias induced by the sampling mechanism; it cannot be fixed by covariate adjustment and requires its own graphical treatment (selection nodes).

With latent confounders the right object is an ADMG with bidirected edges; identifiability is then decided by the ID algorithm's hedge criterion rather than by the back-door criterion alone.

The Potential Outcomes Framework

Day 93 · 85 min
Potential outcomes and the fundamental problem

Each unit has Yi(1),Yi(0); we observe Yi=WiYi(1)+(1−Wi)Yi(0). The individual effect Yi(1)−Yi(0) is never observed — the fundamental problem of causal inference. Causal inference is therefore a missing-data problem, and all progress comes from assumptions that let group averages substitute for individual counterfactuals.

SUTVA bundles two assumptions that are worth separating. (1) No interference: unit i's outcome does not depend on others' treatments — violated in vaccination, network experiments, marketplace pricing, and any two-sided platform. (2) No hidden versions of treatment: "treatment" is well-defined and identical across units — violated whenever "surgery" or "attending college" hides substantial heterogeneity in what was actually done.

Assumptions, stated precisely

Randomization: W⊥(Y(1),Y(0)), giving 𝔼[Y∣W=1]−𝔼[Y∣W=0]=ATE immediately.
Unconfoundedness / ignorability: W⊥(Y(1),Y(0))∣X.
Positivity / overlap: 0<e(x)=P(W=1∣X=x)<1 for all x in the support.
The first is a design; the second and third are assumptions. Positivity is checkable and unconfoundedness is not — which is why overlap diagnostics and sensitivity analysis carry so much weight.

The two languages. An SCM implies potential outcomes (Yi(w) is the value of Y in the SCM with do(W=w) and the unit's Ui fixed) and hence the two frameworks are formally interchangeable. In practice: DAGs are better for reasoning about which variables to adjust for and for detecting collider and selection problems; potential outcomes are better for defining estimands precisely, for finite-population inference, and for handling non-compliance and censoring. Use DAGs to choose the adjustment set and potential outcomes to state and estimate the target.

Fisher's sharp null H0:Yi(1)=Yi(0)∀i makes all potential outcomes known under H0, so the randomization distribution of any test statistic is computable exactly — a finite-sample exact test with no modelling assumption (Lecture 22). Neyman's weak null H0:Y(1)=Y(0) targets the average and yields the repeated-sampling variance S12n1+S02n0, which is conservative because S01 (the variance of the individual effects) is unidentified.

Estimation Under Unconfoundedness

Day 94 · 85 min
Theorem — the propensity score (Rosenbaum & Rubin)

If W⊥(Y(1),Y(0))∣X, then W⊥(Y(1),Y(0))∣e(X), where e(X)=P(W=1∣X).

proofShow P(W=1∣Y(1),Y(0),e(X))=e(X): condition on X inside, use unconfoundedness to get P(W=1∣X)=e(X), then take the expectation over X given e(X) — which is e(X), being measurable.

The score is a balancing score of dimension one: matching or stratifying on it suffices however large X is. This is the theorem that made observational causal inference practical, and its cost is that e must be estimated.

Theorem — double robustness of AIPW

τˆAIPW is consistent for the ATE if either eˆ→e or mˆw→mw, not necessarily both.

proofWrite the estimand's bias as 𝔼[(ee˜−1)(m1−m˜1)]−𝔼[(1−e1−e˜−1)(m0−m˜0)] for limits e˜,m˜. Each term is a product of two errors, so it vanishes if either factor is zero. That product structure is Neyman orthogonality, and it is why AIPW tolerates slowly converging nuisance estimates (Lecture 142).

TMLE reaches the same influence function by a different route: fit mˆ, then update it along a parametric submodel whose score is the efficient influence function, so the resulting plug-in estimator solves the efficient estimating equation and stays within the model's bounds (never predicting probabilities outside [0,1]).

Diagnostics and sensitivity

Overlap: plot eˆ by treatment arm; check standardized mean differences before/after weighting (target <0.1); report the effective sample size (∑w)2/∑w2 — a study with n=10,000 and neff=180 should be reported as such.

Sensitivity analysis is mandatory because unconfoundedness is untestable. Rosenbaum bounds: how large an odds-ratio Γ of differential assignment among matched pairs would overturn the conclusion? E-value (VanderWeele–Ding): the minimum strength of association (on the risk-ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder must have with both treatment and outcome to explain away the effect; computable in closed form from the point estimate and reportable in one number. A causal claim from observational data without a sensitivity analysis is incomplete.

Instruments and Quasi-Experiments

Day 94 · 85 min
Instrumental variables

Z is an instrument for X→Y if: relevance (Z affects X), exclusion (Z affects Y only through X), independence (Z is as-good-as-randomly assigned w.r.t. unobserved confounders). Wald estimator (binary Z): βˆIV=𝔼[Y∣Z=1]−𝔼[Y∣Z=0]𝔼[X∣Z=1]−𝔼[X∣Z=0]. 2SLS generalizes it: regress X on Z and covariates, then Y on the fitted Xˆ — but the second-stage standard errors must account for the first stage.

Theorem — LATE (Imbens & Angrist)

Add monotonicity (no defiers: Xi(1)≥Xi(0) for all i). Then the Wald estimand equals 𝔼[Y(1)−Y(0)∣compliers], the effect among the subpopulation whose treatment status is moved by the instrument.

Two uncomfortable facts usually left implicit. The complier subpopulation is not identified as a set — you can estimate its size and covariate distribution but not membership. And different instruments identify different LATEs, so two valid IV studies of the same treatment can honestly report different numbers. When you report an IV estimate, say which compliers.

Weak instruments. With a weak first stage, 2SLS is biased toward OLS with bias ≈σuvσv2⋅1F and its distribution is non-normal. The "F>10" rule of thumb is inadequate: Lee, McCrary, Moreira & Porter show that valid 5% inference with the usual t-test requires roughly F>105, and recommend either the tF adjustment or identification-robust methods (Anderson–Rubin, conditional likelihood ratio) which are valid regardless of instrument strength.

Heterogeneous Effects and Causal Machine Learning

Day 95 · 85 min

Why you cannot just fit two models. The T-learner fits mˆ1,mˆ0 separately and subtracts. If τ(x) is simple but mw(x) is complex, the difference inherits both models' errors and their regularization biases — a smooth constant effect can be swamped. The CATE is a different estimand with a different difficulty, and the estimator should target it directly.

Theorem — double/debiased ML (Chernozhukov et al. 2018)

Let ψ(W;θ,η) be a Neyman-orthogonal score: ∂η𝔼[ψ(W;θ0,η0)][η−η0]=0. Estimate the nuisances ηˆ on data folds disjoint from those used for θ (cross-fitting). If ‖ηˆ−η0‖=op(n−1/4), then √n(θˆ−θ0)⇒N(0,σ2),σ2=𝔼[ψ2]/(𝔼[∂θψ])2, achieving the semiparametric efficiency bound.

Causal forests (Wager & Athey)

Two modifications to a random forest make it an asymptotically normal CATE estimator: honesty (use one subsample to choose splits and a disjoint one to estimate leaf effects, so the leaf estimate is unbiased conditional on the partition) and subsampling instead of bootstrap (so the forest is a U-statistic). Then τˆ(x)−τ(x)√Vˆ(x)⇒N(0,1) with a computable infinitesimal-jackknife variance. Splits are chosen to maximize heterogeneity in the treatment effect, not in the outcome; the generalized random forest formulation extends this to any local moment condition.

Policy learning. Given τˆ, learn a policy π:𝒳→{0,1} maximizing 𝔼[Y(π(X))] over a constrained class. Athey–Wager: the regret of the empirical maximizer of a doubly-robust score decays at √VC(Π)/n — policy regret is √n-rate even though CATE estimation is slower, because a policy only needs the sign of τ, not its value. That is the same margin phenomenon as Lecture 17.

Evaluation. Qini and uplift curves rank units by predicted effect and plot cumulative incremental outcome; the area is an estimate of targeting value. They are noisy — the underlying quantity is a difference of differences — so report confidence bands, and prefer the doubly-robust version of the curve.

Causal Discovery

Day 95 · 85 min

What the two assumptions buy. The causal Markov condition lets you read independences off the graph; faithfulness lets you read the graph off the independences. Without faithfulness, an observed independence is compatible with a graph whose paths happen to cancel — and near-cancellation, which is not measure-zero in any practical sense, destroys finite-sample reliability (Uhler et al.'s "strong faithfulness" is violated on a large fraction of parameter space).

The ceiling

From observational data alone with only Markov + faithfulness, you can identify the graph only up to its Markov equivalence class (Lecture 97). To do better you need one of: interventional data, a restricted functional form (LiNGAM/ANM), time order, or background knowledge. Every claim to have "discovered causal structure from observational data" rests on one of these, and it should be named.

Realistic expectations

On real data, CI tests with continuous, non-Gaussian, dependent variables are unreliable (Lecture 80), the number of tests grows combinatorially so multiplicity is severe, and sample sizes are rarely adequate for conditioning sets beyond size 2–3. Discovery methods are best used to generate hypotheses and to rule out structures, with the output presented as an equivalence class rather than a single graph. Reporting a single DAG from PC output without the circle marks is a misrepresentation of what was learned.

Causality Meets Learning

Day 96 · 85 min
Shift, understood causally

In the causal direction (X→Y), p(y∣x) is a mechanism and p(x) is a cause distribution; they are independent modules, so covariate shift is the natural perturbation and semi-supervised learning cannot help (extra x's carry no information about p(y∣x)). In the anticausal direction (Y→X, e.g. label causes image), p(x∣y) and p(y) are the modules; label shift is natural, and semi-supervised learning can help. Schölkopf et al.'s independent-causal-mechanisms principle predicts precisely when SSL works, and the empirical record matches.

Invariance-based learning. ICP (Peters, Bühlmann & Meinshausen): search subsets S such that the conditional Y∣XS is identical across environments; under assumptions the intersection of accepted sets is contained in the causal parents, giving a conservative and testable procedure with type-I error control. IRM (Arjovsky et al.) softens this into a penalty enforcing that the same classifier is optimal in every environment. Known failure cases are substantial: Rosenfeld, Ravikumar & Risteski show IRM can fail to recover the invariant predictor in the nonlinear regime and can be no better than ERM outside a narrow setting; Gulrajani & Lopez-Paz's DomainBed finds that with proper model selection, plain ERM is competitive with essentially all domain-generalization methods. The idea is right; the current objectives do not reliably deliver it.

Transportability. Selection diagrams add S-nodes marking where mechanisms differ across populations; do-calculus then decides whether a target-population effect is computable from source data plus limited target data, and returns a transport formula. This generalizes to data fusion across many studies with different designs, selection, and measurement — the most complete formal treatment of external validity available.

Counterfactual fairness. A predictor is counterfactually fair if YˆA←a(u)=YˆA←a′(u) for all u. It requires a full SCM including the unobserved U, so it is not identifiable without strong assumptions, and it can conflict with observational criteria such as calibration within groups (Lecture 151). Path-specific effects let you permit some pathways (e.g. qualification) while blocking others (e.g. direct discrimination), which is closer to how the law reasons — and correspondingly requires specifying which paths are legitimate, a normative rather than statistical judgment.

Causal representation learning aims to recover latent causal variables from raw observations. The identifiability obstacle is exactly Lecture 123's: without auxiliary structure, nonlinear latent variables are unidentifiable. Progress relies on interventions, temporal structure, multiple environments, or sparse mechanism shifts. Honest state of the evidence: the argument that causal structure yields robust generalization is theoretically well-motivated and empirically unproven at scale; the strongest current evidence is negative (DomainBed), and the positive results are in controlled settings with known generative structure.

Recitation 12: Causal Inference Problem Set

Day 96 · 60 min
  1. do-calculus by hand. On a supplied DAG with one latent confounder, derive p(y∣do(x)) using the three rules, naming the rule and the graph mutilation at each step. Then run the ID algorithm mentally and confirm it finds the same expression.
  2. A biasing adjustment set. Construct an M-bias graph, simulate from it, and show that adjusting for the pre-treatment collider Z produces a biased estimate while the unadjusted difference is unbiased. Report the magnitude as a function of the two latent path strengths.
  3. Double robustness. Prove AIPW's product-of-errors bias expression; then simulate with (a) correct outcome model, wrong propensity, (b) wrong outcome, correct propensity, (c) both wrong, and tabulate bias and coverage.
  4. Cross-fitted DML. Implement partially linear DML with random forests as nuisance learners; verify 95% coverage over 500 replications, then rerun without cross-fitting and quantify the resulting under-coverage.
  5. Sensitivity. Compute the E-value for a published observational estimate of your choice and state, in words, what unmeasured confounder would be needed to explain it away.
Two papers

Foundational: Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983). Modern: Chernozhukov et al. (2018), Double/debiased machine learning — reconstruct why both orthogonality and cross-fitting are needed; the paper's counterexamples make the point better than the theorems.
