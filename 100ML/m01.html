Statistical Decision Theory & Estimation

Reading: TPE Ch. 1–5 · vdV Ch. 2–8 · Berger Ch. 1–5. This module answers “what is a good estimator?” before any algorithm is proposed. Everything in Modules 3, 4, 9 and 12 is a special case of what is set up here.

The Decision-Theoretic Framework

Day 11 · 85 min

Given a loss and a model, is there a best procedure — and in what sense of “best”?

Setup

State θ∈Θ, data X∼Pθ, action a∈𝒜, loss L(θ,a)≥0, decision rule δ:𝒳→𝒜. Risk R(θ,δ)=𝔼θL(θ,δ(X)) — a function of θ, not a number. That is the whole difficulty: risk functions are only partially ordered.

Three scalarizations turn the partial order into a total one: Bayes risk r(π,δ)=∫R(θ,δ)π(dθ); maximum risk supθR(θ,δ); and restricted classes (unbiased, equivariant) inside which comparison is easier.

Theorem — the Bayes rule

δπ(x)∈argmina∫L(θ,a)π(dθ∣x) minimizes r(π,⋅), provided the posterior expected loss is finite. Minimizing the posterior expected loss pointwise in x minimizes the average risk — Fubini, plus the fact that δ may be chosen freely at each x.

This is the single most useful move in the module: an integrated global criterion becomes a pointwise optimization. It is why the Bayes classifier is defined pointwise (Lecture 17), why the posterior mean/median/quantile fall out of the loss, and why Bayesian decision theory is computationally a two-step problem — get the posterior, then optimize.

Theorems — admissibility, minimax, complete class

Admissibility. δ is inadmissible if some δ′ has R(θ,δ′)≤R(θ,δ) for all θ with strict inequality somewhere.
A unique Bayes rule is admissible. If it were dominated, the dominating rule would have Bayes risk no larger, contradicting uniqueness.
Minimax = Bayes for the least favourable prior. If δπ has constant risk, it is minimax and π is least favourable. More generally, under compactness/continuity, infδsupθR=supπinfδr(π,δ) — a minimax theorem, i.e. Sion's theorem applied to the bilinear r.
Complete class. Under regularity (Θ compact, L continuous), the admissible rules are exactly the Bayes rules (and limits of them). Frequentist optimality and Bayesian coherence describe the same set of procedures.

Randomization never helps against a fixed θ under convex loss (Jensen: average the actions instead). It helps only in games — minimax, and hypothesis testing at exact level α with discrete data.

Where it breaks

Minimaxity is a pessimist's criterion: the minimax rule can be terrible at every θ anyone cares about, because it is optimized against a least favourable prior that may be absurd (e.g. concentrated on the boundary of the parameter space). And admissibility is a very weak endorsement — the rule “always output θ0” is admissible under squared loss, since nothing beats it at θ0.

Bayes-Optimal Prediction and Surrogate Losses

Bayes error. For binary Y with η(x)=P(Y=1∣x), R⋆=𝔼min(η,1−η), and the excess risk of any h is R(h)−R⋆=𝔼[|2η(X)−1|𝟙{h(X)≠h⋆(X)}]. Read that identity carefully: mistakes are only costly where η is far from 1/2. It is the origin of the Tsybakov margin condition (Lecture 38) and explains why classifiers can have tiny excess risk while getting many near-boundary points wrong.

Why we never minimize 0–1 loss directly

Empirical 0–1 minimization over halfspaces is NP-hard, and the loss is non-convex and has zero gradient a.e. So minimize a convex surrogate ϕ and hope excess ϕ-risk controls excess 0–1 risk. That hope is a theorem, with a condition — classification calibration — proved in Lecture 37.

Where it breaks

The plug-in principle — estimate η, then threshold at 12 — inherits the estimation error of η everywhere, including regions where η is far from 12 and accuracy is irrelevant. Plug-in classifiers can therefore be rate-suboptimal for classification even when ηˆ is rate-optimal for regression, and conversely can achieve fast rates under a margin condition. Never assume the two problems have the same difficulty.

Point Estimation: Sufficiency and Optimality

Day 13 · 85 min

What is the least you can retain from the data without losing anything, and does “unbiased” deserve its reputation?

Theorem — factorization (Fisher–Neyman)

T is sufficient for θ iff pθ(x)=gθ(T(x))h(x). Equivalently, the conditional law of X given T does not depend on θ.

Theorem — Rao–Blackwell

Let T be sufficient, δ an estimator with finite risk under a convex loss, and δ∗(t)=𝔼[δ(X)∣T=t] (well defined and θ-free by sufficiency). Then R(θ,δ∗)≤R(θ,δ) for all θ, strictly unless δ is already a function of T.

proofConditional Jensen: L(θ,𝔼[δ∣T])≤𝔼[L(θ,δ)∣T]; take expectations.

Completeness (𝔼θg(T)=0∀θ⇒g=0 a.s.) upgrades this: by Lehmann–Scheffé, if T is complete sufficient and δ∗ is an unbiased function of T, then δ∗ is the unique UMVU estimator. In a full-rank exponential family, the natural sufficient statistic is complete — which is where almost all UMVU results come from.

Basu's theorem. A complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic. Slick consequence: in N(μ,σ2) with σ known, X (complete sufficient) is independent of S2 (ancillary for μ) — no computation required.

Where it breaks — the overrating of unbiasedness

Unbiasedness is a constraint, not a virtue. (i) Unbiased estimators can be absurd: for X∼Poisson(λ), the unique unbiased estimator of e−2λ is (−1)X — it is negative half the time and estimates a quantity in (0,1). (ii) They may not exist. (iii) They are typically inadmissible in dimension ≥3 (Lecture 25). (iv) The whole of regularization deliberately accepts bias for variance. In this series, unbiasedness matters in exactly two places: as a tool (Rao–Blackwell) and where the estimand's scale is fixed by design (AIPW, Lecture 140).

Information Bounds

Day 14 · 85 min

How much can any estimator possibly know from n samples?

Fisher information

Score sθ(x)=∇θlogpθ(x). Under regularity (support free of θ, differentiation under the integral valid), 𝔼θ[sθ]=0 and I(θ)=𝔼θ[sθsθ⊤]=−𝔼θ[∇2logpθ]. Additivity: In(θ)=nI1(θ) for i.i.d. data. Reparameterization: Iψ=J⊤IθJ with J=∂θ/∂ψ — information is a metric tensor, not a scalar, which is the entire content of Lecture 70's natural gradient.

Theorem — Cramér–Rao

For any estimator with 𝔼θθˆ=θ+b(θ) and regularity, Varθ(θˆ)⪰(1+b′)I(θ)−1(1+b′)⊤. Unbiased scalar case: Var≥1/(nI1(θ)). Equality holds iff θˆ−θ is proportional to the score — which happens exactly in full exponential families for the natural sufficient statistic.

proofCauchy–Schwarz on Cov(θˆ,sθ), using Cov(θˆ,sθ)=1+b′ (differentiate the unbiasedness identity under the integral).

Why CRLB is not enough for minimax lower bounds. It is local, requires regularity, and only constrains unbiased (or fixed-bias) estimators — so it says nothing about shrinkage estimators, which beat it by being biased. The van Trees inequality (Bayesian CRLB) fixes this: for any estimator whatsoever, ∫𝔼θ(θˆ−θ)2π(θ)dθ≥1∫I(θ)π(θ)dθ+ℐ(π), with ℐ(π) the Fisher information of the prior. Because it holds for all estimators, it converts into a minimax bound by choosing π well — the smooth alternative to Fano in Lecture 23.

Where it breaks

Regularity fails when the support depends on θ (uniform on [0,θ]: the MLE converges at rate n−1, beating any “1/√n information bound”), at boundary parameters, and for non-differentiable models. Superefficiency (Hodges' estimator) shows that pointwise asymptotic variance below I−1 is possible on a Lebesgue-null set — which is why the modern statement is local asymptotic minimax (Hájek–Le Cam), not pointwise efficiency.

Maximum Likelihood: The Full Asymptotic Theory

Day 15 · 85 min

What exactly does the MLE converge to when the model is wrong?

Treat MLE as an M-estimator: θˆn=argmaxθℙnmθ with mθ=logpθ. Then two theorems do all the work.

Theorem — consistency

If (i) θ↦ℙmθ has a well-separated maximum at θ0, and (ii) supθ|ℙnmθ−ℙmθ|→0 in probability (a uniform LLN — Lecture 13), then θˆn→θ0 in probability.

Theorem — asymptotic normality

If additionally mθ is twice differentiable near θ0 with nonsingular V=ℙ∇2mθ0, and a local Lipschitz/Donsker envelope condition holds, then √n(θˆn−θ0)⇒N(0,V−1ℙ[∇m∇m⊤]V−1). Correctly specified: V=−I and ℙ[∇m∇m⊤]=I (the information equality), so the sandwich collapses to I−1 — asymptotic efficiency.
Misspecified: θ0 is the KL projection argminθKL(P‖Pθ), the information equality fails, and the correct covariance is the Huber–White sandwich V−1WV−1.

Z-estimation. Replace “maximize” by “solve ℙnψθ=0”. The same expansion gives √n(θˆ−θ0)⇒N(0,Ψ˙−1ℙ[ψψ⊤]Ψ˙−⊤) with Ψ˙=ℙ∇ψ. This covers GEE, IPW, GMM and — crucially — the influence-function formalism used throughout Modules 12 and 13.

Where MLE fails outright
  • Unbounded likelihood: Gaussian mixtures — send one component's variance to 0 at a data point and the likelihood diverges. The global maximizer is degenerate; the "MLE" used in practice is a particular local maximum (Lecture 120).
  • Boundary: variance components at 0. The limit is a mixture of χ2s, not χ21; a naive LRT is conservative.
  • Non-regular support: U[0,θ] gives n-consistency and a non-normal (exponential) limit.
  • Growing dimension: Neyman–Scott — with one nuisance parameter per observation, the MLE is inconsistent. Conditional or profile likelihood is required.

The EM Algorithm as a Theory Object

Day 16 · 85 min

Why does EM always go uphill, and why is it sometimes agonizingly slow?

With latents Z, logpθ(x)=ℒ(q,θ)+KL(q‖pθ(⋅∣x)) where ℒ(q,θ)=𝔼q[logpθ(x,Z)]+H(q) is the ELBO. EM is coordinate ascent on ℒ: the E-step maximizes over q (giving q=pθ(⋅∣x), so KL =0 and the bound is tight), the M-step over θ.

Theorem — monotonicity

logpθt+1(x)≥logpθt(x), with equality only at a stationary point of the observed-data likelihood.

prooflogpθt+1≥ℒ(qt,θt+1)≥ℒ(qt,θt)=logpθt: the first inequality because the ELBO is a lower bound for any q, the second by the M-step, the equality because the E-step made it tight.

Rate. Near a fixed point, θt+1−θ⋆≈M(θt−θ⋆) with M=ImissIcomp−1, the missing information ratio. Convergence is linear with rate ρ(M): fast when little information is missing (well-separated clusters), arbitrarily slow when the latent structure is nearly unidentifiable. This is the precise statement of “EM crawls when components overlap”.

Variants. Generalized EM (increase, don't maximize, ℒ); ECM (conditional maximization steps); stochastic/online EM (Cappé–Moulines) with Robbins–Monro step sizes; variational EM when the E-step posterior is intractable — the bridge to Lecture 103.

Where it breaks

Monotone ascent of the likelihood does not imply convergence of the iterates: EM can converge to a saddle if initialized on a symmetric configuration, and can stall on ridges. Modern analysis (Balakrishnan–Wainwright–Yu, Lecture 120) gives basin-of-attraction results: within an O(1) ball of the truth and under separation, EM converges geometrically to a statistically optimal point — outside that ball, no guarantee.

Testing and Multiple Comparisons

Day 16 · 85 min

What is optimal about the likelihood ratio, and what changes when you test 20,000 hypotheses?

Theorem — Neyman–Pearson

For simple H0:P0 vs H1:P1, the most powerful level-α test rejects when p1(x)/p0(x)>k, with randomization on the boundary to attain exact size.

proofFor any other test ϕ of level ≤α, ∫(ϕ⋆−ϕ)(p1−kp0)≥0 pointwise by construction of ϕ⋆; expand and use ∫ϕp0≤α=∫ϕ⋆p0.

Monotone likelihood ratio in T(x) extends this to a UMP one-sided test for composite H0:θ≤θ0. Two-sided UMP tests generally do not exist, which is why unbiasedness or invariance is imposed to restore uniqueness.

The three asymptotic tests. Wald ((θˆ−θ0)⊤Iˆ(θˆ−θ0)), score/Lagrange multiplier (uses only the null fit), and likelihood ratio (2(ℓ(θˆ)−ℓ(θ0))). All are asymptotically χ2k under H0 (Wilks) and asymptotically equivalent, but differ at finite n: Wald is not invariant to reparameterization (a real defect — testing β=0 vs eβ=1 gives different answers), the score test needs no alternative fit, and the LRT is invariant and generally best behaved.

Theorem — BH controls FDR

Under independence of the p-values (and more generally under PRDS, positive regression dependence on a subset), BH has FDR≤m0mα≤α, where m0 is the number of true nulls. Under arbitrary dependence, using α/∑i=1m1/i (Benjamini–Yekutieli) restores control at a logm price.

Permutation and randomization tests give exact finite-sample validity under an exchangeability null, with no distributional assumption. They are the right default whenever the null is a sharp null (Fisher's, Lecture 139) and the test statistic is computable — and they reappear as the calibration device for MMD (Lecture 80) and conditional randomization tests (Lecture 57).

Where it breaks

FDR is an expectation over a ratio, so it says nothing about any particular realized experiment; the realized false discovery proportion can far exceed α. And BH's guarantee is about the p-values it is handed — if those were computed after looking at the data (selection), no multiplicity correction repairs it. That failure is the subject of Lecture 56.

Minimax Theory and Lower Bounds

Day 17 · 85 min

How do you prove that no estimator can do better?

The universal reduction. If parameters θ1,…,θM are 2ρ-separated in the loss metric, then an estimator with error <ρ identifies which one generated the data. So estimation is at least as hard as an M-ary testing problem, and information-theoretic limits on testing become lower bounds on estimation.

Three tools

Le Cam (two points). infθˆsupθ𝔼d(θˆ,θ)≳ρ(1−‖Pθ1−Pθ2‖TV). Choose θ1,θ2 as far apart as possible subject to their laws being statistically indistinguishable (KL≲1, via Pinsker). Gives the right rate for one-dimensional problems.
Assouad (hypercube). Embed {0,1}d into the parameter space so the loss decomposes coordinatewise; the bound multiplies the per-coordinate two-point bound by d. This is how the d in σ√d/n appears.
Fano (many points). infθˆmaxjℙ(θˆ≠θj)≥1−I+log2logM. Needs a large packing with small mutual information; the workhorse for nonparametric rates.

Three worked rates

  • Gaussian mean, ℝd. Packing of a sphere of radius ρ with M=ecd points at separation ρ; KL≍nρ2/σ2. Fano forces ρ2≍σ2d/n: minimax rate σ√d/n, attained by the sample mean.
  • Hölder regression. Take M≍ecm bump-perturbations of a base function on m=h−d cells, each of height hβ. Balancing separation against KL gives h≍n−1/(2β+d) and rate n−2β/(2β+d) (Lecture 59).
  • s-sparse vectors in ℝp. Packing of s-sparse supports has logM≍slog(p/s); the rate is σ√slog(p/s)/n — the lasso attains it (Lecture 46), so the logp is not an artifact of the method.
How to read a lower bound

Check three things. (1) Which class? A bound over all s-sparse vectors says nothing about a specific well-conditioned instance. (2) Which loss and which norm? Prediction, estimation and support-recovery rates differ (Lecture 46). (3) Is it minimax over a class, or an instance-wise bound? Minimax bounds are compatible with an adaptive method being much better on most of the class — that is not a contradiction, it is Lecture 59.

Robust Statistics

Day 17 · 75 min

What happens to your estimator when 1% of the data is arbitrary?

Contamination model

Huber's ε-contamination: P=(1−ε)Fθ+εQ with Q arbitrary. Adversarial contamination allows the εn bad points to be chosen after seeing the good ones — strictly harder and the standard model in modern work.

Influence function IF(x;T,F)=limt↓0T((1−t)F+tδx)−T(F)t — the derivative of the functional in the direction of a point mass. Gross-error sensitivity is supx|IF|; finite sensitivity is the definition of local robustness. For the mean, IF=x−μ: unbounded. For the median, IF=sign(x−m)/(2f(m)): bounded. The influence function is also the asymptotic-variance generator, AVar=𝔼[IF2], which is why it reappears as the central object of semiparametric efficiency in Lecture 142.

Breakdown point — the largest contamination fraction the estimator tolerates before it can be driven arbitrarily far. Mean: 0. Median: 1/2. Sample covariance: 0. MCD with the right subset size: 1/2. Trimmed mean at level α: α.

Huber's loss ρδ(u)=u2/2 for |u|≤δ, δ(|u|−δ/2) otherwise. It is the solution to a well-posed minimax problem: minimize the maximum asymptotic variance over an ε-contamination neighbourhood of the Gaussian. The tuning constant δ maps to ε — Huber's loss is not a heuristic compromise, it is an optimal one under a stated criterion.

Robust scale and covariance. MAD =med|xi−med(x)|, scaled by 1.4826 for Gaussian consistency. Minimum covariance determinant and Tyler's M-estimator for shape. In high dimensions all classical robust covariance estimators degrade; the modern line (Diakonikolas et al., Lai–Rao–Vempala) achieves O(ε√log(1/ε)) error for Gaussian mean estimation in polynomial time by filtering along directions of anomalous variance — a spectral idea, not an order-statistic one.

Where it breaks

Coordinatewise robustness is not robustness: applying a robust univariate estimator to each coordinate has breakdown 1/2 per coordinate but fails badly against contamination aligned with a direction, and the resulting error grows as ε√d rather than ε. In high dimension you must reason about directions, not coordinates.

Shrinkage and the Stein Phenomenon

Day 18 · 75 min

Three unrelated quantities, measured with independent noise. Why does estimating them jointly beat estimating them separately?

Theorem — Stein (1956), James–Stein (1961)

Let X∼N(θ,σ2Id). For d≥3 the MLE θˆ=X is inadmissible under squared error, and θˆJS=(1−(d−2)σ2‖X‖2)X has strictly smaller risk for every θ.

Lemma — SURE (Stein's unbiased risk estimate)

For θˆ=X+g(X) with g weakly differentiable, 𝔼‖θˆ−θ‖2=𝔼[dσ2+2σ2∇⋅g(X)+‖g(X)‖2].

proof of the theorem from SURETake g(x)=−cσ2x/‖x‖2. Then ∇⋅g=−cσ2(d−2)/‖x‖2 and ‖g‖2=c2σ4/‖x‖2, so the risk is dσ2+σ4𝔼[c2−2c(d−2)]/‖X‖2. This is negative for 0<c<2(d−2) and minimized at c=d−2, giving risk reduction σ4(d−2)2𝔼‖X‖−2>0 for every θ.

Risk of James–Stein vs the MLE, as a function of ‖θ‖

Why d≥3? The divergence term contributes −(d−2), which is positive only from d=3. Geometrically: ‖X‖2 overestimates ‖θ‖2 by dσ2 in expectation, and in high dimension that bias is a reliable, exploitable signal — the concentration of ‖X‖ that makes shrinkage work is the same phenomenon as Lecture 52's concentration of measure.

Positive-part JS (1−cσ2/‖X‖2)+X dominates plain JS (the shrinkage factor should never flip sign) and is itself inadmissible — no explicit admissible improvement is known in closed form.

Empirical Bayes reading. Put θi∼N(0,τ2); the Bayes rule shrinks by τ2/(τ2+σ2); estimating that factor from the marginal ‖X‖2∼(τ2+σ2)χ2d reproduces JS exactly. So JS is not a trick — it is a hierarchical model with the hyperparameter estimated from data (Lecture 112). This is the conceptual ancestor of ridge, of hierarchical models, of partial pooling, and of the entire regularization program.

Where it breaks

The risk improvement is aggregate. JS reduces total squared error while making individual coordinates worse — if one θi is far from the rest, its estimate is shrunk toward the group and its individual error increases. Reporting per-unit shrunken estimates in a policy context (school rankings, hospital quality) without saying this is a known abuse. Shrinkage is also toward an arbitrary point; shrinking toward X instead of 0 costs one degree of freedom and is nearly always what you want.

Recitation 1: Decision Theory Problem Set

Day 18 · 60 min
  1. Bayes rules under non-standard losses. Derive the Bayes action for the LINEX loss L(θ,a)=ec(a−θ)−c(a−θ)−1; show it is −1clog𝔼[e−cθ∣x], and explain why it reduces to the posterior mean as c→0.
  2. Admissibility. Show that under squared loss, δ(x)=c is admissible for every constant c; then show δ(x)=aX+b is inadmissible for a>1. Identify precisely where the argument uses the unboundedness of Θ.
  3. Fisher information in exponential families. Compute I(η) for the Bernoulli, Poisson and gamma families in both natural and mean parameterizations, and verify Iμ=Iη−1 (the metric transforms as a tensor).
  4. A minimax bound end to end. Prove the minimax rate for estimating a σ-known Gaussian mean over the ℓ2 ball of radius B is min(B,σ√d/n), using Le Cam for small B and Fano for large.
  5. Implementation. Simulate d=1..50, verify the JS risk curve numerically, and check SURE is an unbiased risk estimate by comparing 𝔼[SUREˆ] against the Monte Carlo risk.
Two papers

Foundational: Stein (1956), Inadmissibility of the usual estimator for the mean of a multivariate normal distribution. Read for the geometry, not the algebra.
Modern: Efron (2010), Large-scale inference. Read Ch. 1–3 and connect empirical Bayes to the FDR material in Lecture 22 — Efron's point is that they are the same calculation.
