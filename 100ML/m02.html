The Learning Problem, Formalized

Day 19 · 85 min

What would it mean to have an algorithm that learns anything?

PAC learnability

ℋ is agnostic PAC learnable if there is mℋ(ϵ,δ) and an algorithm A such that for every distribution D over 𝒳×𝒴 and every n≥mℋ(ϵ,δ), with probability ≥1−δ over the sample, R(A(S))≤infh∈ℋR(h)+ϵ. In the realizable case one additionally assumes infh∈ℋR(h)=0.

Three features to notice. Distribution-free: the guarantee is uniform over all D — there is no model. Relative: we compete with the best in class, not with the Bayes rule; approximation error is out of scope by construction. Proper vs improper:

if A must output a member of ℋ it is proper learning; allowing A to output anything (improper) is sometimes strictly easier computationally, and the gap is a real phenomenon (Lecture 38).

Theorem — No Free Lunch

Let 𝒳 be infinite (or |𝒳|≥2n) and consider all functions 𝒳→{0,1}. For any algorithm A and any n, there is a distribution D with R⋆=0 such that 𝔼S[R(A(S))]≥1/4 with probability ≥1/7.

proof sketchAverage over all 22n labelings of a fixed 2n-point set; each labeling is equally likely, so on the ≥n unseen points the algorithm's expected error is exactly 1/2 whatever it does. Averaging bounds the max from below, then Markov converts the mean into a probability statement.

What NFL means: the class of all functions is not learnable; some inductive bias is mandatory. What NFL does not mean: that all inductive biases are equally good in practice. The theorem averages over a uniform distribution on labelings — an assumption about problem instances that no one believes. It is a statement about the impossibility of assumption-free learning, not about the futility of choosing well.

Uniform Convergence and Finite Classes

Day 20 · 85 min

Why isn't Hoeffding enough?

Hoeffding bounds |Rˆn(h)−R(h)| for a fixed h. But hˆ is chosen using the data, so it is not fixed — this is the single most common error in the field (Lecture 15). The fix is to make the bound hold for all h simultaneously.

Uniform convergence

S is ϵ-representative if suph∈ℋ|Rˆn(h)−R(h)|≤ϵ. If so, then for hˆ=ERM(S), R(hˆ)≤Rˆn(hˆ)+ϵ≤Rˆn(h⋆)+ϵ≤R(h⋆)+2ϵ. Three inequalities; the factor 2 is the price of using the sample twice.

Theorem — finite classes

For |ℋ|<∞ and loss in [0,1], with probability ≥1−δ, ∀h:R(h)≤Rˆn(h)+√log|ℋ|+log(2/δ)2n. Realizable case (a consistent h exists): the sharper R(hˆ)≤log|ℋ|+log(1/δ)n, so sample complexity O(1ϵlog|ℋ|δ) rather than O(1ϵ2⋅).

proofHoeffding per h at level δ/|ℋ|, then union bound. Realizable case: bad hypotheses (R>ϵ) survive n samples with probability ≤(1−ϵ)n≤e−ϵn; union bound over at most |ℋ| of them.

The 1/n vs 1/√n distinction is the first appearance of fast rates: when the optimum has zero (or very small) risk, variance is small and Bernstein-type behaviour takes over. This recurs in Lecture 38 under the name Massart/Tsybakov noise, and in Lecture 34 under strong convexity.

Where it breaks

log|ℋ| is infinite for every class anyone uses. The next four lectures are four different answers to “what plays the role of log|ℋ| for infinite classes”: VC dimension (combinatorial), Rademacher complexity (distribution-dependent), metric entropy (geometric), and PAC-Bayes KL (measure-theoretic).

VC Theory I: Combinatorial Capacity

Day 21 · 85 min

An infinite class restricted to n points is finite. How finite?

Definitions

Restriction ℋC={(h(c1),…,h(cn)):h∈ℋ}⊆{0,1}n. ℋ shatters C if |ℋC|=2|C|. VC dimension VCdim(ℋ)=sup{|C|:ℋshattersC}. Growth function Πℋ(n)=max|C|=n|ℋC|.

Lemma — Sauer–Shelah

If VCdim(ℋ)=d then Πℋ(n)≤∑i=0d(ni)≤(en/d)d for n≥d.

proofInduction on n+d via the shifting/down-shift operator: show |ℋC|≤|{B⊆C:ℋshattersB}|. Removing a point splits ℋC into the restriction and the part with both labelings on that point; each piece is handled by the inductive hypothesis, and shattered sets combine additively.

The phase transition matters more than the bound. Πℋ(n) is 2n for n≤d and polynomial O(nd) for n>d — there is nothing in between. Since generalization bounds carry √logΠ(n)/n, exponential growth is fatal and polynomial growth is harmless: capacity is binary at the level of growth type.

VC Theory II: Bounds and the Fundamental Theorem

Day 22 · 85 min

From a counting lemma to a distribution-free generalization bound.

Theorem — VC generalization bound

With probability ≥1−δ, simultaneously for all h∈ℋ with VCdim=d: R(h)≤Rˆn(h)+√8(dlog2end+log4δ)n.

proof — three moves (1) Symmetrization / ghost sample: ℙ(suph|R−Rˆn|>ϵ)≤2ℙ(suph|Rˆn−Rˆ′n|>ϵ/2) — replace the unknown R by a second sample. (2) Double sample / permutation: conditional on the 2n points, only Πℋ(2n) distinct behaviours exist; randomly swapping (Xi,Xi′) leaves the law invariant. (3) Union bound + Hoeffding over those Πℋ(2n) behaviours. Sauer–Shelah turns Π into (2en/d)d; take logs.

Fundamental Theorem of PAC learning

For binary classification with 0–1 loss, the following are equivalent: (i) ℋ has the uniform convergence property; (ii) any ERM is an agnostic PAC learner; (iii) ℋ is agnostic PAC learnable; (iv) ℋ is PAC learnable; (v) VCdim(ℋ)<∞. Moreover mℋ(ϵ,δ)=Θ(d+log(1/δ)ϵ2)(agnostic),Θ(dlog(1/ϵ)+log(1/δ)ϵ)(realizable).

The lower bound comes from a No-Free-Lunch argument restricted to a shattered set of size d; the upper bound from the theorem above. Matching to constants means VC dimension is exactly the right notion of capacity for binary classification — a rare complete answer.

When is a VC bound non-vacuous?

Structural risk minimization. Given a nested ℋ1⊂ℋ2⊂… with weights wk, ∑wk≤1, minimize Rˆn(h)+pen(k,n,δwk) over all k and h∈ℋk. The resulting bound holds simultaneously over the whole hierarchy and gives an oracle inequality: SRM performs as well as the best k up to the penalty. This is the theoretical template for every model-selection criterion in Lecture 146.

Where it breaks

VC bounds are famously vacuous for modern models: with d≈WlogW and W≫n, the bound exceeds 1. The correct reading is not that the theory is wrong — the theorem is true and its lower bound is achieved by some distribution — but that distribution-free worst-case capacity is the wrong quantity when the algorithm and the data conspire to select a benign subset of the class. That motivates Rademacher complexity (data-dependent, L31), margins (L33), stability (L34) and PAC-Bayes (L35).

Rademacher Complexity

Day 23 · 85 min

How well can this class fit pure noise — on this data?

Definition

ℜˆS(ℱ)=𝔼εsupf∈ℱ1n∑iεif(xi) (empirical), ℜn(ℱ)=𝔼SℜˆS(ℱ) (expected), εi i.i.d. uniform on {±1}.

Theorem — Rademacher generalization bound

For loss in [0,1], with probability ≥1−δ, for all h: R(h)≤Rˆn(h)+2ℜn(ℓ∘ℋ)+√log(1/δ)2n, and with the empirical version, R(h)≤Rˆn(h)+2ℜˆS(ℓ∘ℋ)+3√log(2/δ)2n.

proofMcDiarmid on Φ(S)=suph(R(h)−Rˆn(h)) (bounded differences 1/n), then symmetrization (Lecture 13) to bound 𝔼Φ by 2ℜn. The empirical version needs a second McDiarmid on ℜˆS itself.

The toolkit

  • Massart's finite class lemma. ℜˆS(A)≤maxa∈A‖a‖2√2log|A|n for finite A⊂ℝn. Combined with Sauer–Shelah this recovers ℜn≲√d/n, so VC theory is a corollary.
  • Contraction (Talagrand/Ledoux). If ϕi are L-Lipschitz with ϕi(0)=0, then ℜˆS(ϕ∘ℱ)≤LℜˆS(ℱ). This is what lets you analyze the hypothesis class and then compose with the loss for free — and it is why Lipschitz losses (hinge, logistic, Huber) are analytically pleasant while the squared loss needs a boundedness argument.
  • Convex hulls. ℜˆS(conv(ℱ))=ℜˆS(ℱ). Ensembling and boosting do not increase Rademacher complexity — a striking fact that explains a great deal about Lecture 92.
  • Linear classes. For ℱ={x↦w⊤x:‖w‖2≤B} with ‖xi‖2≤R: ℜˆS(ℱ)≤BR/√n. For ‖w‖1≤B, ‖x‖∞≤R: ℜˆS≤BR√2log(2p)/n — only logarithmic in dimension. The ℓ1/ℓ∞ pairing is why sparse linear methods survive p≫n (Module 4).
  • Kernel classes. ℜˆS({f∈ℋK:‖f‖K≤B})≤Bn√trK — capacity is the trace of the kernel matrix, not the dimension of the feature space, which may be infinite (Lecture 75).
Where it breaks

Rademacher complexity is a global quantity over the whole class, so it yields 1/√n rates only. Fast 1/n rates require local Rademacher complexity — restricting to {h:𝔼(ℓh−ℓh⋆)2≤r} and solving a fixed-point equation for r. Any claim of an O(1/n) excess risk bound that uses only global Rademacher is wrong.

Covering Numbers, Metric Entropy, Chaining

Day 24 · 85 min

Rademacher complexity is a supremum over a continuum. How is one computed?

Covering/packing equivalence, metric entropy and Dudley's integral were set up in Lecture 13; here they are put to work.

The two bounds, compared

One-step discretization: ℜˆS(ℱ)≤infα>0(α+√2logN2(ℱ,α)n).
Chaining (Dudley): ℜˆS(ℱ)≤12∫0diam√logN2(ℱ,ϵ)ndϵ.
When logN≍dlog(1/ϵ) the first gives √dlogn/n and the second √d/n — chaining removes the log. When logN≍ϵ−p with p<2, chaining gives n−1/2; with p>2 it gives the slower n−1/p, which is the correct nonparametric rate.

Fat-shattering dimension

ℱ γ-shatters {x1..xk} if there are witnesses si such that every ± pattern is realized with margin γ: f(xi)≥si+γ or ≤si−γ. fatγ(ℱ) is the largest such k. It is the scale-sensitive analogue of VC dimension, and it is finite at every scale for classes with infinite VC dimension — which is exactly how margin bounds (next lecture) evade the VC catastrophe.

Margin Theory

Day 25 · 85 min

How can a bound for linear classifiers in ℝ106 not mention 106?

Theorem — margin bound for linear separators

Let ‖x‖≤R, ℱ={x↦w⊤x:‖w‖≤B}, and define the empirical margin error Rˆnγ(f)=1n∑i𝟙[yif(xi)≤γ]. Then w.p. ≥1−δ, for all f∈ℱ: R(f)≤Rˆnγ(f)+2BRγ√n+3√log(2/δ)2n. The ambient dimension does not appear: capacity is controlled by BR/γ, the inverse normalized margin.

proofThe ramp loss ϕγ (1 below 0, 0 above γ, linear between) sandwiches the 0–1 loss between Rˆγn and R; it is 1/γ-Lipschitz, so contraction (L31) gives ℜ(ϕγ∘ℱ)≤ℜ(ℱ)/γ≤BR/(γ√n); apply the Rademacher bound.

Boosting's margin explanation. AdaBoost continues to reduce test error after training error hits zero. Schapire et al. explained this as continued growth of the margin distribution over the convex hull of weak learners, with a bound in terms of Rˆγ and the weak learners' VC dimension, independent of the number of rounds. Breiman then built arc-gv, which maximizes the minimum margin directly, and found it generalized worse — showing the minimum margin is not the operative quantity. Later work (Reyzin–Schapire) attributed the discrepancy to base-learner complexity, and the current view emphasizes the whole margin distribution. Read this episode as a case study in how a theory survives a well-designed refutation attempt in modified form.

Modern descendant. Norm-based capacity control: for deep networks, bounds scale with products of layer norms divided by the margin, not with parameter count. They are still typically vacuous, but they are correlated with generalization across architectures, which is the currently defensible claim.

Algorithmic Stability

Day 25 · 75 min

Forget the hypothesis class. What if the algorithm barely notices when one data point changes?

Uniform stability

A is β-uniformly stable if for all samples S, all i, and all z: |ℓ(A(S),z)−ℓ(A(S∖i),z)|≤β.

Theorem — stability implies generalization

If A is β-uniformly stable then |𝔼S[R(A(S))−Rˆn(A(S))]|≤β. With bounded loss, a high-probability version gives generalization gap O(β√nlog(1/δ)+√log(1/δ)/n) (Feldman–Vondrák sharpen this to βlognlog(n/δ)+√log(1/δ)/n).

proof of the expectation versionBy symmetry 𝔼SRˆn(A(S))=𝔼S,z′ℓ(A(Si←z′),zi), i.e. the empirical risk is a leave-one-out risk in disguise; the difference from 𝔼R is exactly a single-point replacement, bounded by β.

Theorem — regularized ERM is stable

If ℓ(⋅,z) is L-Lipschitz and convex, and the regularizer is λ-strongly convex, then A(S)=argminRˆn+λΩ is 2L2λn-uniformly stable, giving generalization gap O(L2/(λn)).

Set against the excess-risk bound this reproduces the standard λ≍1/√n tuning and O(1/√n) risk — without ever mentioning a hypothesis class. That is the appeal: the analysis follows the algorithm, so it applies where the class is uncontrolled.

SGD is stable. Hardt–Recht–Singer: for convex L-Lipschitz β-smooth losses, T steps of SGD with step sizes ηt is 2L2n∑tηt-stable. For nonconvex smooth losses the bound degrades with T but is still finite, giving the “train fast, generalize well” heuristic a precise (if weak) form.

Stability and privacy. Differential privacy is a strong form of stability — a ε-DP algorithm is O(ε)-stable in a distributional sense — which is why DP implies generalization and underwrites the reusable holdout of Lecture 153. Stability ⇒ generalization, but not conversely: an ERM over a finite class generalizes yet may be maximally unstable (ties broken adversarially).

PAC-Bayesian Analysis

Day 26 · 85 min

What if instead of one hypothesis we output a distribution over hypotheses?

Theorem — McAllester's PAC-Bayes bound

Fix a prior P on ℋ before seeing data. Then with probability ≥1−δ over S, simultaneously for all posteriors Q (data-dependent allowed): 𝔼h∼QR(h)≤𝔼h∼QRˆn(h)+√KL(Q‖P)+log2√nδ2n.

proofLet Δ(h)=R(h)−Rˆn(h). By Donsker–Varadhan (Lecture 11), for any λ, λ𝔼QΔ≤KL(Q‖P)+log𝔼h∼PeλΔ(h). The last term is bounded by Hoeffding under the prior — which is legitimate because P does not depend on the data — and Markov converts it to a high-probability statement. Optimizing λ gives the square root.

The change-of-measure step is the whole idea: pay KL(Q‖P) to move from a data-free measure to a data-dependent one. Note this generalizes the union bound: taking P uniform on a finite class and Q a point mass recovers KL=log|ℋ| exactly.

As a training objective. Because the bound holds for all Q simultaneously, you may minimize it over Q — the result is a certified risk bound for the trained model. Dziugaite–Roy did exactly this for a stochastic neural network on MNIST and obtained the first non-vacuous generalization bound for an overparameterized network; the field's tightest non-vacuous bounds remain PAC-Bayesian.

Where it breaks

The prior must be data-independent. “Data-dependent priors” are legal only if built on a held-out split (which costs sample) or through a differential-privacy argument. And the bound is on the Gibbs risk 𝔼QR(h); converting to the risk of the majority vote or the mean predictor costs a factor of 2 in general, which is often the difference between vacuous and not.

Complexity, Interpolation and Double Descent

Day 26 · 85 min

Classical theory says interpolating noise is fatal. Empirically it often is not. Which hypothesis was wrong?

Double descent — minimum-norm least squares, p features, n samples

What the classical U-curve assumes. That the model is fit by unregularized ERM within a class indexed by complexity, and that at fixed complexity the fit is essentially unique. At p=n the least-squares solution is unique but wildly ill-conditioned (σmin→0 by Marchenko–Pastur, Lecture 52): variance explodes. That spike is the interpolation threshold, and it is real.

What happens past it. For p>n infinitely many interpolators exist and the algorithm picks one — the minimum-ℓ2-norm solution βˆ=X+y. As p grows further, the extra directions absorb the noise while the norm constraint keeps the solution tame, and risk descends again. The implicit bias of the algorithm, not the size of the class, is doing the work.

Theorem — benign overfitting (Bartlett, Long, Lugosi, Tsigler 2020)

For the minimum-norm interpolator in linear regression with covariance Σ (eigenvalues λ1≥λ2≥…), define for k≥0 rk(Σ)=∑i>kλiλk+1,Rk(Σ)=(∑i>kλi)2∑i>kλi2. Overfitting is benign — excess risk →0 — iff there is k=o(n) with rk(Σ)≳n and Rk(Σ)≫n. Interpretation: a few high-variance directions carry the signal, and a large but not too large number of low-variance directions absorb the noise. If the spectrum decays too fast (say λi≍i−2) there is nowhere to hide the noise and interpolation is harmful; if it decays too slowly the signal directions are not estimable.

Exact asymptotics. In the proportional regime p/n→γ with isotropic features, the risk of min-norm least squares is computable in closed form from the Marchenko–Pastur law: it diverges as γ→1 from either side and decreases for γ>1 toward σ2(1+SNR−1⋅something). Ridge with the optimal λ has no peak at all — the double-descent spike is an artifact of using λ=0, and optimal regularization monotonizes the curve. Nakkiran et al. verified this empirically for deep networks too.

What this does and does not overturn

Does not overturn: uniform convergence bounds — they were always worst-case over the class, and a min-norm interpolator lives in a tiny, data-dependent subset. The bias–variance decomposition — still exactly true, just with variance non-monotone in p. Minimax lower bounds — untouched.
Does overturn: the folk claim that “model complexity” is a scalar that must be tuned to a sweet spot, and the practice of reading test-error non-monotonicity as a bug. It also weakens the case for parameter counting as capacity, permanently.

Surrogate Losses and Consistency

Day 27 · 85 min

Minimizing hinge loss is not minimizing error rate. When does the first imply the second?

Classification-calibrated

For margin-based ϕ define H(η)=infα(ηϕ(α)+(1−η)ϕ(−α)) and H−(η)=infα(2η−1)≤0(⋅). ϕ is classification-calibrated if H−(η)>H(η) for all η≠12 — the constrained optimum (wrong sign) is strictly worse.

Theorem — Bartlett, Jordan & McAuliffe

ϕ is classification-calibrated iff, with ψ the convexified H−−H transform, ψ(R(signf)−R⋆)≤Rϕ(f)−Rϕ⋆forallf. Hence ϕ-risk consistency implies 0–1 consistency. For convex ϕ, calibration ⟺ ϕ is differentiable at 0 with ϕ′(0)<0 — an easy test.

The hinge's linear ψ means excess hinge risk transfers to excess error at rate 1 rather than 12 — an argument in the SVM's favour for pure classification. Its flat side means it does not estimate η, which is an argument against it whenever you need probabilities (Lecture 147).

Multiclass is genuinely harder. The natural one-vs-all extension of the hinge is not Fisher consistent (Lee–Lin–Wahba fixed it with a sum-to-zero constraint; Crammer–Singer's multiclass hinge is inconsistent unless a dominant class exists). Weston–Watkins likewise. This is not a corner case — inconsistent multiclass surrogates are widely used, and the failure shows up as systematic bias when no class has probability above 1/2.

Algorithm-level consistency. SVMs with a universal kernel and λn→0 at the right rate are consistent (Steinwart); AdaBoost with early stopping is consistent (Bartlett–Traskin, Zhang–Yu); run to convergence on separable data it is not.

Computational Learning Theory

Day 27 · 85 min

Finite VC dimension makes learning possible. Does it make learning tractable?

Efficient PAC learning requires runtime polynomial in 1/ϵ,1/δ,n and the representation size. Statistical and computational learnability come apart immediately:

  • Representation-dependent hardness. Proper learning of 3-term DNF is NP-hard, but 3-term DNF is improperly learnable in polynomial time as 3-CNF. Hardness was an artifact of the output format.
  • Cryptographic hardness. Under standard assumptions (RSA, discrete log, or LWE), poly-size circuits, intersections of halfspaces, and DNF are not efficiently PAC learnable at all. These are unconditional-in-spirit barriers, not artifacts.
  • Agnostic learning of halfspaces is hard even to weakly approximate; learning a single ReLU under Gaussian marginals is hard in the SQ model.
Theorem — weak learning equals strong learning

If there is an algorithm returning h with R(h)≤12−γ for any distribution, then there is one achieving R≤ϵ for every ϵ>0, in time polynomial in 1/γ,1/ϵ. The proof is constructive: it is boosting (Lecture 92).

Statistical query model. An SQ algorithm sees only noisy estimates of 𝔼D[ψ(x,y)] for chosen ψ. Almost every practical algorithm (gradient methods on population losses, moment methods, EM) is SQ. Kearns showed SQ learnability implies learnability under classification noise, and SQ-dimension lower bounds give unconditional hardness — parity functions are learnable with samples but not by any SQ algorithm with polynomially many queries. This is the cleanest available account of statistical–computational gaps, and it recurs in sparse PCA (L122) and mixture learning (L120).

Noise conditions and fast rates

Massart noise: |η(x)−12|≥h>0 almost surely. Then ERM achieves O(d/(hn)) — a 1/n rate.
Tsybakov noise: ℙ(|η(X)−12|≤t)≤Ctα. Excess risk O((d/n)α+1α+2), interpolating between 1/√n (α=0) and 1/n (α=∞, Massart).
The mechanism is a Bernstein condition 𝔼(ℓh−ℓh⋆)2≤B𝔼(ℓh−ℓh⋆), which makes the variance small near the optimum and lets localization kick in (Lecture 31).

Random vs adversarial noise. Random classification noise (each label flipped w.p. p<1/2 independently) is benign — the SQ reduction handles it. Adversarial (agnostic) noise at the same rate is dramatically harder, with sharp hardness results. Never quote a result for one model as if it applied to the other.

Learning Beyond i.i.d.

Day 28 · 85 min

Train and test distributions differ. What survives?

Taxonomy by factorization

Covariate shift: Ptr(x)≠Pte(x), P(y∣x) shared. Label/prior shift: P(y) changes, P(x∣y) shared. Concept drift: P(y∣x) changes. Domain shift: both. The distinction is causal, not cosmetic — which factorization is stable depends on the causal direction (Lecture 144).

Importance weighting. Under covariate shift with support containment, Rte(h)=𝔼tr[w(X)ℓ(h,X,Y)], w=pte/ptr. Unbiased, but variance ∝𝔼tr[w2]=1+χ2(Pte‖Ptr). The effective sample size neff=(∑wi)2/∑wi2 collapses when the densities differ appreciably — with χ2=100, weighting a million samples buys you ten thousand. This is exactly the IPW instability of Lecture 140.

Theorem — domain adaptation bound (Ben-David et al.)

For a hypothesis class ℋ and source/target S,T: RT(h)≤RS(h)+12dℋΔℋ(S,T)+λ⋆, where dℋΔℋ=2suph,h′∈ℋ|ℙS(h≠h′)−ℙT(h≠h′)| and λ⋆=infh(RS(h)+RT(h)) is the joint optimum.

Two consequences usually skipped: the divergence is class-relative (it can be estimated by training a classifier to distinguish source from target — the classifier two-sample test of Lecture 150), and λ⋆ is not estimable without target labels. Any adaptation method that minimizes the first two terms alone is silently assuming λ⋆ is small — which representation-alignment methods can actively destroy by collapsing features.

Dependent data. With β-mixing, blocking arguments (Yu) replace n by the number of nearly independent blocks μn, and every bound in this module goes through with n→μn and an additive mixing-coefficient term. For Markov chains, μn≈n/tmix: the effective sample size is the number of mixing times observed.

DRO. Minimize supQ:D(Q‖Pn)≤ρ𝔼Qℓ. With χ2-balls this is asymptotically variance-regularized ERM: 𝔼Pnℓ+√2ρVarPn(ℓ)/n — so DRO's generalization benefit is a variance penalty, and its robustness guarantee holds only against shifts inside the chosen ball.
