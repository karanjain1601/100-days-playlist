Optimization for Machine Learning

Reading: B&V Ch. 9–11 · Nesterov Ch. 1–3 · Bubeck · Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal. Two numbers (L, μ) and one structural fact (finite sums) generate every algorithm and every rate below.

61

The Optimization View of Learning

Day 43 · 85 min

How accurately should you solve a problem whose answer you do not actually want?

Recall Lecture 1: total excess risk = approximation + estimation + optimization. Estimation error is Θ(√d/n) or so. Driving optimization error to 10−12 when estimation error is 10−2 is pure waste. Bottou–Bousquet formalize this: at a fixed compute budget, the best method is the one minimizing the sum, and that is usually a stochastic method with a mediocre convergence rate but a very cheap iteration.

Oracle complexity model

The algorithm queries a first-order oracle returning (f(x),∇f(x)) and is assumed to produce iterates in x0+span{∇f(x0),…,∇f(xk−1)}. Complexity = number of queries to reach ϵ accuracy, worst case over the function class.

Theorem — first-order lower bounds (Nemirovski–Yudin, Nesterov)

For L-smooth convex f on ℝd with d≥2k+1: f(xk)−f⋆≥3L‖x0−x⋆‖232(k+1)2.
For L-smooth μ-strongly convex on ℓ2: ‖xk−x⋆‖2≥(√κ−1√κ+1)2k‖x0−x⋆‖2.
For nonsmooth Lipschitz convex: Ω(1/ϵ2) queries.
For smooth nonconvex, reaching ‖∇f‖≤ϵ: Ω(ϵ−2); with second-order smoothness, Ω(ϵ−7/4).

The proof is a resisting oracle on the "worst function in the world" f(x)=L8[(x1−1)2+∑(xi−xi+1)2]: each gradient query reveals only one new coordinate, so after k steps the iterate is supported on k coordinates and cannot be close. This is why the lower bound needs d>k — in low dimension the bound is vacuous, and methods can be faster.

ClassLower boundOptimal method
Nonsmooth convexO(1/ϵ2)Subgradient / mirror descent
Smooth convexO(1/√ϵ)Nesterov AGD
Smooth strongly convexO(√κlog1ϵ)Nesterov AGD (strongly convex form)
Finite sum, smooth SCΩ((n+√nκ)log1ϵ)Katyusha / accelerated SVRG
Stochastic convexΩ(1/ϵ2)SGD with averaging — already optimal

The last row is worth pausing on: SGD is minimax optimal for the statistical problem. All the sophistication in Lectures 63–70 buys nothing in the pure stochastic setting; it buys speed only when you exploit finite-sum structure or curvature.

Gradient Descent: Rates and Conditions

Day 44 · 85 min
Descent lemma and the two rates

L-smoothness gives f(x−η∇f)≤f(x)−η(1−Lη2)‖∇f‖2; with η=1/L this is f+≤f−12L‖∇f‖2.
Convex: f(xk)−f⋆≤L‖x0−x⋆‖22k — O(1/k).
μ-strongly convex: ‖xk−x⋆‖2≤(1−μ/L)k‖x0−x⋆‖2 — linear, with rate governed by κ=L/μ.
Nonconvex smooth: mink<K‖∇f(xk)‖2≤2L(f(x0)−f⋆)K — O(1/√K) to an ϵ-stationary point.

proof, strongly convex case‖x+−x⋆‖2=‖x−x⋆‖2−2η∇f(x)⊤(x−x⋆)+η2‖∇f‖2. Use co-coercivity ∇f(x)⊤(x−x⋆)≥μLμ+L‖x−x⋆‖2+1μ+L‖∇f‖2; with η=2/(μ+L) everything cancels into a contraction factor (κ−1κ+1)2.

First-order methods on an ill-conditioned quadratic

Exact analysis on quadratics. For f=12x⊤Ax, GD is xk=(I−ηA)kx0, so in the eigenbasis each coordinate decays as (1−ηλi)k. The optimal step is η=2/(λ1+λd) and the worst-case factor is κ−1κ+1 — attained simultaneously by the largest and smallest eigendirections, which is why GD zig-zags: it oscillates in the stiff direction while creeping in the flat one.

Beyond convexity: the PL condition 12‖∇f(x)‖2≥μ(f(x)−f⋆) gives linear convergence with no convexity at all, and covers overparameterized least squares, some matrix factorizations, and (locally) wide networks. It is strictly weaker than strong convexity and is the right hypothesis for many "why does GD work here" questions.

Line search. Exact line search is rarely worth it. Backtracking with the Armijo condition f(x−ηg)≤f(x)−c1η‖g‖2 (c1≈10−4) is the practical default; adding the curvature (Wolfe) condition matters only for quasi-Newton, where it guarantees the BFGS update stays positive definite.

Acceleration

Day 45 · 85 min
Nesterov's accelerated gradient

yk=xk+βk(xk−xk−1), xk+1=yk−1L∇f(yk).
Convex: βk=k−1k+2 gives f(xk)−f⋆≤2L‖x0−x⋆‖2(k+1)2.
Strongly convex: β=√κ−1√κ+1 gives rate (1−1/√κ)k.
Both match the lower bounds of Lecture 61 up to constants: AGD is optimal.

Estimate sequences (Nesterov's original proof): construct ϕk(x)=ϕk⋆+γk2‖x−vk‖2 with ϕk(x)≤(1−λk)f(x)+λkϕ0(x) and f(xk)≤ϕk⋆. Inductive bookkeeping; correct but unilluminating.

Three modern readings. (i) ODE limit: as η→0 AGD becomes X¨+3tX˙+∇f(X)=0 — a damped oscillator with vanishing friction (Su–Boyd–Candès); the 3/t damping is exactly what buys O(1/t2). (ii) Lyapunov: Ek=k2(f(xk)−f⋆)+2L‖vk−x⋆‖2 is non-increasing — a three-line proof. (iii) Linear coupling (Allen-Zhu–Orecchia): AGD interleaves a gradient step (good when gradients are large) with a mirror step (good when they are small); the momentum is the coupling weight.

Why acceleration is fragile

Not a descent method: f(xk) can increase; that is fine in theory and alarming in practice. Noise accumulates: with inexact gradients of accuracy δ, plain GD's error stays O(δ) while AGD's grows like kδ — momentum amplifies bias. This is why acceleration gives little in the stochastic regime and why "Nesterov momentum" in deep learning is doing something else (variance smoothing, effective step-size scaling) than the theorem describes. Restart — reset momentum whenever f increases or ∇f(yk)⊤(xk+1−xk)>0 — recovers linear convergence adaptively without knowing μ, and is the fix worth implementing.

Proximal and Composite Methods

Day 45 · 85 min

Objective F=f+g with f smooth and g convex but nonsmooth (a regularizer or an indicator). Do not subgradient the whole thing — that throws away the smoothness of f and drops you to O(1/√k).

Proximal operator

proxηg(v)=argminx{g(x)+12η‖x−v‖2}. It is firmly nonexpansive, and x⋆ minimizes F iff x⋆=proxηg(x⋆−η∇f(x⋆)).

Moreau envelope Mηg(v)=minx{g(x)+12η‖x−v‖2} is C1 with ∇Mηg(v)=1η(v−proxηg(v)), and M↑g as η↓0: the prox is a gradient step on a smoothed surrogate. Moreau decomposition v=proxg(v)+proxg*(v) links primal and dual proxes and is how soft thresholding and ℓ∞-ball projection turn out to be the same computation.

Proximal Newton replaces the quadratic 12η‖x−v‖2 with 12(x−v)⊤H(x−v); each step is a scaled lasso subproblem solved by coordinate descent. This is exactly how glmnet fits penalized GLMs: outer IRLS/Newton, inner coordinate descent on a weighted lasso.

Coordinate Descent

Day 46 · 75 min

Update one coordinate at a time, exactly. For quadratics and separable penalties each update is closed form and costs O(n) rather than O(np) — a p-fold saving per unit of progress if progress per coordinate is comparable.

Rates

Randomized CD on Li-coordinate-smooth convex f: 𝔼[f(xk)]−f⋆≤2pLR2k; strongly convex: linear with rate (1−μpL)k. Compare full GD's (1−μ/L)k per iteration but p× the cost: CD wins whenever L≪L, i.e. when coordinates are nearly decoupled. Gauss–Southwell (greedy on |∇if|) can be up to p times faster than random but costs a full gradient to select — worth it only when the gradient is cheap to maintain incrementally.

Theorem — separability is essential

If F=f+∑igi(xi) with f smooth convex and gi convex, every coordinatewise minimum is a global minimum. Without separability this fails: Tseng's classic counterexample f(x,y)=|x−y|+12|x+y|-type nonsmooth couplings have points where no single coordinate move improves but a diagonal move does. The lasso is separable; the fused lasso is not, and naive coordinate descent gets stuck on it.

Block CD updates groups (needed for group lasso). Parallel/asynchronous: Hogwild! runs CD updates in shared memory without locks; the analysis shows the convergence rate is essentially unaffected provided the maximum overlap between concurrently updated coordinates is small (Δ≲√p sparsity condition), because stale gradients act like a bounded delay perturbation. On dense problems, Hogwild!'s guarantee evaporates.

Duality-Based and Distributed Methods

Day 46 · 85 min

Dual ascent maximizes g(ν)=infxL(x,ν) by gradient ascent, with ∇g(ν)=Ax⋆(ν)−b. It needs strict convexity for x⋆(ν) to be unique and is fragile otherwise. The augmented Lagrangian Lρ=f(x)+ν⊤(Ax−b)+ρ2‖Ax−b‖2 restores strong convexity in x and gives the method of multipliers, which converges under far weaker conditions — at the cost of destroying separability in x.

ADMM

For minf(x)+g(z) s.t. Ax+Bz=c: xk+1=argminxLρ(x,zk,uk),zk+1=argminzLρ(xk+1,z,uk),uk+1=uk+Axk+1+Bzk+1−c, with the scaled dual u=ν/ρ. Convergence: for closed proper convex f,g and any ρ>0, the residuals →0 and the objective →p⋆. Rate is O(1/k) in the ergodic sense, linear under strong convexity plus full rank.

ADMM's appeal is that it splits: the x- and z-steps are separate proxes. Lasso becomes a ridge solve plus a soft threshold; consensus ADMM (xi local, z global, constraint xi=z) turns any distributed ERM into local solves plus an average. Its weakness is ρ-sensitivity: convergence is guaranteed for all ρ but the practical rate varies by orders of magnitude, and adaptive ρ schemes are heuristic.

Multi-block ADMM can diverge

The direct three-block extension is not convergent in general — Chen, He, Ye & Yuan give a 3×3 linear system on which it cycles. Fixes: Gaussian back-substitution, prediction–correction, or reformulating as two blocks. Any paper using three-block ADMM without one of these is relying on luck.

SDCA maximizes the dual of a regularized ERM by randomized coordinate ascent on the n dual variables. For L-smooth losses with λ-strong regularization it converges linearly at rate (n+L/λ)log(1/ϵ) — the same complexity class as SVRG, reached from the dual side, and with a computable duality gap as a stopping criterion, which SGD lacks.

Stochastic Gradient Methods

Day 47 · 85 min
Robbins–Monro and the rates

xk+1=xk−ηkgk with 𝔼[gk∣xk]=∇f(xk), 𝔼‖gk‖2≤G2. Classical conditions ∑ηk=∞, ∑ηk2<∞ give a.s. convergence.
Convex, ηk=RG√k: 𝔼f(xK)−f⋆≤RG√K.
μ-strongly convex, ηk=1μk: 𝔼f(xK)−f⋆≤G22μK.
Both are optimal for the stochastic oracle (Lecture 61).

proof, convex case𝔼‖xk+1−x⋆‖2=‖xk−x⋆‖2−2ηk𝔼[∇f(xk)⊤(xk−x⋆)]+ηk2𝔼‖gk‖2≤‖xk−x⋆‖2−2ηk(f(xk)−f⋆)+ηk2G2. Telescope, divide by ∑ηk, apply Jensen to the average iterate.

The noise ball. With constant η, SGD converges linearly to a neighbourhood of radius O(ησ2/μ) and then stalls — that plateau is the entire reason for step-size decay schedules. The practical recipe (constant, then ×0.1 at plateaus) is exactly a discretization of ηk≍1/k with better constants.

Polyak–Ruppert averaging. Use a larger step ηk≍k−α, α∈(1/2,1), and average the iterates. Then √K(xK−x⋆)⇒N(0,H−1ΣH−1) — the same asymptotic covariance as the sample-path optimum, i.e. asymptotically efficient, without any second-order information. This is one of the most under-used results in applied optimization.

Minibatching. Variance falls as σ2/B, so the step can grow ∝B — linear speedup — until the deterministic term dominates at B⋆≍σ2/(Lϵ); beyond that, larger batches buy nothing per unit compute. This is the theoretical basis of the observed "critical batch size".

Sampling without replacement. Random reshuffling beats with-replacement sampling: O(1/K2) versus O(1/K) for strongly convex objectives after enough epochs (Gürbüzbalaban–Ozdaglar–Parrilo; HaoChen–Sra). Every practical implementation reshuffles; every classical proof assumes with replacement. The gap was open for years and is now closed in the right direction.

Variance Reduction

Day 48 · 85 min

The 1/√K rate comes entirely from the gradient noise, which does not vanish at the optimum. For a finite sum f=1n∑fi the noise is artificial: at x⋆ every ∇fi(x⋆) is known in principle. Exploit that.

SVRG

Every epoch, store a snapshot x˜ and the full gradient μ˜=∇f(x˜). Inner steps use vk=∇fik(xk)−∇fik(x˜)+μ˜, which is unbiased and has variance →0 as xk,x˜→x⋆. With η=O(1/L) and epoch length m=O(κ), SVRG converges linearly: complexity O((n+κ)log1ϵ) gradient evaluations, versus O(nκlog1ϵ) for full GD and O(κ/ϵ) for SGD.

key stepBound 𝔼‖vk‖2≤4L[f(xk)−f⋆+f(x˜)−f⋆] using smoothness and the variance identity 𝔼‖X−𝔼X‖2≤𝔼‖X‖2. The noise is now controlled by the suboptimality rather than by a constant σ2 — that is the entire trick.

MethodMemoryPer-stepComplexity
SAG / SAGAO(np) (or O(n) for GLMs)1 gradientO((n+κ)log1ϵ)
SVRGO(p)2 gradients + periodic full passO((n+κ)log1ϵ)
KatyushaO(p)2 gradients + momentumO((n+√nκ)log1ϵ) — optimal

For GLMs the stored gradient is ∇fi=ℓ′(xi⊤β)xi, so only the scalar ℓ′ needs storing: SAGA's memory drops to O(n), which is what makes it practical.

Why it disappoints in practice

(i) The gain is κ-dependent; for well-conditioned or heavily regularized problems SGD is already near-optimal. (ii) It requires a fixed finite sum — data augmentation, dropout, streaming data all break the premise. (iii) In deep learning the gradient variance at the relevant scale is dominated by curvature heterogeneity, not by sampling, and the snapshot goes stale within a fraction of an epoch (Defazio–Bottou's careful negative result). Variance reduction is a triumph for convex finite-sum ERM and largely inapplicable to modern nonconvex training.

Adaptive Methods, Analyzed

Day 48 · 75 min
AdaGrad, from regret

xk+1=xk−ηdiag(Gk)−1/2gk with Gk=∑t≤kgtgt⊤ (diagonal in practice). Regret RK≤√2D∞p∑j=1(K∑k=1gk,j2)1/2, which is ≪DG√K when gradients are sparse or heterogeneous in scale. AdaGrad is derived, not invented: it is the regularizer minimizing an online-learning regret bound (Lecture 130).

Adam replaces the sum by an EMA (vk=β2vk−1+(1−β2)gk2) plus momentum on the numerator and bias correction. Reddi, Kale & Kumar exhibited a simple convex online problem — a large informative gradient every third round, small opposing gradients otherwise — on which Adam converges to the worst point: the EMA forgets the rare large gradient, so its effective step on that coordinate is too small. The original convergence proof had an error in the sign of a telescoping term. AMSGrad fixes it by keeping vˆk=max(vˆk−1,vk), restoring the non-increasing step size that the analysis needs. Other fixes: AdaBound, Yogi, decoupled β2 schedules.

Adaptive vs SGD generalization gap. Wilson et al. exhibit a linearly separable construction where AdaGrad-family methods converge to a solution with poor test error while SGD finds the max-margin one; empirically, well-tuned SGD+momentum matches or beats Adam on convolutional vision tasks while Adam is clearly better for attention-based models and sparse-gradient problems. The honest summary: the choice is architecture- and conditioning-dependent, and the theory currently explains the phenomenon only in special cases.

Decoupled weight decay (AdamW). ℓ2 regularization added to the loss gets scaled by the adaptive preconditioner and is therefore not weight decay; decoupling (x←(1−ηλ)x−ηmˆ/√vˆ) restores the intended geometry. This is a genuine bug fix, not a hyperparameter, and it changes results materially.

What adaptivity is, geometrically: diagonal preconditioning. It is a cheap approximation to ∇2f and inherits both the benefit (scale invariance across coordinates) and the limitation (it cannot see rotations, so it does nothing for correlated coordinates — the exact case where curvature matters most).

Second-Order and Quasi-Newton Methods

Day 49 · 85 min
Newton's method

x+=x−∇2f(x)−1∇f(x). If ∇2f is M-Lipschitz and ∇2f(x⋆)⪰μI, then near x⋆: ‖x+−x⋆‖≤M2μ‖x−x⋆‖2 — quadratic convergence. It is affine invariant: the iterates under x→Ax are the images of the original iterates, so κ does not appear in the local rate at all.

Globalization requires damping (x+=x−tΔx with backtracking) because pure Newton can diverge far from the optimum, and regularization (∇2f+τI) because the Hessian may be indefinite. Self-concordance (|f′′′|≤2f′′3/2) removes the unknown constants: for self-concordant f, damped Newton's complexity is bounded by an explicit function of f(x0)−f⋆ with no problem-dependent constants. This is what makes interior-point methods provably polynomial.

BFGS

Maintain Bk≈∇2f satisfying the secant equation Bk+1sk=yk with sk=xk+1−xk, yk=∇fk+1−∇fk. Among all symmetric matrices satisfying it, BFGS is the one closest to Bk in a weighted Frobenius norm: Bk+1=Bk−Bksksk⊤Bksk⊤Bksk+ykyk⊤yk⊤sk. Positive definiteness is preserved iff yk⊤sk>0 — guaranteed by the Wolfe curvature condition. Superlinear local convergence, no Hessians.

L-BFGS stores only the last m≈5–20 pairs (s,y) and applies Hk∇f via the two-loop recursion in O(mp). It is the default for smooth deterministic problems with p up to millions, and it fails on stochastic gradients — the secant pairs become noise-dominated. Stochastic quasi-Newton variants exist but none is a settled default.

Gauss–Newton and the natural gradient. For f=12∑ri(θ)2, GN uses J⊤J in place of the Hessian, discarding ∑ri∇2ri — exact when residuals are small. Levenberg–Marquardt adds τI and interpolates to gradient descent. Separately, the natural gradient ∇˜=F−1∇ with F the Fisher information performs steepest descent in KL geometry rather than Euclidean geometry, and is therefore invariant to reparameterization. Key identity: for exponential-family likelihoods with the canonical link, the Fisher information equals the Gauss–Newton matrix — the two methods coincide, which is why "natural gradient" and "generalized Gauss–Newton" are used interchangeably in that setting. K-FAC approximates F as a Kronecker product A⊗G of input and gradient covariances per layer, making F−1 cheap.

Nonconvex Optimization

Day 49 · 85 min

Finding a global minimum of a general smooth nonconvex function is intractable; even checking local optimality is NP-hard for quartics. So the theory changes the question.

Stationarity ladder

ϵ-first-order: ‖∇f‖≤ϵ. Reached by GD in O(ϵ−2), matching the lower bound.
(ϵ,γ)-second-order: additionally λmin(∇2f)≥−γ.
Strict saddle property: every stationary point is a local min or has λmin(∇2f)<0. Under it, saddles are escapable.

Theorem — escaping saddles (Jin, Ge, Netrapalli, Kakade, Jordan)

Perturbed gradient descent — add a small random perturbation whenever the gradient has been small for a while — finds an (ϵ,√ρϵ)-second-order stationary point in O˜(ϵ−2) iterations, only polylogarithmically worse than reaching a first-order point. Saddles are not the obstacle they were assumed to be.

ideaAround a strict saddle, the set of initializations from which GD does not escape is contained in a thin "stuck region" — a pancake of width O˜(ϵ) around the stable manifold. A random perturbation of radius r misses it with high probability; two nearby points on opposite sides of the pancake separate exponentially along the negative-curvature direction.

Structured problems where global guarantees exist

ProblemLandscape resultCaveat
Matrix sensing/completion under RIPNo spurious local minima; all saddles strictNeeds incoherence and enough samples
Phase retrievalBenign landscape with m≳nlog3nSpectral init still preferred for speed
Orthogonal tensor decompositionAll local maxima of the objective are true componentsFails for overcomplete tensors
Dictionary learning (complete case)Benign on the sphereOvercomplete case open
“No spurious local minima” ≠ “easy”

The landscape can be free of bad local minima and still take exponential time — because the negative curvature may be exponentially small over an exponentially large plateau (as in some spin-glass and tensor PCA regimes). Landscape results bound one obstacle; they do not bound running time by themselves. Always ask whether the paper proves a rate or only proves the absence of bad stationary points.

Submodularity and Discrete Optimization in ML

Day 50 · 75 min
Submodularity

F:2V→ℝ is submodular if F(A∪{v})−F(A)≥F(B∪{v})−F(B) for A⊆B, v∉B — diminishing returns. Equivalently F(A)+F(B)≥F(A∪B)+F(A∩B).

The convexity analogy is exact. The Lovász extension fL(x)=𝔼θ∼U[0,1][F({i:xi≥θ})] is convex iff F is submodular, and minSF(S)=minx∈[0,1]nfL(x) with integral minimizers. Hence submodular minimization is polynomial (via the ellipsoid method or combinatorial algorithms, O˜(n3) oracle calls). Total-variation penalties are Lovász extensions of cut functions — which is why fused-lasso and graph-cut denoising problems are exactly solvable.

Theorem — greedy maximization

For monotone submodular F with F(∅)=0, greedy selection of k elements achieves F(Sk)≥(1−1/e)max|S|=kF(S)≈0.632⋅OPT.

proofLet S⋆ be optimal. Submodularity plus monotonicity give F(S⋆)≤F(Si)+∑v∈S⋆[F(Si∪v)−F(Si)]≤F(Si)+kδi+1 where δi+1 is the greedy gain. So OPT−F(Si+1)≤(1−1/k)(OPT−F(Si)); iterate k times and use (1−1/k)k≤1/e.

(1−1/e) is optimal: beating it requires exponentially many oracle queries (Nemhauser–Wolsey), and under P≠NP it is also hard for explicitly given instances like max-coverage (Feige). This is a rare case where a simple greedy algorithm is provably the best possible.

Where it appears. Sensor placement and experimental design (mutual information is submodular under conditional-independence assumptions; log-det of a submatrix is submodular — connecting to Bayesian experimental design in Lecture 116); active learning batch selection; document/video summarization via facility-location objectives; coreset construction; feature selection when the objective is mutual information (though I(XS;Y) is not submodular in general — a widely repeated error). Matroid constraints preserve a 1/2 guarantee for greedy and (1−1/e) for continuous greedy plus pipage rounding.

Recitation 5: Optimization Problem Set

Day 50 · 60 min
  1. Proximal gradient rate from scratch. Prove F(xk)−F⋆≤L‖x0−x⋆‖22k using only the prox optimality condition and the descent lemma. Identify the single place convexity of g is used.
  2. Break cyclic coordinate descent. Construct a convex nonsmooth non-separable f on ℝ2 with a coordinatewise minimum that is not a minimum; verify numerically that CD stalls there and that a diagonal step escapes.
  3. Implement SVRG. On ℓ2-regularized logistic regression, plot log(f−f⋆) vs epochs for GD, SGD, SVRG. Confirm the linear rate and locate the κ at which SVRG overtakes SGD.
  4. Prove the greedy submodular bound and implement lazy greedy (Minoux) for a facility-location objective; measure the speedup from lazy evaluation and explain why it is safe.
  5. Acceleration under noise. Add Gaussian noise of size δ to gradients and empirically compare GD and AGD error floors; verify the O(δ) vs O(kδ) prediction.
Two papers

Foundational: Robbins & Monro (1951). Modern: Johnson & Zhang (2013), Accelerating stochastic gradient descent using predictive variance reduction.
