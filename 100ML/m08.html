Module 8 · Days 66–73 · theoretical spine

Probabilistic Graphical Models

Reading: K&F Ch. 3–13 & 17–20 · PML2 Ch. 4 & 9–12 · Wainwright & Jordan. A graph is a claim about conditional independence, and every inference algorithm is a way of exploiting that claim. Read Lectures 109 and 111 first if you are taking the modules in printed order.

97

Representation I: Directed Models

Day 66 · 85 min
Theorem — factorization ⟺ local Markov

For a DAG G, p factorizes as p(x)=∏ip(xi∣xpa(i)) iff p satisfies the local Markov property Xi⊥Xnd(i)∖pa(i)∣Xpa(i).

proof sketch(⇒) Order the variables topologically; the chain rule gives p=∏p(xi∣x1:i−1), and the factorization forces each conditional to depend only on the parents, which is the local property. (⇐) Apply the local property term by term to the topologically ordered chain rule.

Theorem — soundness and completeness of d-separation

Soundness: X⊥dY∣C in G implies X⊥Y∣C in every p that factorizes over G.
Completeness: if X⊥dY∣C, then there exists a distribution factorizing over G in which they are dependent — indeed all but a measure-zero set of parameterizations. So d-separation reads off exactly the independences implied by the graph alone.

Faithfulness is the converse assumption: every independence in p is entailed by G. It fails on a measure-zero set of parameters — e.g. two paths whose effects exactly cancel — but the set of nearly unfaithful distributions is not small, which is the practical problem for causal discovery (Lecture 143).

Markov blanket of Xi: parents, children, and children's other parents. Conditional on it, Xi is independent of everything else — this is exactly the set needed for a Gibbs update (Lecture 101).

I-maps and equivalence. G is an I-map of p if every d-separation holds in p; minimal I-maps depend on the variable ordering. A perfect map captures all and only p's independences; some distributions have none (the classic: a symmetric 4-cycle MRF has no DAG perfect map, and a v-structure has no undirected one). Two DAGs are Markov equivalent iff they share a skeleton and the same v-structures (Verma–Pearl); the equivalence class is represented by a CPDAG. Observational data can never distinguish within a class — the ceiling on causal discovery.

Representation II: Undirected Models

Day 67 · 85 min

An MRF factorizes as p(x)=1Z∏c∈𝒞ψc(xc) over cliques. Three Markov properties: pairwise (Xi⊥Xj∣ rest, for non-adjacent i,j), local (Xi⊥ rest ∣ neighbours), global (separation in the graph implies independence). In general global ⇒ local ⇒ pairwise, and the converses need positivity.

Theorem — Hammersley–Clifford

If p(x)>0 for all x, then p satisfies the pairwise Markov property w.r.t. G iff it factorizes as a Gibbs distribution over the cliques of G.

Positivity is not decorative. Moussouris' counterexample: on a 4-cycle with binary variables, put uniform mass on the 8 configurations that are "consistent" and zero elsewhere. The distribution satisfies the global Markov property but does not factorize over the 4-cycle's cliques (which are just the edges). Any deterministic constraint in your model breaks Hammersley–Clifford, which matters for constraint-satisfaction-style models.

Factor graphs are the honest representation: bipartite, with variable nodes and factor nodes. They distinguish ψ123(x1,x2,x3) from ψ12ψ13ψ23, which an undirected graph cannot. All message-passing algorithms are cleanest on factor graphs.

Log-linear form. p(x)∝exp{∑kθkfk(x)} — an exponential family (Lecture 12) with A(θ)=logZ(θ). Ising (f=xixj, x∈{±1}), Potts (multiclass), Gaussian MRF (Θ=Σ−1 with zeros off the graph — Lecture 54). CRFs condition on x: p(y∣x)∝exp{∑kθkfk(yc,x)}, so Z(x) depends on the input; features may use the whole of x without modelling it, which is the discriminative advantage.

Expressiveness gap in both directions. A v-structure X→Z←Y has no undirected perfect map (moralizing adds an edge X−Y, destroying the marginal independence). The 4-cycle MRF has no directed perfect map. Chain graphs combine both, with a block-recursive semantics; they are the right formalism when some relations are causal and others symmetric, and they are much less used than they should be.

Exact Inference I: Variable Elimination

Day 68 · 85 min

Queries: marginal p(xQ), conditional p(xQ∣xE), MAP argmaxxp(x), marginal-MAP argmaxxA∑xBp. They are not equally hard: marginal-MAP is NPPP-complete and can be hard even on trees.

Variable elimination

To compute ∑x−Q∏cψc, pick an elimination order; for each variable, multiply all factors mentioning it into one, then sum it out, producing a new factor over its neighbours. Cost is O(∑i|scope(τi)|), exponential in the largest intermediate scope.

Induced graph and width. Running VE with order ≺ induces fill-in edges connecting all neighbours of each eliminated variable; the induced width is the largest clique in the induced graph minus one. Cost is O(nrw+1) for r-ary variables. Treewidth is the minimum induced width over all orders.

Theorem — exact inference is exponential only in treewidth

VE with an optimal order runs in O(nrw⋆+1). Finding the optimal order is NP-hard, but good heuristics exist (min-fill, min-degree, and for structured graphs, nested dissection). For a k×k grid, w⋆=k — so inference on a 100×100 Ising model costs 2100 and is hopeless exactly.

Hardness. Exact inference in Bayesian networks is NP-hard (reduce from 3-SAT: build a network whose probability of a designated event is positive iff the formula is satisfiable); computing the exact marginal is #P-hard (counting satisfying assignments). Worse, approximate inference to within a multiplicative factor is also NP-hard in general (Dagum–Luby) — though additive approximation is achievable under bounded conditionals. So the approximate methods of Lectures 101–104 are not merely convenient; there is no alternative.

Exact Inference II: Clique Trees and Belief Propagation

Day 68 · 85 min

VE recomputes work for each query. A clique tree caches it: one calibration pass gives all marginals.

Clique tree / junction tree

A tree whose nodes are clusters Ci⊆𝒳 containing every factor's scope, satisfying the running intersection property: if X∈Ci∩Cj, then X is in every cluster on the path between them. Construction: moralize (for DAGs), triangulate, take maximal cliques, and build a maximum-spanning tree on the clique graph weighted by separator sizes.

Sum-product

δi→j(Sij)=∑Ci∖Sijψi∏k∈N(i)∖jδk→i. Two passes (leaves-to-root, root-to-leaves) calibrate the tree: βi=ψi∏kδk→i is then the unnormalized marginal of Ci, and adjacent clusters agree on their separator — the clique tree invariant p(x)∝∏iβi∏(i,j)μij.

correctness on treesInduction on the tree: the message from a subtree is exactly the sum over that subtree's variables of its factors, by the running intersection property (which guarantees the subtrees interact only through the separator). Replacing ∑ by max gives max-product, and correctness follows by the same induction with the distributive law max(ab,ac)=amax(b,c) for a≥0.

Max-product / Viterbi gives the MAP value; recovering the assignment requires back-pointers or a traceback, and in the presence of ties, a consistent tie-breaking rule — otherwise you can assemble locally-optimal choices into a globally infeasible configuration.

Cost is exponential in the largest clique, i.e. in treewidth again. VE and clique trees are the same computation; the tree is VE with memoization and a schedule.

Approximate Inference I: Monte Carlo Foundations

Day 69 · 85 min

Rejection sampling needs q with p≤Mq and accepts with probability 1/M. In d dimensions the optimal M for a Gaussian proposal covering a Gaussian target with slightly larger variance grows exponentially in d — rejection sampling is unusable past a handful of dimensions.

Importance sampling

𝔼p[f]=𝔼q[fw], w=p/q. Variance Varq(fw) is minimized by q⋆∝|f|p; with q far from p the weights degenerate. Effective sample size neff=(∑wi)2∑wi2, and Var(w)=χ2(p‖q) — the same quantity that governs covariate shift in Lecture 39 and IPW in Lecture 140. Self-normalized IS (μˆ=∑wifi/∑wi) needs only unnormalized densities and is consistent but biased at O(1/n).

Markov chain basics

A chain with transition T is irreducible (all states reachable), aperiodic, and has π as stationary if πT=π. Under irreducibility + aperiodicity + positive recurrence, the ergodic theorem gives 1N∑tf(Xt)→𝔼πf a.s. from any start. Detailed balance π(x)T(x→y)=π(y)T(y→x) is sufficient (not necessary) for stationarity.

Metropolis–Hastings

Propose y∼q(⋅∣x), accept with α=min(1,p(y)q(x∣y)p(x)q(y∣x)). Detailed balance holds by construction, and only ratios of p are needed — the normalizing constant cancels. Gibbs is MH with q=p(xi∣x−i), for which α≡1; the update needs only the Markov blanket.

Gibbs sampling an Ising model — watch mixing collapse at the critical temperature

Blocking and collapsing. Updating correlated variables jointly (blocked Gibbs) or integrating some out analytically (collapsed Gibbs, i.e. Rao–Blackwellization) both reduce variance — the second provably so, by Lecture 18's theorem. Collapsed Gibbs for LDA and DP mixtures (Lectures 114, 127) is the canonical example.

Diagnostics. Spectral gap 1−|λ2| controls mixing time tmix≍1/(1−|λ2|); in practice use integrated autocorrelation time τ=1+2∑kρk and ESS=N/τ, plus Rˆ across chains (Lecture 117). No diagnostic can prove convergence — they can only fail to detect non-convergence, and multimodal targets defeat all of them.

Approximate Inference II: Modern MCMC

Day 70 · 85 min

Why random walks fail in high dimension. A random-walk proposal of scale ϵ must have ϵ≍d−1/2 to keep the acceptance rate from vanishing, so the chain diffuses a distance ϵ√T and needs T≍d2 steps to traverse the typical set. The optimal RW acceptance rate is 0.234 (Roberts–Gelman–Gilks).

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

Augment with momentum r∼N(0,M) and define H(x,r)=−logp(x)+12r⊤M−1r. Simulate Hamiltonian dynamics with the leapfrog integrator r←r−ϵ2∇U(x),x←x+ϵM−1r,r←r−ϵ2∇U(x), which is volume-preserving (a shear composition, Jacobian 1) and reversible (negate r), so the MH acceptance is min(1,e−ΔH) and the discretization error is the only source of rejection.

Because trajectories move coherently rather than diffusively, HMC needs O(d1/4) steps where RW needs O(d2), with optimal acceptance 0.65. Tuning: ϵ by dual averaging to hit a target acceptance; trajectory length by NUTS, which doubles the trajectory until it starts to double back (ddt‖x+−x−‖2<0) using a recursive, reversible doubling scheme that preserves detailed balance. Riemannian HMC uses a position-dependent M(x) (e.g. the Fisher metric, Lecture 70) to handle varying curvature, at high cost.

Where HMC breaks

Multimodality: HMC follows energy contours and does not cross barriers any better than RW — parallel tempering or mode-jumping proposals are needed. Discrete variables: no gradient, so they must be marginalized (Stan's approach) or handled by a separate Gibbs kernel. Funnel geometries (hierarchical models, Lecture 112): the required step size varies over orders of magnitude, producing divergent transitions; the fix is reparameterization, not more samples.

Approximate Inference III: Variational Methods

Day 70 · 85 min
The ELBO, two derivations

(1) Jensen: logp(x)=log∫qp(x,z)q≥𝔼qlogp(x,z)q=ℒ(q).
(2) Exact decomposition: logp(x)=ℒ(q)+KL(q‖p(z∣x)). The second is more useful: it shows the gap is exactly a KL, so maximizing the ELBO is minimizing KL(q‖p(⋅∣x)), and the ELBO is a bound on the evidence whose tightness is the approximation error.

Mean field. q(z)=∏jqj(zj). Coordinate ascent gives the closed-form update logqj⋆(zj)=𝔼q−j[logp(x,z)]+const, and for conditionally conjugate models this is a member of the same exponential family with natural parameter 𝔼q−j[ηj(x,z−j)] — the update is: take the Gibbs conditional and replace the conditioning values by their expectations. That one sentence generates every CAVI update you will derive.

Reverse KL is mode-seeking

KL(q‖p)=∫qlogqp is infinite wherever q>0 and p=0, so q must be contained in p's support: it under-covers, picking one mode and underestimating variance. KL(p‖q) (used by EP) is mass-covering and over-disperses. Neither is "correct" — they answer different questions, and mean-field's variance underestimation is a systematic, quantifiable defect, not noise.

Beyond mean field. Structured VI keeps some dependencies (e.g. a chain within q); mixture variational families; normalizing flows for expressive q. SVI (Hoffman et al.) uses natural gradients on the global parameters with minibatches — natural because the Fisher metric of the variational family makes the natural gradient of the ELBO have a strikingly simple form (the difference between the current and the "intermediate" natural parameters).

Black-box VI: two gradient estimators

Score function / REINFORCE: ∇ϕℒ=𝔼q[∇ϕlogqϕ(z)(logp(x,z)−logqϕ(z))]. Works for any q including discrete, but has high variance; needs control variates and Rao–Blackwellization.
Reparameterization: z=gϕ(ϵ), ∇ϕℒ=𝔼ϵ[∇ϕlogp(x,gϕ(ϵ))qϕ(gϕ(ϵ))]. Much lower variance because it differentiates through the sample, but requires a continuous, differentiable reparameterization.

EP minimizes the forward KL locally: iteratively remove a site from the approximation, compute the tilted distribution, moment-match, and reinsert. Usually more accurate than mean field for GP classification (Lecture 82); no convergence guarantee and no bound on the evidence.

Approximate Inference IV: Loopy BP, MAP and Relaxations

Day 71 · 85 min

Run sum-product on a graph with cycles and see what happens. Sometimes it is excellent (turbo codes, LDPC), sometimes it oscillates, and there is a precise account of why.

Theorem — BP fixed points are Bethe stationary points (Yedidia, Freeman & Weiss)

The Bethe free energy FBethe(b)=∑a∑xabalogbaψa−∑i(di−1)∑xibilogbi has stationary points (subject to local consistency) in exact correspondence with loopy BP fixed points. On a tree, FBethe is exact and convex, which recovers Lecture 100.

So loopy BP is a variational method with an outer approximation: it optimizes over the local polytope 𝕃 (pseudo-marginals that are pairwise consistent) rather than the marginal polytope M, using a non-convex, non-exact entropy approximation. Non-convexity of FBethe is why BP can have multiple fixed points and oscillate. TRW-BP replaces it with a provably convex surrogate built from a distribution over spanning trees, guaranteeing a unique fixed point and an upper bound on logZ. Generalized BP / Kikuchi uses larger regions, trading cost for accuracy.

MAP as an integer program

maxx∑aθa(xa) is an ILP over the marginal polytope; relaxing to 𝕃 gives an LP. The relaxation is tight when the LP optimum is integral, which happens for trees, for balanced/binary submodular models, and (empirically) often in practice. Dual decomposition splits the problem into trees, solves each exactly, and coordinates with subgradient updates on the Lagrange multipliers — giving both a solution and a certificate (the duality gap) when it is optimal.

Graph cuts

For binary pairwise MRFs with submodular energies (θij(0,0)+θij(1,1)≤θij(0,1)+θij(1,0)), exact MAP reduces to min-cut/max-flow and is solvable in polynomial time. Multi-label extensions (α-expansion, αβ-swap) give constant-factor guarantees for metric/semi-metric pairwise costs. This is the connection to Lecture 72's submodularity — the tractable island inside an NP-hard problem.

Learning Graphical Models

Day 72 · 85 min

Directed, complete data: easy. The log-likelihood decomposes over families, so the MLE is per-node: θˆxi∣u=N(xi,u)/N(u) for tabular CPDs. Decomposability is what makes both parameter estimation and score-based structure search feasible.

Undirected: hard. ∇θℓ=𝔼data[f]−𝔼pθ[f] — moment matching (Lecture 12), but the second expectation requires inference at every gradient step, which is #P-hard. Hence the family of surrogates:

Structure learning

Score-based. Maximize BIC or BDe over DAGs. The score decomposes over families, so local search (add/delete/reverse an edge) needs only local rescoring. The space is super-exponential; GES searches over equivalence classes and is consistent under faithfulness. Exact solutions via dynamic programming or ILP are feasible to ≈30 nodes.

Constraint-based. Run conditional independence tests and orient by v-structures — the PC algorithm (Lecture 143).

ℓ1-regularized. For Gaussians, graphical lasso / neighbourhood selection with the guarantees of Lecture 54. For Ising models, ℓ1-regularized logistic regression of each node on the rest recovers the neighbourhood with n=Ω(d3logp) samples under an incoherence condition (Ravikumar–Wainwright–Lafferty); Bresler's greedy algorithm removes the incoherence assumption at a worse dependence on degree.

Temporal and State-Space Models

Day 72 · 85 min

HMM's three problems. (1) Likelihood p(x1:T) — forward recursion αt(j)=bj(xt)∑iαt−1(i)aij, with scaling at each step or you underflow within ~100 steps. (2) Decoding — Viterbi, max-product with back-pointers, in log space. (3) Learning — Baum–Welch, i.e. EM with E-step γt(i)∝αt(i)βt(i) and ξt(i,j)∝αt(i)aijbj(xt+1)βt+1(j); the M-step is closed-form counting.

Identifiability. HMM parameters are identifiable only up to permutation of the states (label switching), and only if the emission distributions are distinct and the transition matrix is ergodic. Averaging MCMC draws of state-specific parameters across iterations is therefore meaningless without relabelling.

Kalman filter, two derivations

Bayesian: predict zˆt|t−1=Azˆt−1, Pt|t−1=APt−1A⊤+Q; update with the Gaussian conditioning formula, giving gain K=Pt|t−1C⊤(CPt|t−1C⊤+R)−1, zˆt=zˆt|t−1+K(xt−Czˆt|t−1), Pt=(I−KC)Pt|t−1.
Least squares: the same recursion is recursive weighted least squares with a forgetting structure. The innovation xt−Czˆt|t−1 is white under a correct model — the basis of model checking for state-space models.

RTS smoother is the backward pass. The information filter propagates P−1 and P−1zˆ instead — cheap updates, expensive predictions, and it handles a diffuse (infinite-variance) prior gracefully. EKF linearizes around the current estimate and is famously inconsistent for strongly nonlinear systems (it can converge confidently to the wrong state); UKF propagates sigma points through the true nonlinearity and captures the mean and covariance to second order, usually better and no more expensive. Switching state-space models are exactly intractable: the posterior over continuous states is a mixture with KT components, so all methods (GPB, IMM, variational, particle) are approximations that collapse the mixture.

Structured Prediction

Day 73 · 85 min

Predict y from an exponentially large structured space (sequences, trees, matchings, segmentations) with a scoring function s(x,y)=w⊤ψ(x,y) and yˆ=argmaxys(x,y). Everything hinges on being able to do that argmax.

Margin vs slack rescaling. Margin rescaling requires Δ to decompose the same way ψ does, so that loss-augmented inference stays tractable — Hamming loss on a chain does; F1 does not. Slack rescaling (Δ⋅[1+w⊤Δψ]) is tighter but its loss-augmented inference is intractable for most Δ. This is the practical reason margin rescaling dominates.

Cutting planes. Exponentially many constraints, but the most-violated one is found by a single loss-augmented inference call; adding constraints until the violation is ≤ϵ terminates in O(1/ϵ2) iterations independent of |𝒴| (Joachims et al.). Stochastic subgradient (Pegasos-style) is simpler and usually as good.

CRF gradient as moment matching. ∇wlogZ(x)=𝔼pw[ψ(x,Y)] — Lecture 12 again. So CRF training needs marginals (harder than argmax) at every step, which is why CRFs are trained on chains and trees and rarely on general graphs.

Energy-based models drop the normalization entirely and train with contrastive objectives (Lecture 105's toolbox). Their difficulty is exactly the one identified there: without a normalizer, "increase the score of the data" must be paired with "decrease it somewhere else", and choosing that somewhere is the whole problem.

Recitation 8: Graphical Models Problem Set

Day 73 · 60 min
  1. Read independences off a graph. For a supplied 8-node DAG, list all d-separations involving a designated pair, and prove two of them from the factorization directly.
  2. No perfect map. Construct a distribution with no DAG perfect map and one with no MRF perfect map, and explain which independence each formalism cannot express.
  3. VE by hand. Eliminate in two different orders on a 6-node network; compute the induced width for each and verify the cost ratio empirically.
  4. Mean-field updates. Derive CAVI for a Gaussian mixture with conjugate priors from the single rule logqj⋆=𝔼−jlogp(x,z), and identify where the variance underestimation enters.
  5. Ising Gibbs. Implement the demo above; measure the integrated autocorrelation time of the magnetization as a function of β and locate the critical slowing-down; then implement Swendsen–Wang and show it does not slow down.
Two papers

Foundational: Pearl (1988), Ch. 3–4. Modern: Hoffman & Gelman (2014), The No-U-Turn Sampler — reconstruct why the doubling scheme preserves detailed balance; it is the subtle part.
