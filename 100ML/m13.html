The Theory of Model Evaluation

Day 97 · 85 min
Optimism and the covariance penalty

For fixed inputs and yˆ=Sy, the expected optimism of the training error is 𝔼[Errin]−𝔼[err]=2n∑iCov(yˆi,yi)=2σ2ntr(S). Hence Mallows' Cp=err+2dσˆ2n, and for likelihood losses AIC=−2ℓ+2d. Optimism is exactly "how much does the fit chase its own noise", and tr(S) measures it — the same effective degrees of freedom as Lectures 44, 86 and 88.

What does cross-validation estimate? Not the risk of the model you fitted. K-fold CV is a nearly unbiased estimate of 𝔼S′[Err(S′)] for training sets of size n(1−1/K) — the expected error over training sets, not the conditional error Err(S) of your particular fit. Bates, Hastie & Tibshirani show CV is essentially uncorrelated with the conditional error it is usually reported as estimating. So a CV number is a statement about your procedure, not about your model.

  • Bias: decreases with K (larger training folds). LOOCV is nearly unbiased for Errn; 5- and 10-fold are pessimistic by an amount that matters on steep learning curves.
  • Variance: LOOCV's is often high because the n fits are highly correlated and the estimate is an average of n nearly identical, unstable quantities. The bias–variance sweet spot is empirically K∈{5,10}.
  • No unbiased variance estimator. Bengio & Grandvalet: there is no universal unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold CV. The folds are dependent, and the dependence structure is not determined by n and K alone. Every "CV standard error" you have seen is an approximation with no guarantee, and comparing two models by "CV mean ± SE" is not a valid test.

Nested CV is required whenever hyperparameters are tuned: the inner loop selects, the outer loop estimates. Reporting the best inner-loop score as performance is selection bias, and the magnitude is often larger than the differences being claimed. Cost is KoutKin fits — expensive and non-negotiable.

Bootstrap estimators. The naive bootstrap is optimistic (≈63.2% of points appear in each resample). Leave-one-out bootstrap over-corrects; the .632 estimator mixes 0.368err+0.632Errˆ(1), and .632+ adjusts the weight by the relative overfitting rate. Both assume a stable no-information error rate and degrade for interpolating classifiers, where err=0 by construction.

Theorem — you cannot have both (Yang, 2005)

No model-selection procedure is simultaneously consistent (selects the true model with probability →1 when it exists) and minimax-rate optimal for prediction when it does not. BIC-type criteria achieve the first; AIC/CV-type criteria achieve the second. Choose your loss before choosing your criterion.

Proper Scoring Rules and Calibration

Day 97 · 85 min
Propriety

A scoring rule S(p,y) is proper if 𝔼y∼qS(q,y)≤𝔼y∼qS(p,y) for all p — honesty is optimal — and strictly proper if equality forces p=q. Brier ‖p−ey‖2 and log score −logpy are strictly proper; accuracy and AUC are not proper at all, which is why optimizing them gives no reason to expect calibrated probabilities.

Savage representation and the decomposition

Every proper scoring rule has the form S(p,y)=−G(p)−⟨G′(p),ey−p⟩ for a convex G (the associated entropy); strict propriety corresponds to strict convexity. And every proper score decomposes as 𝔼[S]=𝔼d(p,p)calibration(reliability)−(G(pmarg)−𝔼G(p))refinement(resolution)+G(pmarg)uncertainty, where p=𝔼[Y∣pˆ]. Calibration and sharpness are different axes: the constant forecast pˆ≡π is perfectly calibrated and useless. The operational goal (Gneiting) is maximize sharpness subject to calibration.

Kinds of calibration

  • Marginal / average: 𝔼[Y∣pˆ=p]=p. What reliability diagrams display.
  • Conditional (individual): 𝔼[Y∣X=x]=pˆ(x) — equivalent to being correct. Untestable from finite data with continuous X: any finite sample sees each x once.
  • Group / multicalibration (Hébert-Johnson et al.): calibrated simultaneously on every set in a rich family 𝒞. Achievable with sample complexity depending on 𝒞's capacity — the useful middle ground between the two above, and the right target when fairness across subgroups matters.
ECE is biased, and the bias favours you

Binned ECE =∑bnbn|accb−confb| systematically underestimates true calibration error, because within-bin averaging cancels opposing deviations, and the bias grows as bins get wider. It is also not comparable across binning schemes, and it can be reduced simply by choosing fewer bins. Use adaptive/equal-mass binning, report the debiased estimator, or use a binning-free measure (kernel calibration error, or the Kolmogorov–Smirnov-type calibration test). Comparing two methods on ECE with different bin counts is meaningless.

Under shift, calibration is not preserved. A model calibrated on the source is generally miscalibrated on the target, and temperature fitted on validation data does not transfer (Ovadia et al. document this across many models and shift types). Recalibration must be redone on target-domain data, or made shift-robust by importance-weighted calibration.

Conformal Prediction

Day 98 · 85 min
Theorem — split conformal coverage

Let (Xi,Yi)i=1n and (Xn+1,Yn+1) be exchangeable. Fit fˆ on a proper training set; on a disjoint calibration set of size n compute scores si=s(Xi,Yi) (e.g. |Yi−fˆ(Xi)|), let qˆ be the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile, and set C(x)={y:s(x,y)≤qˆ}. Then 1−α≤ℙ(Yn+1∈C(Xn+1))≤1−α+1n+1.

proofBy exchangeability, the rank of sn+1 among s1,…,sn+1 is uniform on {1,…,n+1}. The event {sn+1≤qˆ} is the event that the rank is at most ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉, which has probability ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/(n+1)≥1−α. The upper bound holds when scores are almost surely distinct.

Read what is not assumed: no model correctness, no distributional form, no asymptotics, no independence beyond exchangeability. The model can be arbitrarily bad — then the intervals are wide, but they still cover. This is the only distribution-free finite-sample guarantee in the whole series.

Theorem — no distribution-free conditional coverage (Vovk; Barber, Candès, Ramdas & Tibshirani)

Any procedure with ℙ(Y∈C(X)∣X=x)≥1−α for all x and all distributions with continuous X must produce intervals of infinite expected length. Conditional coverage is not merely hard; it is impossible for free.

The achievable relaxations. Group-conditional (Mondrian conformal): calibrate separately within each of finitely many groups — exact coverage per group, at a cost in calibration sample. Approximate conditional: localized weighting or conditional-quantile scores get closer without a guarantee. Cluster-conditional for many-class problems.

Beyond exchangeability. Under covariate shift with known likelihood ratio w(x)=pte(x)/ptr(x), weighted conformal restores exact coverage by weighting calibration points by w (Tibshirani et al.). Under general distribution drift, coverage degrades by a total-variation term; adaptive conformal (ACI) updates αt online with a gradient step and guarantees long-run empirical coverage without any distributional assumption at all.

Conformal risk control (Angelopoulos et al.) extends the guarantee from coverage to any monotone loss: choose a threshold so that 𝔼[L(Cλ(X),Y)]≤α — covering false-negative rate in multilabel problems, IoU in segmentation, and FDR-type criteria.

Uncertainty, Decomposed

Day 98 · 75 min
Aleatoric vs epistemic, and why the split is model-relative

H(𝔼θ[p(y∣x,θ)])total=𝔼θ[H(p(y∣x,θ))]aleatoric+I(y;θ∣x)epistemic(BALD). Epistemic uncertainty is reducible by data; aleatoric is not. But the split depends entirely on the model class: noise that one model calls irreducible is signal to a richer model. There is no model-free decomposition, and claims of "measuring aleatoric uncertainty" are always conditional on a hypothesis space.

IntervalCoversWidth as n→∞
Confidence intervalA parameter, over repeated sampling→0
Credible intervalA parameter, given the data and prior→0
Prediction intervalA single future observation→ the noise level, not 0
Tolerance intervalA stated proportion of the population, with confidence→ a population quantile range

Confusing the first with the third is the single most common uncertainty error in applied work; it produces intervals that are too narrow by a factor that grows with n.

Ensembles. Deep ensembles are the strongest practical baseline for epistemic uncertainty and consistently beat single-model Bayesian approximations on shift benchmarks. What they measure, though, is variability under retraining — initialization and data-order randomness — which is a proxy for, not identical to, posterior uncertainty. Members trained on the same data with the same architecture share the same blind spots, so ensemble disagreement systematically underestimates uncertainty about model misspecification.

Jackknife+ (Barber, Candès, Ramdas & Tibshirani 2021)

Using leave-one-out fits fˆ−i and residuals Ri=|yi−fˆ−i(xi)|, the interval [q−α{fˆ−i(x)−Ri},q+1−α{fˆ−i(x)+Ri}] has coverage ≥1−2α with no assumption beyond exchangeability, and ≥1−α under an algorithmic stability condition. The naive jackknife has no such guarantee. CV+ is the K-fold analogue with the same 1−2α bound at K-fold cost.

Bayesian predictive uncertainty under misspecification is miscalibrated in a specific way: the posterior contracts at rate 1/n around the KL projection regardless of whether the model is right, so credible intervals become confidently wrong. Remedies: power likelihoods with a learning rate (SafeBayes), model expansion (Lecture 117), or wrapping the whole procedure in conformal prediction, which restores marginal validity whatever the model believes.

OOD detection is either density-ratio estimation (pin/pout, but pout is undefined) or one-class estimation (a level set of pin). Both are fundamentally hard: Nalisnick et al. showed deep generative models assign higher likelihood to some OOD data than to in-distribution data, because likelihood in high dimension is dominated by typicality volume rather than by proximity. Typicality tests and likelihood ratios against a background model are partial fixes; there is no general solution, and detection performance should always be reported against a stated OOD distribution rather than claimed in the abstract.

Distribution Shift in Theory and Practice

Day 99 · 85 min

The taxonomy of Lecture 39, now with estimators and detection.

Label shift and BBSE

Under label shift (p(x∣y) fixed, p(y) changes), with fˆ any fixed predictor and C its source confusion matrix Cij=ptr(fˆ=i,y=j): w=C−1μte,μte,i=pte(fˆ=i), where wj=pte(y=j)/ptr(y=j). Identifiability requires C invertible — the predictor must be informative about y. This is black-box shift estimation (Lipton, Wang & Smola); MLLS is the EM-based refinement (Saerens–Latinne–Decaestecker), consistent when the source classifier is calibrated.

Importance weighting under covariate shift. Unbiased, and neff=(∑w)2/∑w2=n/(1+χ2(pte‖ptr)). Practical rule: if maxiwi/∑iwi>0.05, a single point dominates and no asymptotics apply. Estimate w by a probabilistic classifier discriminating source from target — w(x)=p(target∣x)1−p(target∣x)⋅ntrnte — and clip.

Under covariate shift with overlap, importance-weighted estimates of the target risk are consistent. Under concept shift (p(y∣x) changes), target performance is not identifiable from unlabelled target data — any p(y∣x) is compatible with the observed x's. Methods that claim label-free accuracy estimation (average thresholded confidence, difference-of-confidences, ATC) are implicitly assuming no concept shift; they work when that holds and fail silently when it does not. Monitoring design follows: track input shift as an early warning, track model-output shift as the actionable signal, and buy a small labelled sample from the target — there is no substitute.

Fairness: Formal Criteria and Impossibility

Day 99 · 85 min
CriterionFormal statementFamily
Statistical / demographic parityYˆ⊥AIndependence
Equalized oddsYˆ⊥A∣YSeparation
Equal opportunityP(Yˆ=1∣Y=1,A=a) equalSeparation (positives only)
Predictive parity / calibration within groupsY⊥A∣YˆSufficiency
Theorem — impossibility (Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan; Chouldechova)

If base rates differ across groups (P(Y=1∣A=a)≠P(Y=1∣A=b)) and the classifier is not perfect, then calibration within groups and equalized false-positive and false-negative rates cannot all hold. Any two of the three are attainable; all three are not.

proof (Chouldechova's version)For a binary classifier with prevalence p, PPV v, FPR f and FNR q, algebra gives f=p1−p⋅1−vv(1−q). Fix v (predictive parity) and let p differ across groups: f and q cannot both be equal. The identity is a rearrangement of the confusion matrix — the impossibility is arithmetic, not statistical.

What follows. The choice among criteria is normative and cannot be settled by data. Independence ignores Y entirely and can be satisfied by choosing the least-qualified members of one group; separation licenses different thresholds; sufficiency is what a well-calibrated risk score naturally satisfies. Barocas–Hardt–Narayanan's framing — independence, separation, sufficiency, pairwise incompatible under mild conditions — is the clearest available map.

Individual fairness ("treat similar individuals similarly", Dwork et al.) shifts the entire difficulty into specifying the similarity metric, which is precisely the contested normative question. Metric learning from human judgments is an active but unresolved line.

Achieving constraints. The reductions approach (Agarwal et al.) casts fair classification as a saddle-point problem — minimize error subject to linear fairness constraints — and solves it with a sequence of cost-sensitive classification problems via exponentiated gradient, giving a randomized classifier with explicit tradeoff curves for any base learner. Post-processing (Hardt–Price–Srebro) achieves equalized odds by group-specific (possibly randomized) thresholds on a fixed score, which is optimal given that score but cannot recover information the score discarded.

Measurement precedes all of it

Every criterion above conditions on Y — the recorded label. If Y is arrest rather than offence, re-hospitalization rather than health, or "clicked" rather than "found useful", then equalizing error rates with respect to Y equalizes performance on a biased proxy and can entrench the bias while satisfying the metric. Jacobs & Wallach's construct-validity framing is the right first question: what is the target construct, and is Y a valid measurement of it? No amount of constrained optimization repairs a construct-invalid label, and the cost of fairness constraints in accuracy is only meaningful once you believe the label.

Privacy and Robustness

Day 100 · 85 min
Differential privacy

ℳ is (ε,δ)-DP if for all neighbouring datasets D,D′ (differing in one record) and all measurable S: ℙ(ℳ(D)∈S)≤eεℙ(ℳ(D′)∈S)+δ. Pure DP is δ=0. It is a property of the algorithm, holds against arbitrary side information, and is closed under post-processing.

MechanismNoiseGuarantee

Exponential∝exp(εu(D,r)/2Δu)ε-DP for non-numeric outputs

Laplace mechanismThe density ratio at any output z is exp(|z−f(D′)|1−|z−f(D)|1Δ1/ε)≤eε by the triangle inequality. One line, and it is the whole proof.

Composition. Sequential: k mechanisms give kε. Advanced composition: (ε′,kδ+δ′) with ε′=ε√2klog(1/δ′)+kε(eε−1) — a √k rather than k growth, the difference between usable and not. Rényi DP and the moments accountant track the log-MGF of the privacy loss random variable, composing additively and converting to (ε,δ) at the end; this is what makes DP-SGD's accounting tight enough to train real models.

DP-SGD: clip per-example gradients to norm C, add N(0,σ2C2I), account with the moments accountant. Subsampling amplifies privacy (each example participates rarely). The costs are real and worth stating: utility loss concentrated on under-represented subgroups (Bagdasaryan et al.), compute overhead from per-example gradients, and hyperparameter sensitivity. For ERM the theory gives excess risk O˜(√d/(nε)) for convex losses, matching lower bounds.

Attacks calibrate the abstraction. Membership inference (Shokri et al.; the likelihood-ratio attack LiRA is the strong version) tests whether a record was in the training set; reconstruction attacks recover training examples from generative and even discriminative models; and the fundamental law of information recovery says overly accurate answers to too many queries destroy privacy entirely. The stability–privacy–generalization triangle: DP implies stability (Lecture 34) implies generalization — which is the basis of the reusable holdout in the next lecture.

Robustness

Adversarial examples. For a linear model with ‖δ‖∞≤ϵ the worst-case perturbation shifts the margin by ϵ‖w‖1, which is exactly a certified bound — and shows the tension: ℓ∞ robustness demands small ‖w‖1, i.e. sparsity. For kernel methods, the Lipschitz constant of f in the RKHS gives a similar certificate. Robust training solves a min–max (Madry et al.), with a documented robustness–accuracy tradeoff (Tsipras et al.; Zhang et al.'s TRADES makes it explicit).

Certified robustness via randomized smoothing (Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter): define g(x)=argmaxcPη∼N(0,σ2I)(f(x+η)=c). If the top class has probability pA and the runner-up pB, then g is constant on an ℓ2 ball of radius σ2(Φ−1(pA)−Φ−1(pB)). It scales to ImageNet, gives a real certificate, and its radius degrades with dimension for ℓ∞ threats — a known and quantified limitation.

Data poisoning and the influence function. The effect of upweighting training point z on a test loss is −∇θL(ztest)⊤H−1∇θL(z) — the influence function of Lecture 24, now used both to explain predictions (Koh–Liang) and to construct poisons. The same derivative serves attack and diagnosis; that duality is worth internalizing.

The Research Process

Day 100 · 75 min
Theorem — adaptive data analysis (Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Roth)

With a holdout of size n used non-adaptively, O(en) queries can be answered to accuracy O(n−1/2). With adaptive queries — each chosen after seeing previous answers — a naive holdout can be broken with O(n) queries, and the reported accuracy becomes arbitrarily optimistic. But if answers are provided by a differentially private mechanism, O(n2) adaptive queries can be answered accurately: privacy buys generalization, via the stability argument of Lecture 34.

The reusable holdout (Thresholdout) implements this: report the training estimate unless it differs from the holdout by more than a noisy threshold, and charge a budget when it does. It is the theoretically correct way to run a leaderboard, and the practical lesson stands even without the mechanism: every look at the test set spends something.

Benchmark overfitting. The reassuring evidence: Recht et al. built new CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets and found accuracy dropped substantially but the ranking of models was preserved — the drop was attributable to distribution differences in the new set rather than adaptive overfitting. The worrying evidence: on small benchmarks with many published attempts and small margins, the effective number of hypotheses tested by the community is enormous and no one accounts for it. Both can be true; the right reading is that leaderboard ranking is more robust than leaderboard numbers.

Researcher degrees of freedom

  • The garden of forking paths (Gelman & Loken): even with no explicit p-hacking, an analysis contingent on the data — "we would have used a different outcome/subgroup/transform had the data looked different" — has an unstated multiplicity. This is not fraud, and it is not fixed by preregistering the analysis you actually ran.
  • Preregistration fits ML awkwardly: exploration is the point, and the object of study (a model) is often built during the work. What transfers: preregister the evaluation — datasets, metrics, seeds, comparison baselines, stopping rules — even if the method is developed freely.
  • Ablation ≠ hyperparameter search. An ablation removes a component with everything else held fixed and re-tunes the remainder to be fair to it. Removing a component while keeping hyperparameters tuned for the full method proves nothing (Lipton & Steinhardt's "failure to identify the sources of empirical gains"; Melis et al. found tuned LSTM baselines matched years of architectural progress).
  • Report variability. Single-seed numbers are not results. Report mean ± SD over ≥5 seeds, or a distribution; where budgets differ, report expected max performance as a function of the tuning budget (Dodge et al.). Effect sizes and CIs, not asterisks.

Two skills to leave with

Refereeing. Read the assumptions section first and ask what breaks without each. Check whether the theorem's quantity is the paper's claim (a bound on Gibbs risk is not a bound on the plug-in classifier; a rate over a class is not a statement about an instance). Check the baselines were tuned. Check the code exists. Then write the review you would want: specific, actionable, and separating "wrong" from "not what I would have done".

Choosing a thesis problem. A good problem is one where (i) you can state precisely what would count as a solution, (ii) a small version is solvable in a month, (iii) the tools you have give leverage on it, and (iv) the negative result is also publishable. Problems failing (i) consume years. Problems failing (iv) are gambles.

Capstone: A Research-Grade Project 🔴

Day 100 · 90 min · heavy day: 250 min total

One problem, carried through the full arc. The point is not the result; it is that every step is defensible.

Specification for your own

  1. Question and estimand. One sentence, with the population and the functional named. "Does method X help?" is not an estimand. "Excess risk of X relative to Y on distributions satisfying Z, at n=103" is.
  2. Assumptions. A numbered list. For each, say what is known to fail without it and how you would detect the failure in your data.
  3. A theoretical contribution. A new result, a sharpened constant, an extension to a weaker hypothesis, or a counterexample. A counterexample to a published claim is a first-class contribution and is usually the fastest route to one.
  4. Experiments that could falsify. Pre-specify what result would make you abandon the claim. If no result would, the claim is not empirical.
  5. From-scratch implementation of the core estimator, verified against a reference on a case where the answer is known analytically.
  6. Uncertainty quantified properly — using the machinery of this module, not standard errors from a misspecified model.
  7. Write-up to conference standard: claims that match theorems, baselines tuned as hard as the method, ablations that re-tune, code and seeds released, limitations that a hostile referee would have raised written by you first.
Worked demonstration (in lecture)

Conformalized CATE intervals under covariate shift: estimand τ(x) with a 1−α interval valid under weighted exchangeability; assumptions unconfoundedness + overlap + known/estimated shift ratio; theory combines Lecture 148's weighted conformal with Lecture 142's orthogonal scores; falsification by simulation where positivity is deliberately violated; implementation from scratch with cross-fitting; uncertainty reported as coverage curves against overlap. The negative finding — coverage degrades exactly where the effective sample size collapses — is the paper's most useful sentence.

Two papers

Foundational: Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer (2005). Modern: Barber, Candès, Ramdas & Tibshirani (2021), Predictive inference with the jackknife+.

End of the series. 154 lectures, and the through-line has been one question asked in fourteen dialects: what did you assume, and what happens when it is false? Everything else — the rates, the algorithms, the architectures — is downstream of that.
