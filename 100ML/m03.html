The Geometry of Least Squares

Day 29 · 85 min

Every result about OLS is a statement about one projection. Which statement, for which result?

Model y=Xβ+ε, X∈ℝn×p of full column rank. βˆ=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y, yˆ=Hy with H=X(X⊤X)−1X⊤.

Geometric factStatistical reading
H2=H=H⊤, trH=pDegrees of freedom =p; 𝔼‖yˆ−𝔼y‖2=pσ2
Hii∈[0,1], ∑Hii=pLeverage; Var(yˆi)=σ2Hii, Var(ei)=σ2(1−Hii)

‖y‖2=‖yˆ‖2+‖e‖2ANOVA decomposition; R2 is a squared cosine
Theorem — Gauss–Markov

If 𝔼ε=0 and Cov(ε)=σ2I (no normality, no independence beyond uncorrelatedness), then for any c, c⊤βˆ has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators of c⊤β.

proofLet β˜=Ay be unbiased, so AX=I. Write A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤+D with DX=0. Then Cov(β˜)=σ2[(X⊤X)−1+DD⊤]⪰Cov(βˆ).

What “best” excludes. Nonlinear estimators (the MLE under non-Gaussian noise beats OLS), and biased estimators (ridge, Lecture 44, beats it in MSE for some λ always). Gauss–Markov is an optimality statement inside a small box, and its rhetorical weight in applied writing far exceeds its content.

Theorem — Frisch–Waugh–Lovell

Partition X=[X1X2]. Then βˆ2 from the full regression equals the coefficient from regressing M1y on M1X2, where M1=I−H1. The residuals of the two regressions coincide.

Numerics. Never form X⊤X. QR via Householder gives βˆ=R−1Q⊤y with backward-stable error proportional to κ(X), while the normal equations suffer κ(X)2. On a design with κ=108 — routine with polynomial features — the normal equations return noise in double precision.

Distribution Theory for the Linear Model

Day 30 · 85 min

Where does the t-distribution actually come from?

Theorem — Cochran

If z∼N(0,In) and P1,…,Pk are symmetric idempotent with ∑Pj=I, then the quadratic forms z⊤Pjz are independent χ2rank(Pj).

Apply with P1=H, P2=I−H: βˆ and ‖e‖2 are independent, ‖e‖2/σ2∼χ2n−p, and βˆ∼N(β,σ2(X⊤X)−1). Hence tj=βˆj−βjσˆ√[(X⊤X)−1]jj∼tn−p, because a standard normal divided by an independent √χ2ν/ν is exactly tν. The independence — a purely geometric fact about orthogonal projections of a spherical Gaussian — is the whole reason the t-distribution exists.

F-tests. Comparing nested models with q restrictions: F=(RSS0−RSS1)/qRSS1/(n−p)∼Fq,n−p, again by Cochran on orthogonal complements. Geometrically it compares the squared length of the extra projection to the residual length.

Simultaneous inference. A single t interval has 1−α coverage for one contrast. For all contrasts simultaneously use Scheffé: c⊤βˆ±√pFp,n−p,ασˆ‖c‖(X⊤X)−1 — the confidence ellipsoid projected onto direction c. Bonferroni is narrower for a small pre-specified family; Scheffé wins once the family is large or chosen after looking.

Prediction vs confidence. Var(yˆ0)=σ2x0⊤(X⊤X)−1x0 for the mean; add σ2 for a new observation. The prediction interval does not shrink to zero as n→∞ — a distinction that Lecture 149 shows is routinely confused in practice.

Without normality

βˆ is still unbiased and Gauss–Markov still holds; the t and F distributions do not. Under Lindeberg–Feller conditions on the rows, √n(βˆ−β)⇒N(0,σ2Q−1) with Q=limX⊤X/n, so t-statistics are asymptotically normal — but the approximation degrades with high leverage, because a single row with Hii≈1 violates Lindeberg. High leverage is thus not merely an outlier concern; it invalidates the CLT itself.

The PRESS identity yi−yˆ(i)=ei/(1−hii) is worth memorizing: leave-one-out cross-validation for a linear smoother costs one fit, not n. It generalizes to any linear smoother yˆ=Sy with hii=Sii — used for ridge, splines, and kernel smoothers (Lectures 44, 86, 88).

Broken assumptions, in order of severity

  1. Wrong mean function. Fatal: everything else is conditional on it. Detect with residual-vs-fitted and partial residual plots, not with a test.
  2. Correlated errors. Point estimates stay unbiased; SEs can be off by orders of magnitude. Durbin–Watson for AR(1); clustered/HAC (Newey–West) SEs; GLS with Ω known, feasible GLS with Ωˆ — but FGLS can be worse than OLS+robust SEs at small n because Ωˆ is noisy.
  3. Heteroscedasticity. Unbiased, inefficient, wrong SEs. Use HC3 (not HC0) sandwich estimators — HC0 is anti-conservative at small n. Breusch–Pagan and White tests detect it, but the fix does not depend on the test.
  4. Collinearity. Not an assumption violation at all — the model is correctly specified and the estimator is BLUE. The problem is variance. Condition indices √λ1/λj>30 with two variance-decomposition proportions >0.5 on the same index localizes which variables are entangled — more informative than VIF, which is a marginal summary.
Where it breaks

Deleting flagged points and refitting produces standard errors that are wrong in an unquantified direction — the deletion rule is data-dependent and no longer part of the model. If robustness is the goal, use a robust estimator (Lecture 24) whose sampling distribution is known, rather than a diagnostic-then-delete pipeline whose sampling distribution is not.

Ridge Regression: Theory

Day 31 · 85 min

Why does a biased estimator beat an unbiased one, and by exactly how much?

Three equivalent definitions: βˆλ=argmin‖y−Xβ‖2+λ‖β‖2; the constrained problem min‖y−Xβ‖2 s.t. ‖β‖≤t; the posterior mode under β∼N(0,τ2I), λ=σ2/τ2. Solution βˆλ=(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y.

SVD analysis

With X=UΣV⊤, yˆλ=∑jσj2σj2+λujuj⊤y. Each principal direction is shrunk by σj2/(σj2+λ): directions with large variance are barely touched, low-variance directions are crushed. Effective degrees of freedom df(λ)=∑jσj2σj2+λ, decreasing from p to 0.

Theorem — existence of a beneficial λ

MSE(λ)=σ2∑jσj2(σj2+λ)2+λ2∑jαj2(σj2+λ)2 where α=V⊤β. Differentiating at λ=0 gives MSE′(0)=−2σ2∑jσj−2<0: there always exists λ>0 with strictly lower MSE than OLS, whatever β is.

The derivative is −2σ2∑σj−2, dominated by the smallest singular value — ridge helps most exactly where OLS is worst-conditioned. The catch: the optimal λ depends on the unknown β, so the theorem guarantees existence, not attainability. Cross-validation or the GCV criterion ‖y−yˆλ‖2/n(1−df(λ)/n)2 estimates it.

Regularization paths — ridge vs lasso on a correlated design

p>n. (X⊤X+λI)−1 is still invertible, so ridge is defined where OLS is not; the dual/kernel form βˆ=X⊤(XX⊤+λI)−1y costs O(n3) instead of O(p3) — this identity is exactly the representer theorem (Lecture 75). The ridgeless limit λ→0+ converges to X+y, the minimum-norm interpolator of Lecture 36.

The Lasso I: Geometry, Optimality, Algorithms

Day 32 · 85 min

Why does ℓ1 produce exact zeros and ℓ2 does not?

Two answers, both correct. Geometric: the ℓ1 ball has vertices on the axes, and the level sets of a quadratic generically first touch a convex body at an extreme point. Analytic (better): the subdifferential of |⋅| at 0 is the interval [−1,1], so a whole range of gradients is consistent with βj=0.

KKT conditions for the lasso

βˆ solves min12n‖y−Xβ‖2+λ‖β‖1 iff 1nXj⊤(y−Xβˆ)=λsign(βˆj)ifβˆj≠0,|1nXj⊤(y−Xβˆ)|≤λifβˆj=0. Consequence: βˆ=0 entirely iff λ≥λmax=1n‖X⊤y‖∞ — a free, exact starting point for any path algorithm.

Orthonormal design. If X⊤X=nI, the problem separates and βˆj=Sλ(β˜j)=sign(β˜j)(|β˜j|−λ)+ — soft thresholding of the OLS coefficients. Compare: ridge scales by 1/(1+λ); best-subset hard-thresholds. Lasso is the only one of the three that is both convex and sparse, and it pays for that with a constant λ bias on the surviving coefficients.

Algorithms

  • Coordinate descent. Update βˆj←Sλ(1nXj⊤r(−j))/1n‖Xj‖2. Converges because the objective is smooth-plus-separable-nonsmooth (Tseng): coordinatewise minima of such functions are global minima. Warm starts along a λ-grid make the whole path cheaper than one fit at a single λ.
  • LARS. The solution path λ↦βˆλ is piecewise linear with knots where the active set changes; LARS walks the knots exactly in O(p3+np2) for the full path.
  • Screening. Safe rules (SAFE, strong rules) discard coordinates whose KKT condition provably cannot be tight at the next λ; strong rules discard heuristically and are checked afterwards. This is what makes p=106 routine.
Theorem — degrees of freedom of the lasso

(Zou–Hastie–Tibshirani) df(yˆλ)=𝔼|{j:βˆj≠0}| — the expected number of selected variables, exactly, for a fixed λ.

The result is startling because model selection normally costs extra degrees of freedom; here shrinkage exactly cancels the search cost. It holds for fixed λ, not for λ chosen by cross-validation, which is why plugging a CV-selected model into an F-test is invalid (Lecture 56).

Three different goals, three different conditions. Confusing them is the standard error in applied high-dimensional work.

Theorem — fast rate under RE

Suppose λ≥2n‖X⊤ε‖∞ (which holds w.p. 1−δ with λ≍σ√2log(p/δ)/n for Gaussian noise), and the design satisfies the restricted eigenvalue condition ‖Xv‖22n≥γ‖v‖22forallv∈𝒞(S)={v:‖vSc‖1≤3‖vS‖1}. Then ‖βˆ−β‖2≤3√sλγ and 1n‖X(βˆ−β)‖2≤9sλ2γ.

proof skeletonFrom optimality, the "basic inequality" 12n‖XΔ‖2≤1nε⊤XΔ+λ(‖β‖1−‖βˆ‖1). Bound the noise term by λ2‖Δ‖1 via the event on λ; decompose ‖⋅‖1 over S and Sc and use ‖β‖1−‖βˆ‖1≤‖ΔS‖1−‖ΔSc‖1. This forces Δ into the cone 𝒞(S) — the error is automatically approximately sparse. On that cone RE converts ‖XΔ‖2/n into ‖Δ‖22, and Cauchy–Schwarz gives ‖ΔS‖1≤√s‖Δ‖2.

The logp enters only through λ — through a maximum of p Gaussians. This is why p can be exponential in n: you pay logarithmically for the search.

Theorem — irrepresentable condition (Zhao–Yu; Wainwright)

Exact sign recovery sign(βˆ)=sign(β) requires (essentially necessarily and sufficiently) ‖ΣScSΣSS−1sign(βS)‖∞≤1−η,η>0, plus βmin≳λ‖ΣSS−1‖∞ and n≳slogp.

Read the condition: no irrelevant variable may be too well predicted by the relevant ones. It is a condition on the design, is not implied by RE, is not checkable without knowing S, and fails routinely — with two correlated predictors of which one is relevant, ρ>1/2 already breaks it. Practical consequence: the lasso's selected set should be treated as a screening device, not as an estimate of the truth.

Bias, and the fixes. The lasso shrinks all surviving coefficients by ≈λ, so it cannot be simultaneously consistent for selection and efficient for estimation. Remedies: adaptive lasso (weights 1/|β˜j|γ) achieves the oracle property under weaker conditions; SCAD/MCP are nonconvex penalties whose derivative vanishes for large |β|, giving unbiased large coefficients at the cost of a nonconvex program with local optima; relaxed lasso / debiasing refits or corrects after selection. Elastic net λ(α‖β‖1+1−α2‖β‖22) restores strict convexity, yields a grouping effect for correlated predictors, and can select more than n variables.

Group lasso optimality. βˆg=0 iff ‖Xg⊤r(−g)/n‖2≤λ√pg: the ℓ2 norm of a block's correlation replaces the absolute correlation. Note the √pg scaling — without it, large groups are unfairly favoured.

Overlapping groups. The naive penalty ∑g‖βg‖2 with overlaps zeroes out the complement of a union of groups; the latent group lasso (Jacob et al.) decomposes β=∑gvg with supp(vg)⊆g and penalizes ∑‖vg‖, giving supports that are unions of groups. These are different priors and the difference matters; papers routinely use the first while describing the second.

Atomic norms — the unifying frame

Given a set of atoms 𝒜 (unit vectors for ℓ1; rank-one unit matrices for nuclear; group-supported vectors for group lasso), the gauge ‖x‖𝒜=inf{t>0:x∈tconv(𝒜)} is a norm, and the sample complexity of recovering x⋆ from m Gaussian measurements is governed by the statistical dimension δ(𝒯𝒜(x⋆)) — the Gaussian squared width of the descent cone. Recovery succeeds w.h.p. once m≳δ, and fails below it: a sharp phase transition (Amelunxen–Lotz–McCoy–Tropp). Sparse: δ≈2slog(p/s). Rank-r n1×n2: δ≈r(n1+n2−r) — exactly the parameter count.

Generalized Linear Models

Day 35 · 85 min

One template, one algorithm, a dozen models.

The GLM template

Random: yi from an exponential dispersion family, p(y;θ,ϕ)=exp{yθ−b(θ)a(ϕ)+c(y,ϕ)}, so μ=b′(θ), Var(y)=a(ϕ)b′′(θ)=a(ϕ)V(μ).
Systematic: ηi=xi⊤β. Link: g(μi)=ηi. The canonical link is g=(b′)−1, making θi=ηi.

Score equations. ∂ℓ∂β=∑i(yi−μi)a(ϕ)V(μi)g′(μi)xi. With the canonical link g′V=1 and this collapses to X⊤(y−μ)=0 — the moment-matching identity of Lecture 12, and the reason canonical links give clean, always-concave problems.

Deviance D=2{ℓsat−ℓ(βˆ)} generalizes RSS; differences of deviances between nested models are asymptotically χ2. Quasi-likelihood keeps only the mean–variance relationship Var=ϕV(μ) and solves the same estimating equation; the estimator remains consistent and asymptotically normal with a sandwich covariance, without any full distributional assumption. Overdispersion: Poisson forces Var=μ; real count data usually has more. Quasi-Poisson multiplies SEs by √ϕˆ; negative binomial changes the likelihood (Var=μ+μ2/k) and is preferable when you want a genuine predictive distribution.

Complete separation

If a hyperplane perfectly separates the classes, the logistic MLE does not exist — ‖βˆ‖→∞ while the likelihood increases. Software reports huge coefficients and huge SEs. This is the boundary case of Lecture 12: T has landed on the boundary of the mean space. Remedies: Firth's penalized likelihood (Jeffreys prior, gives finite estimates with reduced bias), or any ridge penalty. In p>n separation is generic, so unpenalized logistic regression is simply undefined there.

Modelling Beyond the Conditional Mean

Day 35 · 75 min

When is the mean the wrong summary?

Quantile regression minimizes ∑iρτ(yi−xi⊤β) with the check loss ρτ(u)=u(τ−𝟙[u<0]). It is a linear program (dual: a maximum-flow-like problem), has no likelihood, and yields √n(βˆτ−βτ)⇒N(0,τ(1−τ)D1−1D0D1−1) with D1=𝔼[fY|X(Qτ)xx⊤] — the conditional density at the quantile appears, which is why quantile SEs need bandwidth choices or a bootstrap.

  • Equivariance to monotone transforms: Qτ(h(Y)∣x)=h(Qτ(Y∣x)) for increasing h. The mean has no such property — this alone justifies quantile regression for skewed outcomes where analysts otherwise log-transform and then cannot back-transform the mean.
  • Crossing. Fitting several τ separately can produce crossing quantile curves; monotonicity must be imposed (rearrangement, Chernozhukov et al.) or estimated jointly.
  • Expectiles replace the check loss with asymmetric squared loss; they are smooth, have closed-form estimating equations, and are elicitable together with the mean — but they lack the quantile's direct probabilistic interpretation.
  • Distributional regression (GAMLSS) models every parameter of a chosen family as a smooth function of covariates. Powerful, and easy to overfit: with location, scale, skew and kurtosis all covariate-dependent, effective degrees of freedom explode.

Modelling the whole conditional distribution beats modelling the mean whenever the decision is not squared-error (Lecture 17), whenever the loss is asymmetric (inventory, staffing), whenever tails are the object of interest (risk), and whenever you intend to produce prediction intervals — for which conformalized quantile regression (Lecture 148) is the current best practice.

Generative Linear Classifiers

Day 36 · 85 min

LDA and logistic regression have the same functional form. Why do their coefficients differ, and which should you use?

LDA. Assume X∣Y=k∼N(μk,Σ) with shared Σ, priors πk. Then logP(Y=1∣x)P(Y=0∣x)=logπ1π0−12(μ1+μ0)⊤Σ−1(μ1−μ0)β0+x⊤Σ−1(μ1−μ0)β. The quadratic terms cancel because Σ is shared; drop that assumption and you get QDA with a quadratic boundary and Kp(p+1)/2 covariance parameters instead of p(p+1)/2.

The exact relationship to logistic regression

Both models posit logitP(Y=1∣x)=β0+x⊤β. They differ in what is fitted: LDA maximizes the joint likelihood ∏p(xi,yi); logistic regression maximizes the conditional ∏p(yi∣xi) and leaves p(x) unmodelled. So LDA uses the marginal distribution of X as extra information — an advantage if the Gaussian assumption holds, a liability otherwise.

Ng & Jordan. The generative classifier (naive Bayes) converges to its asymptotic error at rate O(√logp/n) but that limit is biased; the discriminative classifier (logistic regression) has a O(√p/n) rate to a lower limit. Hence a crossover: generative wins for small n, discriminative wins asymptotically. The crossover point scales roughly like n≍p. This is the cleanest available statement of the “bias of a simpler model can beat the variance of a better one” tradeoff, and it is empirical as well as theoretical.

Regularized and sparse variants. RDA shrinks Σˆk toward the pooled Σˆ and toward σ2I (two mixing parameters). In p>n, Σˆ is singular and plain LDA is undefined; the "independence rule" (diagonal Σˆ) is often better than a regularized full covariance because estimating p2/2 correlations from n samples adds more variance than the correlations remove bias (Bickel–Levina). Sparse LDA imposes ℓ1 on β directly.

Naive Bayes is LDA with a diagonal covariance restriction (for Gaussian features). Its conditional independence assumption is nearly always false, yet its classification performance is often fine — because the argmax of the posterior is robust to badly calibrated but correctly ordered scores. Its probability estimates, by contrast, are notoriously overconfident, pushed to 0 and 1 by multiplying dependent likelihood factors. Use it for ranking, never for calibrated probabilities without post-hoc correction (Lecture 147).

Recitation 3: Linear Model Problem Set

Day 36 · 60 min
  1. Gauss–Markov without normality. Prove it by the A=(X⊤X)−1X⊤+D decomposition, then find a non-Gaussian error distribution and a nonlinear estimator with lower variance than OLS (hint: Laplace errors, median regression).
  2. Lasso on an orthogonal design. Derive soft thresholding from the KKT conditions; then show that best subset selection is hard thresholding and ridge is linear scaling, and plot all three transfer functions on one axis.
  3. Irrepresentable failure. Take p=3, S={1,2}, Σ equicorrelated with ρ. Show the condition reads 2ρ/(1+ρ)≤1, i.e. it fails for ρ>1… then redo it with sign(β1)=−sign(β2) and find the exact threshold. Explain why the sign pattern matters.
  4. IRLS from scratch. Implement logistic and Poisson regression via the loop in Lecture 48, verify against a reference fit to 10−8, then break it: construct a separable dataset and observe divergence; add a ridge term and watch it converge.
  5. PRESS. Prove yi−yˆ(i)=ei/(1−hii) using the Sherman–Morrison formula, and use it to compute LOOCV for ridge across a λ-grid at the cost of one SVD.
Two papers

Foundational: Tibshirani (1996). Modern: Zhao & Yu (2006) — read the counterexamples in §3, they are the real content.
