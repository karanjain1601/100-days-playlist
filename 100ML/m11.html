Online Learning, Bandits & Sequential Decisions

Reading: PLG Ch. 2–6 · Bandit Ch. 4–12 & 18–21 · Hazan · Orabona. Drop the i.i.d. assumption entirely and replace risk with regret. Remarkably, almost everything still works — and several algorithms get simpler.

The Online Learning Framework

Day 87 · 85 min
Protocol and regret

For t=1..T: the learner picks pt, the adversary reveals a loss ℓt, the learner suffers ⟨pt,ℓt⟩. Regret RT=T∑t=1⟨pt,ℓt⟩−mini∈[N]T∑t=1ℓt(i). No distributional assumption whatsoever; the comparator is the best fixed action in hindsight.

Regret is the right criterion when i.i.d. fails because it is a relative, pathwise guarantee: it does not claim the world is predictable, only that you did nearly as well as the best simple strategy on the sequence that actually occurred.

Realizable case — halving. If some expert never errs, keep the set of consistent experts and predict by majority. Each mistake halves the set, so at most log2N mistakes. Clean, and the template for everything that follows.

Hedge / exponential weights

wt(i)∝exp(−η∑s<tℓs(i)), pt=wt/‖wt‖1. With losses in [0,1] and η=√8logN/T: RT≤√T2logN.

proofTrack the potential Φt=log∑iwt(i). Upper bound one step by Hoeffding's lemma applied to the loss under pt: Φt+1−Φt≤−η⟨pt,ℓt⟩+η2/8. Sum over t, and lower bound ΦT+1≥−ηmini∑tℓt(i) by keeping one term. Rearranging gives RT≤logNη+ηT8; optimize η.

Note the mechanism: the same Hoeffding lemma as Lecture 4, applied to a weighting over experts instead of to data. Randomized weighted majority is the same algorithm read as a mistake bound.

Matching lower bound

Let each ℓt(i) be an independent fair coin. Then 𝔼[∑t⟨pt,ℓt⟩]=T/2 for any learner, while 𝔼[mini∑tℓt(i)]=T/2−Θ(√TlogN) by the expected minimum of N near-Gaussian walks. So RT=Ω(√TlogN) — Hedge is optimal.

Unknown horizon. The doubling trick restarts with η tuned for T=2k on each epoch, costing a factor √2√2−1≈3.41. Anytime step sizes ηt≍√logN/t achieve the same rate with better constants and no restarts, and are preferred in practice.

Online Convex Optimization

Day 88 · 85 min
Online gradient descent

xt+1=Π𝒦(xt−ηtgt), gt∈∂ft(xt). With ‖gt‖≤G, diam(𝒦)≤D, ηt=DG√t: RT≤32GD√T. If each ft is α-strongly convex and ηt=1/(αt): RT≤G22α(1+logT).

proof‖xt+1−u‖2≤‖xt−u‖2−2ηtgt⊤(xt−u)+ηt2G2 (projection is nonexpansive). Convexity gives ft(xt)−ft(u)≤gt⊤(xt−u). Rearrange, telescope with varying ηt.

Follow the leader fails. Playing xt=argmin∑s<tfs can incur Ω(T) regret: alternate losses ±x on [−1,1] and FTL oscillates to the wrong endpoint every round. The cure is stabilization.

xt=argminx∈𝒦{∑s<t⟨gs,x⟩+1ηψ(x)} with ψ strongly convex w.r.t. ‖⋅‖. Then RT≤ψ(u)−minψη+η∑t‖gt‖*2.

Choosing a regularizer is choosing a geometry, and the geometry sets the dimension dependence. This is the same ℓ1/ℓ∞ pairing that made sparse linear classes cheap in Lecture 31.

AdaGrad, rederived. Optimizing the FTRL bound over diagonal preconditioners gives ψt(x)=12x⊤diag(∑s≤tgs2)1/2x and regret √2D∞∑j‖g1:T,j‖2 — Lecture 69's algorithm, derived rather than invented.

Online-to-batch

If ft(x)=ℓ(x,zt) with zt i.i.d. and x=1T∑xt, then 𝔼[R(x)]−minuR(u)≤𝔼[RT]/T. So a √T regret bound gives a 1/√T generalization bound, for free and with no complexity measure. This is the cheapest route to Lecture 67's SGD rate, and it is why online learning is worth studying even if you only care about i.i.d. problems.

Extensions of the Online Framework

Day 88 · 75 min
  • Bandit convex optimization. Only ft(xt) is observed. One-point gradient estimate: sample u uniformly on the sphere, play xt+δu, and use gˆ=dδft(xt+δu)u, which is an unbiased gradient of the smoothed fˆδ. Gives O(T3/4) regret (Flaxman–Kalai–McMahan); the optimal rate is O˜(√T) (Bubeck–Eldan–Lee), achieved by far more elaborate methods.
  • Partial monitoring generalizes further: feedback is an arbitrary function of the action and outcome. Games classify into four regret regimes — trivial (0), easy (Θ(√T)), hard (Θ(T2/3)), hopeless (Θ(T)) — determined by the structure of the feedback matrix (Bartók et al.). Dynamic pricing with censored demand is the canonical T2/3 example.
  • Tracking / shifting regret. Compare against a comparator sequence with k switches: fixed-share (mix a little uniform mass into the weights each round) achieves O(√T(klogN+klogTk)). Strongly adaptive regret bounds the regret on every interval simultaneously.
  • Minimax sequential prediction. The value of the game is characterized by sequential Rademacher complexity (Rakhlin–Sridharan–Tewari), a tree-indexed analogue of Lecture 31's quantity: ℜseq(ℱ)=supx𝔼εsupf1T∑tεtf(xt(ε)), where the data are a binary tree the adversary may adapt. Learnability online requires finite Littlestone dimension, which is strictly stronger than finite VC dimension — thresholds on ℝ have VC 1 and Littlestone ∞.
  • Calibration and approachability. A forecaster is calibrated if, among rounds where it predicted p, the empirical frequency tends to p. Foster & Vohra: calibrated forecasters exist, using randomization; deterministic ones do not. The proof is Blackwell approachability — a vector-valued minimax theorem asserting that a convex set is approachable iff every halfspace containing it is. Approachability is also the general engine behind regret minimization with vector payoffs.
  • Connections. Boosting is online learning played between a booster and a weak learner (the weights are the adversary's distribution); solving a zero-sum game by having both players run no-regret algorithms converges to a Nash equilibrium at rate O(1/√T), and this is how large extensive-form games are solved in practice (CFR is regret matching applied per information set).

Stochastic Multi-Armed Bandits

Day 89 · 85 min
Setup and the decomposition lemma

K arms with means μi, optimal μ⋆, gaps Δi=μ⋆−μi. Regret RT=Tμ⋆−𝔼∑tμAt=∑iΔi𝔼[Ni(T)]. So bounding regret is bounding how often each suboptimal arm is pulled. Everything else is a concentration argument.

Explore-then-commit with m pulls per arm gives RT≤m∑Δi+T∑iΔie−mΔi2/4, optimized at m≈4Δ2logTΔ24 and RT=O(√TKlogT) in the worst case — but it requires knowing Δ, and its instance-dependent constant is worse than UCB's. Committing early is the failure mode.

UCB1

Pull At=argmaxi[μˆi(t)+√2logtNi(t)]. Then RT≤∑i:Δi>08logTΔi+O(∑iΔi), and worst-case RT=O(√KTlogT).

proofThe bonus is exactly the Hoeffding radius, so w.p. ≥1−2t−4 every confidence interval contains its mean. On that event, arm i can only be chosen if its interval reaches μ⋆, which requires Ni(t)≤8logT/Δi2; sum failures over the union bound. Optimism in the face of uncertainty in one line: either the optimistic estimate was right (you played a good arm) or it was wrong (you learned a lot).

Regret in the wild — ε-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling, EXP3
Theorem — Lai & Robbins lower bound

For any consistent policy (subpolynomial regret on every instance), liminfT→∞RTlogT≥∑i:Δi>0ΔiKL(νi‖ν⋆).

ideaA change-of-measure argument: if arm i were pulled fewer than logTKL times, an alternative bandit in which i is optimal would be statistically indistinguishable, and consistency on that instance would be violated. The divergence decomposition 𝔼[∑iNi(T)]KL(νi‖νi′)≥kl(ℙ(E),ℙ′(E)) makes it precise.

Note the KL rather than Δ2: for Bernoulli arms near 0 or 1, KL≫Δ2/2, so UCB1's Hoeffding-based bonus is loose. KL-UCB uses the tight divergence-based index and is asymptotically optimal, matching Lai–Robbins exactly.

Thompson sampling. Maintain a posterior per arm; each round, sample a parameter from each posterior and play the argmax. Bayesian regret is optimal (Russo–Van Roy's information-ratio analysis), and the frequentist regret matches Lai–Robbins for Bernoulli arms (Kaufmann–Korda–Munos; Agrawal–Goyal). It is usually the best empirically, needs no bonus tuning, and extends trivially to structured and contextual settings by changing the posterior.

Best-arm identification is a different objective — pure exploration, no exploitation. Fixed confidence: successive elimination / LUCB needs O(∑iΔi−2log1δ) samples; the lower bound involves the same H1=∑Δi−2 complexity. Fixed budget: successive halving. A policy optimal for regret is not optimal for identification — UCB under-explores for the identification goal, which is a common practical confusion in A/B testing.

Contextual, Adversarial and Structured Bandits

Day 90 · 85 min
LinUCB

Assume 𝔼[rt∣a,x]=θ⋆⊤ϕ(x,a). Maintain Vt=λI+∑s<tϕsϕs⊤, θˆt=Vt−1∑sϕsrs, and play at=argmaxaθˆt⊤ϕ+βt‖ϕ‖Vt−1,RT=O˜(d√T).

the elliptical potential lemma∑t=1Tmin(1,‖ϕt‖2Vt−1)≤2logdetVTdetV0≤2dlog(1+TL2λd). It says the confidence width can be large only O(dlogT) times — a determinant potential argument, and the single most reusable lemma in linear bandits and linear RL.

Adversarial bandits — EXP3. With importance-weighted estimates ℓˆt(i)=ℓt(i)𝟙[At=i]pt(i) (unbiased) fed to Hedge: 𝔼[RT]≤O(√TKlogK), matching the Ω(√TK) lower bound up to √logK (removed by the implicitly-normalized-forecaster / Tsallis-INF, which is also simultaneously optimal in the stochastic regime — "best of both worlds"). The variance of the importance weights is 1/pt(i), so explicit exploration (γ-mixing with uniform) or an implicit barrier is required. EXP4 runs Hedge over N policies rather than K arms, giving O(√TKlogN) — logarithmic in the policy class, so a huge class costs little statistically and everything computationally.

Combinatorial and semi-bandit feedback. Choosing a subset/path/matching with feedback on each chosen component: regret O˜(√mdT) rather than √(dm)T — the structure is worth an exponential factor.

Reinforcement Learning: The Theory Core

Day 90 · 85 min
MDP and Bellman operators

(𝒮,𝒜,P,r,γ). For a policy π, (Tπv)(s)=r(s,π(s))+γ∑s′P(s′∣s,π(s))v(s′); the optimality operator replaces π(s) by maxa. Both are γ-contractions in ‖⋅‖∞.

contraction|(Tv)(s)−(Tw)(s)|≤γmaxa∑s′P(s′|s,a)|v(s′)−w(s′)|≤γ‖v−w‖∞, using |maxaf(a)−maxag(a)|≤maxa|f−g|. Banach's fixed-point theorem then gives existence, uniqueness of v⋆, and geometric convergence of value iteration at rate γk.

Existence of an optimal deterministic stationary policy: the greedy policy w.r.t. v⋆ attains v⋆, and the max over actions is attained (finite 𝒜). Policy iteration converges in finitely many steps because policies improve monotonically and there are finitely many; it is Newton's method on the Bellman equation, hence typically much faster than value iteration. LP formulation: minv∑sμ(s)v(s) s.t. v(s)≥r+γPv for all (s,a) — the dual variables are the occupancy measure, which is the object policy-gradient methods implicitly optimize.

TD learning and Q-learning

v(st)←v(st)+αt[rt+γv(st+1)−v(st)] is stochastic approximation (Lecture 67) applied to the fixed-point equation v=Tπv; convergence follows from the ODE method with ∑αt=∞, ∑αt2<∞, and the contraction supplying a Lyapunov function. Q-learning converges a.s. to Q⋆ under the same step-size conditions plus every state–action pair being visited infinitely often (Watkins–Dayan, Jaakkola–Jordan–Singh; Tsitsiklis for the asynchronous case).

The deadly triad

Bootstrapping + off-policy + function approximation can diverge. Baird's counterexample: a 7-state MDP with a linear value function and off-policy TD updates in which the weights diverge to infinity, even though the representable value function class contains v⋆. The mechanism: with function approximation, ΠTπ (projection composed with the Bellman operator) is a contraction under the on-policy distribution but need not be under any other. Fixes: on-policy sampling (SARSA, which converges under linear approximation), gradient-TD methods (GTD2/TDC — true stochastic gradient of the projected Bellman error, convergent off-policy), emphatic TD (reweighting to restore the contraction), or target networks and replay (heuristic stabilizers). This is not an implementation bug: the fixed point may not exist.

Policy gradient theorem

∇θJ(θ)=𝔼s∼dπ,a∼πθ[∇θlogπθ(a∣s)Qπθ(s,a)]. Crucially, no ∇θdπ term appears — the state-distribution derivative cancels. Any baseline b(s) may be subtracted without bias (𝔼[∇logπ]=0), giving the advantage A=Q−V and the variance reduction that makes actor–critic usable.

Sample complexity. With a generative model, Θ˜(|𝒮||𝒜|(1−γ)3ϵ2) samples suffice and are necessary for an ϵ-optimal policy (Azar et al.; Agarwal–Kakade–Yang) — the (1−γ)−3 is the correct horizon dependence, not (1−γ)−2. For online exploration, UCBVI achieves O˜(√H3SAT) regret, minimax optimal for large T. Relation to bandits: an MDP with |𝒮|=1 is exactly a bandit, and UCBVI's bonus is UCB's bonus propagated through the Bellman backup — the elliptical potential lemma reappears in the linear-MDP setting (LSVI-UCB).

Sequential Experimentation and Anytime-Valid Inference

Day 91 · 85 min

The problem. Peeking at a p-value and stopping when it dips below 0.05 gives type-I error →1 as the horizon grows: by the law of the iterated logarithm, √n|Xn|/σ crosses any fixed threshold almost surely if you wait long enough. Continuous monitoring with fixed-sample tests is not a small distortion; it is total.

e-values, e-processes, Ville

An e-value E≥0 satisfies 𝔼H0[E]≤1; by Markov, ℙ(E≥1/α)≤α. An e-process is a nonnegative supermartingale (Et) under H0 with E0=1. Ville's inequality: ℙ(∃t≥1:Et≥1/α)≤α. So 1/sups≤tEs is an anytime-valid p-value: you may stop whenever you like, for any reason, including looking at the data.

Where the e-process comes from. The likelihood ratio ∏s≤tp1(xs)p0(xs) is a martingale under H0 with mean 1 — that is Wald's SPRT. For composite alternatives, mix: Et=∫∏spθ(xs)p0(xs)dπ(θ) is still an e-process (Fubini), giving the mixture SPRT and, inverted, a confidence sequence Ct={θ:Et(θ)<1/α}withℙ(∀t:θ⋆∈Ct)≥1−α. Its width shrinks like √loglogt+log(1/α)t — the extra loglog is the LIL price of validity at all times, and it is provably necessary.

ApproachGuaranteeCost
Fixed-sample testValid at one pre-specified nNo peeking, ever

e-values compose. Products of independent e-values are e-values; averages of arbitrarily dependent e-values are e-values. That makes them the natural currency for combining evidence across studies and for multiple testing under dependence (e-BH, Lecture 57) — where p-values require Bonferroni-type corrections, e-values require none.

Adaptively collected data breaks standard inference

If arms are chosen by a bandit algorithm, the sample mean of an arm is biased: an arm is played more when its early estimates were lucky, so 𝔼[μˆi]≠μi — typically downward-biased for the arm that was played most (Nie et al., Shin–Ramdas–Rinaldo). Standard t-intervals under-cover badly. Fixes: adaptively-weighted estimators (Hadad et al.'s adaptive weighting restores asymptotic normality), always-valid confidence sequences, sample-splitting by batch, or forced exploration. Running a bandit and then analysing it like an A/B test is invalid, and this is now a common real-world error in online experimentation.

Recitation 11: Online Learning and Bandits Problem Set

Day 91 · 60 min
  1. Hedge regret. Prove the √(T/2)logN bound by the potential argument; then prove the anytime version with ηt∝√logN/t and compare constants with the doubling trick.
  2. UCB's radius. Derive √2logt/Ni from Hoeffding and a union bound over t and Ni; carry the constants through to the 8logT/Δi regret and identify exactly where a factor of 2 could be saved.
  3. A Lai–Robbins instance. Construct two Bernoulli bandits differing only in arm 2, and use the divergence-decomposition lemma to show any consistent policy must pull arm 2 at least logT/KL times. Compute the bound for μ=(0.9,0.8) and compare with Δ2-based reasoning.
  4. Implement and compare. ε-greedy, UCB1, KL-UCB, Thompson sampling, EXP3 on (a) a stochastic Bernoulli bandit and (b) an adversarial sequence designed against UCB. Plot regret against logT and against √T and identify which curve is straight in each regime.
  5. Confidence sequences. Implement a mixture-SPRT confidence sequence for a Bernoulli mean; verify uniform coverage over 104 replications with continuous monitoring, and contrast with the realized coverage of naive repeated t-intervals.
