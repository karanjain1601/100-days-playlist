Bayesian Inference & Model Selection

Reading: Gelman et al. BDA · PML2 Ch. 3–7 · Ghosal & van der Vaart · Robert The Bayesian Choice. The prior is not the interesting part. The interesting parts are what the posterior does asymptotically, what the evidence penalizes, and where both quietly fail.

109

Bayesian Foundations

Day 74 · 85 min

Why should degrees of belief obey the probability axioms at all?

Theorem — Dutch book / coherence

If your betting quotients violate the probability axioms, there is a finite set of bets, each of which you accept by your own quotients, whose net payoff is negative in every state. Conversely, if they satisfy the axioms, no such book exists.

This is a consistency argument, not an epistemological one: it says incoherent beliefs are exploitable, not that any particular coherent belief is right. Savage's axioms reach the same place from preferences over acts, giving simultaneously a probability and a utility.

Likelihood principle

All the evidence about θ in the data is contained in the likelihood function θ↦p(x∣θ), up to proportionality. It follows from the sufficiency and conditionality principles (Birnbaum's theorem).

What violates it. p-values and confidence intervals depend on the sample space — on what could have been observed. The canonical illustration: 9 successes in 12 trials gives a different p-value under binomial sampling (n fixed) than under negative-binomial sampling (stop at 3 failures), even though the likelihood is identical up to a constant. Bayesian inference is unchanged. Whether this is a defect of frequentism or a virtue (it is what makes error rates controllable, and what makes optional stopping detectable — Lecture 135) is the live disagreement, and both sides are stating something true.

Theorem — de Finetti

If (X1,X2,…) is an infinitely exchangeable sequence of binary variables, there is a unique distribution π on [0,1] with P(x1,…,xn)=∫01θ∑xi(1−θ)n−∑xiπ(dθ).

proof sketch (binary case)By exchangeability, P depends only on sn=∑i≤nxi. Compute P of a pattern by conditioning on sN for large N: it is a hypergeometric expression that converges to the binomial as N→∞, with the empirical frequency sN/N converging a.s. to a random limit θ whose law is π. Uniqueness follows from moment determinacy.

The content: parameters and priors are not extra assumptions — they are forced by exchangeability. "i.i.d. given θ, with θ∼π" is the only representation of an exchangeable belief. This is the strongest available justification for hierarchical modelling (Lecture 112).

Where it breaks

Marginal likelihoods are undefined under improper priors (the normalizing constant is arbitrary), so Bayes factors computed with flat priors are meaningless — the arbitrary constant does not cancel between models of different dimension. Prior sensitivity analysis is therefore not an optional robustness check; for any conclusion that depends on the evidence, it is part of the analysis.

Bayesian Asymptotics

Day 75 · 85 min
Theorem — Bernstein–von Mises

Under LAN regularity, a prior positive and continuous at θ0, and a consistent testing condition, ‖p(θ∣X1:n)−N(θˆn,(nI(θ0))−1)‖TVP⟶0. Consequently Bayesian credible sets are asymptotically valid frequentist confidence sets, and the prior washes out at rate O(1/n).

sketchExpand the log-likelihood ratio quadratically around θˆn (LAN); the prior contributes O(1/n) after normalization; Doob/Schwartz consistency confines the posterior mass to a shrinking neighbourhood, so the quadratic expansion is uniformly valid where the mass is.

Posterior consistency. Doob: for any prior, the posterior is consistent at π-almost every θ — which sounds strong and is nearly vacuous, since the null set can contain every parameter you care about. Schwartz: if θ0 is in the KL support of the prior (π{θ:KL(pθ0‖pθ)<ϵ}>0 for all ϵ) and uniformly consistent tests exist, the posterior is consistent at θ0. This is the checkable version.

Where BvM fails
  • Nonparametric models. Freedman's example: a Dirichlet-type prior on discrete distributions can be inconsistent for the mean. BvM for functionals holds only under bias conditions on the prior's smoothness (the "semiparametric BvM" of Castillo–Nickl); credible bands generally do not have frequentist coverage.
  • High dimension. With p growing, BvM requires roughly p3/n→0 (Ghosal) or plogp/n→0 under stronger conditions. Beyond that, credible sets can under-cover badly.
  • Boundary and non-regular models (variance components at 0, U[0,θ]): the posterior limit is not normal.
  • Misspecification. The posterior concentrates on the KL projection with a sandwich-type covariance, so credible intervals from a misspecified likelihood are the wrong width — the Bayesian analogue of Lecture 20's Huber–White correction. Remedies: the "BayesBag" bagged posterior, or explicit power/robust likelihoods.
Laplace approximation

∫eng(θ)dθ=eng(θˆ)(2πn)d/2|−∇2g(θˆ)|−1/2(1+O(n−1)). Applied to the marginal likelihood this gives the derivation of BIC in the next lecture; applied to a posterior it gives the Gaussian approximation whose error is O(n−1) for smooth unimodal posteriors and unbounded for skewed or multimodal ones.

Contraction rates. Ghosal–Ghosh–van der Vaart: the posterior contracts at ϵn if (i) the prior puts mass ≥e−cnϵn2 on a KL ball of radius ϵn, and (ii) sieves exist with entropy ≤nϵn2 and negligible remaining prior mass. Prior mass versus testing — the Bayesian mirror of the bias–variance trade, and it reproduces the minimax rates of Lecture 59 when the prior is matched to the truth's smoothness.

Model Comparison and the Evidence

Day 75 · 85 min
The Occam factor — why a flexible model can lose to a rigid one
The evidence and its automatic penalty

p(D∣M)=∫p(D∣θ,M)π(θ∣M)dθ. Laplace-expanding, logp(D∣M)≈logp(D∣θˆ)fit+logπ(θˆ)+d2log2πn−12log|I(θˆ)|Occamfactor. The Occam factor is log of (posterior width / prior width): a model that spreads prior mass over configurations the data did not select pays for that spread.

BIC. Dropping the O(1) terms leaves logp(D∣M)≈logp(D∣θˆ)−d2logn, i.e. BIC=−2ℓ(θˆ)+dlogn. It is an approximation to the evidence, so it targets the true model (selection consistency under ℳ-closed) and is not an estimate of predictive error.

AIC. −2ℓ(θˆ)+2d estimates the expected out-of-sample deviance −2𝔼newℓ. It targets prediction, is asymptotically efficient for choosing among wrong models, and is not selection consistent. AIC and BIC answer different questions; arguing about which is "better" without stating the question is a category error, and the theorem of Lecture 146 says no criterion can do both.

Lindley's paradox

With a point null H0:θ=0 against H1:θ∼N(0,τ2), the Bayes factor is B01=√1+nτ2exp(−z22⋅nτ21+nτ2). Fix z=2 (so p≈0.05) and let n→∞: B01→∞ — the Bayes factor favours the null exactly where the p-value rejects. Worse, B01→∞ as τ→∞ for any fixed data: making the alternative's prior vaguer makes the null win arbitrarily. There is no "uninformative" prior for a Bayes factor, and this is why the ℳ-open/ℳ-closed distinction matters: if you do not believe any candidate is true, do not compute Bayes factors — compute predictive scores.

Hierarchical Models and Empirical Bayes

Day 76 · 85 min
The hierarchical normal model, solved

yj∣θj∼N(θj,σj2), θj∼N(μ,τ2), j=1..J. Then 𝔼[θj∣y,μ,τ]=(1−Bj)yj+Bjμ,Bj=σj2σj2+τ2, with posterior variance (1−Bj)σj2. Groups with noisy estimates (large σj) are shrunk more; small τ (homogeneous groups) shrinks everything toward μ.

This is partial pooling: complete pooling (τ=0) and no pooling (τ=∞) are the endpoints, and the data choose the interpolation. Plugging in τˆ2=max(0,1J∑(yj−y)2−σ2) gives an empirical-Bayes estimator that is essentially the positive-part James–Stein rule of Lecture 25 — the two derivations meet exactly here.

Nonparametric empirical Bayes. Robbins' formula: for Poisson yj∣λj∼Pois(λj) with any prior G, 𝔼[λ∣y=k]=(k+1)f(k+1)f(k) where f is the marginal pmf — estimable from counts alone, with no assumption on G. Tweedie's formula: for y∣μ∼N(μ,σ2), 𝔼[μ∣y]=y+σ2ddylogf(y). Both convert an inverse problem into a marginal-density estimation problem, and both are the backbone of large-scale inference (Efron) and of modern denoising.

Two failure modes

Understated uncertainty. Empirical Bayes conditions on τˆ as if known, so credible intervals are too narrow — badly so when J is small. Full Bayes with a prior on τ (half-Cauchy or half-normal on the scale, never inverse-gamma(ϵ,ϵ), which is improper-ish and puts a spike near zero) fixes it.
The funnel. In the centred parameterization θj∼N(μ,τ), the posterior has a funnel geometry: as τ→0 the feasible range of θj collapses, so no single HMC step size works and you get divergences. The non-centred reparameterization θj=μ+τηj, ηj∼N(0,1) decouples them and mixes well. This is one of the most consequential practical facts in applied Bayesian modelling: the same model, two parameterizations, one of which is unsamplable.

Bayesian Regression and Sparsity Priors

Day 77 · 85 min

Conjugate linear model. β∼N(b0,Σ0), y∣β∼N(Xβ,σ2I) gives β∣y∼N(Σn(Σ0−1b0+σ−2X⊤y),Σn) with Σn=(Σ0−1+σ−2X⊤X)−1. With Σ0=τ2I the posterior mean is ridge (Lecture 44). Zellner's g-prior Σ0=gσ2(X⊤X)−1 makes the prior covariance match the data's geometry, giving closed-form Bayes factors; g=n reproduces BIC-like behaviour. Its weakness is that it is improper without a prior on g, and mixtures of g-priors (hyper-g) are the modern fix.

Posterior predictive p(y⋆∣y)=∫p(y⋆∣β)p(β∣y)dβ=N(x⋆⊤β,σ2+x⋆⊤Σnx⋆) — note the two variance terms, exactly the prediction-vs-confidence distinction of Lecture 42.

Sparsity: two families

PriorFormBehaviour
Spike-and-slabβj∼(1−w)δ0+wN(0,c2)Exact zeros; posterior inclusion probabilities are interpretable; 2p model space, explored by SSVS/MCMC
Laplace (Bayesian lasso)βj∼Lap(0,1/λ)Mode is the lasso, but the posterior mean is not sparse and the posterior does not concentrate correctly — it is a poor sparsity prior despite the name
Horseshoeβj∣λj,τ∼N(0,τ2λj2), λj∼C+(0,1)Infinite spike at 0 and Cauchy tails: aggressive shrinkage of noise, essentially none of large signals

The global–local frame. βj∼N(0,τ2λj2): τ controls overall sparsity, λj lets individual coefficients escape. The shrinkage factor κj=1/(1+τ2λj2) has, under the horseshoe, a Beta(12,12) prior — a horseshoe shape with mass piled at κ=0 (no shrinkage) and κ=1 (total shrinkage). That bimodality is precisely what a good sparsity prior needs and what the Laplace prior lacks.

Bayesian vs penalized sparsity

The lasso's solution is a posterior mode, but the posterior itself under a Laplace prior does not contract at the minimax rate and its credible sets do not cover. Conversely, horseshoe posteriors contract optimally but give no exact zeros — selection requires a separate decision rule (e.g. thresholding κj<1/2, or a decision-theoretic rule under a chosen loss). Do not report a Bayesian sparse model's "selected variables" without saying which rule produced them.

Bayesian Nonparametrics I: Dirichlet Processes

Day 78 · 85 min
Definition — three equivalent ones

(1) Finite marginals. G∼DP(α,G0) iff for every finite partition A1..Ak, (G(A1),..,G(Ak))∼Dir(αG0(A1),..,αG0(Ak)).
(2) Stick-breaking (Sethuraman). vk∼Beta(1,α), πk=vk∏l<k(1−vl), ϕk∼G0, G=∑kπkδϕk — so G is discrete with probability one.
(3) CRP. Customer n+1 joins table k with probability nkn+α and a new table with probability αn+α.

Conjugacy. G∣θ1:n∼DP(α+n,αG0+∑iδθiα+n) — the posterior base measure is a weighted average of the prior and the empirical measure, with α acting as a prior sample size. The predictive is the CRP, and the "rich get richer" dynamics are exactly this conjugacy.

DP mixture. Since G is discrete, use it as a mixing measure: θi∼G, xi∼F(⋅∣θi). Ties among θi induce clusters, and the number of clusters is random and grows as 𝔼[Kn]≈αlogn — logarithmic, not linear.

Inference. Collapsed Gibbs (Neal's Algorithm 3 for conjugate F,G0; Algorithms 8 and the auxiliary-variable schemes for non-conjugate) integrates out G and samples cluster assignments from the CRP predictive times the likelihood. Slice sampling and truncated stick-breaking VI (Blei–Jordan) work with an explicit but truncated π; the truncation level must be checked, since a truncation that binds silently changes the model.

The inconsistency result

Miller & Harrison: the DP mixture posterior on the number of components is inconsistent — even when the data come from a finite mixture with k0 components, P(Kn=k0∣x1:n)→1; the posterior keeps mass on extra small clusters. The DP is a consistent estimator of the density and an inconsistent estimator of the component count. So: never report "the DP found k clusters" as an estimate of a true k. If k is the estimand, use a mixture-of-finite-mixtures prior with an explicit prior on k.

Bayesian Nonparametrics II: Beyond the DP

Day 78 · 85 min

GPs revisited. A GP is a prior on functions; a DP is a prior on distributions. Both are Bayesian nonparametrics, but the DP's draws are discrete and the GP's are continuous — the appropriate one is determined by whether the unknown object is a mixing measure or a regression surface.

Contraction and prior rigidity

Posterior contraction for nonparametric Bayes (Ghosal–van der Vaart) requires the prior to place enough mass near the truth and not too much mass on overly complex alternatives. Two failure modes: a prior that is too rigid (a GP smoother than the truth) contracts at the prior's rate, not the truth's — extra data cannot fix it; a prior that is too diffuse spreads mass so thin that the prior-mass condition fails. Hierarchical priors on the smoothness (e.g. a prior on the GP lengthscale, or a mixture over sieve dimensions) restore rate adaptivity over a range — the Bayesian counterpart of Lepski's method.

Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Day 79 · 85 min

The posterior is not the answer; the action is. Lecture 16's framework, now made operational.

Value of information

EVPI =𝔼θ[maxaU(a,θ)]−maxa𝔼θ[U(a,θ)]≥0 by Jensen — information is never harmful in expectation to a coherent agent. EVSI for a sample z: 𝔼z[maxa𝔼θ∣zU]−maxa𝔼θU. Both are nested expectations, hence the standard Monte Carlo cost O(N2); multilevel MC and regression (least-squares Monte Carlo) reduce it.

Bayesian experimental design. Choose the design d maximizing expected information gain EIG(d)=𝔼y∣d[KL(p(θ∣y,d)‖p(θ))]=I(θ;Y∣d). For linear-Gaussian models this reduces to classical alphabetic criteria: D-optimality maximizes logdet(X⊤X) (exactly the EIG), A-optimality minimizes tr of the covariance, and so on. Note the submodularity of A↦I(θ;YA) under conditional independence — Lecture 72's greedy (1−1/e) guarantee applies, and this is the theoretical backing for greedy sensor placement.

Risk-sensitive objectives

Expected utility is risk-neutral in the outcome. CVaR at level α — the mean of the worst α-fraction — is a coherent risk measure with the dual representation CVaRα(Z)=maxQ∈𝒬α𝔼Q[Z], which is exactly a distributionally robust objective (Lecture 39). Rockafellar–Uryasev's variational form CVaRα=mint{t+1α𝔼(Z−t)+} makes it a convex program.

Label complexity

The disagreement coefficient ϑ measures how fast the disagreement region shrinks with the hypothesis ball's radius. Agnostic active learning (A2, DHM) achieves label complexity O˜(ϑ2d(log1ϵ+ν2ϵ2)) versus passive O(d(ν2/ϵ2)). In the realizable case (ν=0) this is an exponential log(1/ϵ) improvement; with noise ν>0 the gain shrinks to a constant factor. Active learning does not help much when the Bayes error is large — the single most useful thing to know before building an active-learning pipeline.

The Bayesian Workflow in Practice

Day 79 · 75 min
  1. Prior predictive checks. Simulate θ∼π, y∼p(⋅∣θ), and look at the simulated data. If your "weakly informative" prior generates patients 400 years old or log-odds of 106, it is not weakly informative — it is strongly and absurdly informative on the outcome scale. This catches more errors than any posterior check.
  2. Simulation-based calibration. Draw θ0∼π, y∼p(⋅∣θ0), run your sampler, and compute the rank of θ0 among the posterior draws. Over many replications the ranks must be uniform; systematic deviations diagnose specific bugs — a ∪ shape means the posterior is too narrow, a ∩ shape too wide, a slope means bias. This validates the implementation, not the model, and it is the only tool that does.
  3. MCMC diagnostics. Rˆ (rank-normalized, split, Vehtari et al.) should be <1.01; bulk-ESS and tail-ESS both >400 per chain-set; divergent transitions in HMC mean the sampler failed to conserve energy — usually a funnel (Lecture 112), and they invalidate the draws rather than merely slowing them; the E-BFMI energy diagnostic detects poor momentum resampling.
  4. Posterior predictive checks. Compare T(yrep) to T(y) for test statistics the model does not fit by construction. A PPC on the mean of a model with a free mean parameter tells you nothing; a PPC on the proportion of zeros, the max, or the autocorrelation tells you a great deal. Bayesian p-values are conservative because the data are used twice — treat them as descriptive.
  5. Model expansion, not model rejection. When a check fails, the productive move is to expand the model to include the discrepancy (add overdispersion, a group effect, a heavier tail) rather than to discard it.
  6. Report prior sensitivity honestly. Refit under two or three defensible priors and show the conclusion's dependence. If it changes, that is a finding.
The workflow is not a pipeline

Gelman et al.'s point is that these steps are iterated, and the iteration means your final model is data-dependent in ways the posterior does not represent. That is the Bayesian version of the post-selection problem in Lecture 56, and it has no clean solution — only disclosure, holdout data, and preregistration where feasible.

Recitation 9: Bayesian Problem Set

Day 80 · 60 min
  1. Conjugate posterior and predictive. For the normal–inverse-gamma model derive the posterior and show the predictive is Student-t with 2an degrees of freedom; explain why the tails are heavier than the likelihood's.
  2. The Occam factor. For two nested linear models, compute the Bayes factor exactly under g-priors and identify the term that penalizes dimension; verify the √n-per-parameter scaling that yields BIC.
  3. Collapsed Gibbs for a DP mixture. Implement Neal's Algorithm 3 for a Gaussian DP mixture; check the αlogn growth of cluster count, and demonstrate the Miller–Harrison inconsistency by generating from a 3-component mixture and plotting p(K∣x) for increasing n.
  4. Diagnose a pathological posterior. Fit the 8-schools model in the centred parameterization with HMC; record divergences; plot logτ against θ1 to see the funnel; refit non-centred and show the divergences vanish.
  5. SBC. Run simulation-based calibration on a small model with a deliberately introduced bug (e.g. a missing Jacobian for a transformed parameter) and identify the rank-histogram signature.
Two papers

Foundational: Neal (2000), Markov chain sampling methods for Dirichlet process mixture models. Modern: Vehtari, Gelman & Gabry (2017), Practical Bayesian model evaluation using leave-one-out cross-validation and WAIC — pay attention to the Pareto kˆ diagnostic; it is what makes LOO trustworthy rather than merely convenient.
