01

Orientation: The Three Cultures of Machine Learning

Day 1 · 60 min

What are we actually trying to do, and why do three research communities give three incompatible-looking answers to that question?

The three primitives

Each tradition takes something different as given and derives the rest.

TraditionPrimitiveTargetSuccess criterion
Statistical (Fisher, Neyman, Le Cam)A model class 𝒫={Pθ} containing or approximating the truthThe parameter θRisk under a loss, asymptotic efficiency, minimax rate
Computational learning theory (Valiant, Vapnik)A hypothesis class ℋ; the distribution D is arbitrary and unknownA predictor with small risk relative to infh∈ℋR(h)Sample complexity, uniformly over D
Bayesian (Laplace, de Finetti, Jaynes)A joint distribution over parameters and dataThe posterior p(θ∣x)Coherence; calibration of predictive distributions

The disagreements are real but narrower than they look. All three end up minimizing an expected loss; they differ on what is random. A useful reduction: the frequentist asks for guarantees uniform in θ, the Bayesian for guarantees averaged over θ, and the complete class theorem (Lecture 16) says every admissible uniform guarantee is an averaged one for some prior.

The decomposition that organizes the series

Theorem — error decomposition

Let R(h)=𝔼(X,Y)∼Dℓ(h(X),Y), let h⋆ be the Bayes rule over all measurable h, let h⋆ℋ=argminh∈ℋR(h), let hˆn minimize empirical risk exactly, and let h˜n be what your optimizer actually returns. Then

R(h˜n)−R(h⋆)=R(h⋆ℋ)−R(h⋆)approximation+R(hˆn)−R(h⋆ℋ)estimation+R(h˜n)−R(hˆn)optimization.

The three terms trade off. Enlarging ℋ decreases the first and increases the second. Optimizing harder decreases the third at the cost of compute, and — crucially — past a point the third term is smaller than the second, so further optimization buys nothing statistically. This is the entire argument for stochastic methods in Lecture 67.

Notation held fixed for 154 lectures

  • X∈𝒳 covariates, Y∈𝒴 response, D or P their joint law, S={(Xi,Yi)}i=1niid∼D.
  • R(h) population risk, Rˆn(h) empirical risk, ℓ a loss. Design matrix X∈ℝn×p, rows xi⊤.
  • an≲bn means an≤Cbn for an absolute constant; ≍ is two-sided; O˜ suppresses logs.
  • ‖⋅‖ without subscript is ℓ2 on vectors and the operator norm on matrices.
Where it breaks

The decomposition assumes h⋆ℋ exists — the infimum is attained. For non-compact ℋ (e.g. unbounded linear predictors under logistic loss on separable data) it is not, and “estimation error” is then measured against an infimum that no member achieves. Every statement in Module 2 that quantifies over argmin needs this checked.

How to read a proof, and how to read a paper

Reading a proof: identify (i) which hypothesis is used where — annotate the margin; (ii) the one inequality that is not an equality, which is where the slack lives; (iii) whether constants are absolute or depend on suppressed quantities. Reading a paper: read abstract → theorem statements → assumptions section → experiments → introduction, in that order. The assumptions section is where papers are honest and the introduction is where they are not.

02

Measure-Theoretic Probability, Only What Is Needed

Day 1 · 85 min

Why can't we do probability with densities and sums, and what exactly does “conditional expectation” mean when we condition on a continuous variable?

Definition

A σ-algebra ℱ on Ω is a collection of subsets containing Ω, closed under complement and countable union. A measure μ:ℱ→[0,∞] satisfies μ(∅)=0 and countable additivity on disjoint sets. A probability measure adds μ(Ω)=1. A random variable is a measurable map X:(Ω,ℱ)→(ℝ,ℬ), i.e. X−1(B)∈ℱ for all Borel B.

Two facts justify the whole apparatus. First, on ℝ there is no translation-invariant countably additive measure defined on all subsets giving [0,1] measure 1 (Vitali). So we must restrict the domain, and ℬ is the restriction. Second, the Lebesgue integral commutes with limits under hypotheses that Riemann's does not.

Theorem — the three convergence theorems

Monotone convergence: 0≤fn↑f pointwise ⇒∫fn↑∫f.
Fatou: fn≥0⇒∫liminffn≤liminf∫fn.
Dominated convergence: fn→f a.e. and |fn|≤g with ∫g<∞ ⇒∫fn→∫f.

why it mattersEvery interchange of 𝔼 and lim, every differentiation under the integral sign (score function in Lecture 19, gradient of a log-partition in Lecture 12, policy gradient in Lecture 134) is one of these three, and the domination hypothesis is exactly what fails in heavy-tailed and non-regular models.

Radon–Nikodym: what “density” means

ν≪μ (ν absolutely continuous w.r.t. μ) means μ(A)=0⇒ν(A)=0. Then there is a μ-a.e. unique f≥0 with ν(A)=∫Afdμ; write f=dν/dμ. A “pdf” is dP/dλ against Lebesgue measure; a “pmf” is dP/d# against counting measure. This unification is not cosmetic: likelihood ratios, KL divergences and importance weights are all Radon–Nikodym derivatives, and they are undefined exactly when absolute continuity fails — which is precisely the failure mode of importance sampling in Lecture 101 and of IPW under positivity violations in Lecture 140.

Definition — conditional expectation

For X∈L1 and a sub-σ-algebra 𝒢⊆ℱ, 𝔼[X∣𝒢] is the a.s.-unique 𝒢-measurable Z∈L1 with ∫GZdℙ=∫GXdℙforallG∈𝒢. Existence follows from Radon–Nikodym applied to ν(G)=∫GXdℙ on (Ω,𝒢).

For X∈L2 this is exactly the orthogonal projection onto the closed subspace L2(𝒢). That single sentence explains: the tower property (𝔼[𝔼[X∣𝒢]]=𝔼X is “projections preserve means”), the fact that the conditional mean minimizes squared error (Lecture 17), the Pythagorean law of total variance, and Rao–Blackwell (Lecture 18) — projecting onto a smaller σ-algebra cannot increase the norm.

Product measures and Fubini–Tonelli

Tonelli: for f≥0 measurable on a product of σ-finite spaces, the double integral equals either iterated integral. Fubini: same conclusion for f∈L1. Independence is the statement that the joint law is a product measure; the factorization of likelihoods across an i.i.d. sample is Tonelli.

Where it breaks

σ-finiteness is not decorative. Regular conditional distributions (needed to say “P(Y∈⋅∣X=x) is a probability measure for each x”) exist on standard Borel spaces but not in general; on non-standard spaces conditioning on a null event is genuinely ambiguous — the Borel–Kolmogorov paradox. Every time you write p(y∣x) for continuous x you are invoking a regular conditional distribution and implicitly choosing a version.

Convergence and Asymptotic Machinery

Day 2 · 85 min

Four notions of convergence, one lattice of implications, and the counterexample for every arrow that does not exist.

Theorem — Borel–Cantelli

I. ∑nℙ(An)<∞⇒ℙ(Ani.o.)=0. II. If the An are independent and ∑nℙ(An)=∞ then ℙ(Ani.o.)=1.

proof of Iℙ(limsupAn)≤ℙ(⋃n≥NAn)≤∑n≥Nℙ(An)→0 as N→∞ by convergence of the series.

BC-I is the standard route from a summable tail bound to an almost-sure statement: it is how “with probability 1−δ for each n” becomes “eventually, almost surely”, provided the δn are summable. That is why bounds are usually written with δ/n2 rather than δ/n.

Laws of large numbers and the CLT

  • WLLN (Khinchine): Xi i.i.d., 𝔼|X1|<∞⇒Xn→μ in probability.
  • SLLN (Kolmogorov): same hypothesis, convergence a.s. Finite variance is not needed.
  • Lindeberg–Lévy CLT: i.i.d., σ2<∞ ⇒√n(Xn−μ)⇒N(0,σ2).
  • Lindeberg–Feller: for triangular arrays, asymptotic normality holds iff the Lindeberg condition ∑i𝔼[Zni2𝟙(|Zni|>ϵ)]→0 holds. This is the version you need for regression with non-identically distributed rows.
Theorem — Slutsky, CMT, delta method

If Xn⇒X and Yn→c in probability, then Xn+Yn⇒X+c and XnYn⇒cX. If g is continuous at X a.s. then g(Xn)⇒g(X). If √n(θˆn−θ)⇒N(0,Σ) and g is differentiable at θ with Jacobian J, then √n(g(θˆn)−g(θ))⇒N(0,JΣJ⊤). If J=0, the first-order term vanishes and n(g(θˆn)−g(θ))⇒12Z⊤∇2g(θ)Z — a weighted χ2, not a normal. This second-order case is exactly what happens to a likelihood ratio at a boundary and to the deviance of a nested model.

Op/op calculus

Xn=op(1) means Xn→0 in probability; Xn=Op(1) means tightness: ∀ϵ∃M with supnℙ(|Xn|>M)<ϵ. The algebra op(1)Op(1)=op(1), Op(1)+op(1)=Op(1) turns three-page Taylor arguments into three lines, and is used unchanged in Lectures 20, 110 and 142.

Where it breaks

Convergence in distribution says nothing about the same probability space, so Xn⇒X and Yn⇒Y do not give Xn+Yn⇒X+Y. Slutsky works only because one limit is constant. Also, ⇒ does not imply convergence of moments without uniform integrability — a standard error can diverge while the distribution converges.

Concentration I: From Markov to Bernstein

Day 3 · 85 min

The CLT is asymptotic. Learning theory needs a bound valid at n=50. Where does one come from?

Everything below is a single move applied with increasing cleverness: Markov's inequality on a transformed variable.

Theorem — Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff

Z≥0: ℙ(Z≥t)≤𝔼Z/t. Applying to (Z−𝔼Z)2 gives Chebyshev, ℙ(|Z−μ|≥t)≤σ2/t2. Applying to eλZ and optimizing gives the Chernoff bound ℙ(Z≥t)≤infλ>0e−λt𝔼eλZ=e−ψZ*(t), where ψZ* is the Fenchel conjugate of the cumulant generating function ψZ(λ)=log𝔼eλZ.

Note what just happened: concentration is convex duality applied to the CGF. This is the thread that reappears in Lecture 10 (conjugates), Lecture 12 (exponential families), and Lecture 35 (PAC-Bayes, via Donsker–Varadhan).

Lemma — Hoeffding

If a≤X≤b a.s. and 𝔼X=0 then 𝔼eλX≤eλ2(b−a)2/8 for all λ∈ℝ.

proof sketchLet ψ(λ)=log𝔼eλX. Then ψ(0)=0, ψ′(0)=𝔼X=0, and ψ′′(λ)=Varλ(X) under the exponentially tilted law, which is supported in [a,b] and so has variance at most (b−a)2/4 by Popoviciu. Taylor with remainder gives ψ(λ)≤λ2(b−a)2/8.

Theorem — Hoeffding's inequality

X1,…,Xn independent, Xi∈[ai,bi], S=∑i(Xi−𝔼Xi). Then ℙ(S≥t)≤exp(−2t2∑i(bi−ai)2). For the mean of i.i.d. variables in [0,1]: ℙ(|X−μ|≥t)≤2e−2nt2, equivalently |X−μ|≤√log(2/δ)/(2n) w.p. ≥1−δ.

Theorem — Bernstein

If additionally |Xi−𝔼Xi|≤b and ∑iVar(Xi)=v, then ℙ(S≥t)≤exp(−t22v+2bt/3). Two regimes: for small t the bound is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy v; for large t it degrades to sub-exponential e−3t/(2b).

Bernstein is strictly sharper than Hoeffding whenever the variance is small relative to the range — the case in every low-noise or rare-event problem. It is the reason “fast rates” O(1/n) exist at all: when the excess risk itself bounds the variance (a Bernstein condition, Lecture 38), the quadratic term dominates and √v/n becomes v/n.

Bound comparison — Bernoulli mean, one-sided deviation
Where it breaks

All of the above need 𝔼eλX<∞ near 0. For Pareto, Student-t with few degrees of freedom, or any polynomially tailed loss, the MGF is infinite for every λ>0 and the Chernoff method returns a vacuous bound. Replacements: (i) truncation plus Bernstein on the truncated part; (ii) median-of-means, which gives sub-Gaussian deviations assuming only a finite variance; (iii) Catoni's M-estimator. Median-of-means is the modern default and appears again in robust mean estimation (Lecture 24).

# median-of-means: sub-Gaussian deviations from finite variance alone
def mom(x, k):
    # k blocks; deviation ~ sigma*sqrt(k/n) w.p. 1-e^{-k}, no MGF needed
    n = len(x); idx = np.random.permutation(n)
    blocks = np.array_split(x[idx], k)
    return np.median([b.mean() for b in blocks])

Concentration II: Sub-Gaussianity and Beyond

Day 4 · 85 min

How do we concentrate functions that are not sums — a supremum, an eigenvalue, an empirical risk?

Definition — sub-Gaussian, sub-exponential

X is sub-Gaussian with proxy σ2 if any (hence all, up to constants) of: tails ℙ(|X|≥t)≤2e−t2/2σ2; moments (𝔼|X|p)1/p≲σ√p; MGF 𝔼eλ(X−𝔼X)≤eλ2σ2/2; Orlicz norm ‖X‖ψ2=inf{t>0:𝔼eX2/t2≤2}<∞. Sub-exponential replaces √p by p and ψ2 by ψ1(u)=eu−1. Key relation: ‖XY‖ψ1≤‖X‖ψ2‖Y‖ψ2, so squares of sub-Gaussians are sub-exponential — which is why χ2 concentration (Bernstein-type) governs quadratic forms and covariance estimation.

Theorem — bounded differences (McDiarmid)

Let f:𝒳n→ℝ satisfy |f(x)−f(x′)|≤ci whenever x,x′ differ only in coordinate i. For independent X1,…,Xn, ℙ(f−𝔼f≥t)≤exp(−2t2∑ici2).

proofDoob martingale Mk=𝔼[f∣X1:k], so f−𝔼f=∑k(Mk−Mk−1). Bounded differences imply each increment lies in an interval of length ck conditionally, so Hoeffding's lemma applies conditionally; multiply the conditional MGF bounds through the tower property and apply Chernoff.

This is the workhorse of Module 2: the supremum suph∈ℋ|R(h)−Rˆn(h)| changes by at most 1/n when one sample point moves (for losses in [0,1]), so it concentrates at rate √log(1/δ)/2n around its mean — whatever that mean is. Bounding the mean is then the separate job of symmetrization and Rademacher complexity.

Theorem — Efron–Stein

Var(f(X))≤12∑i=1n𝔼[(f(X)−f(X(i)))2] where X(i) replaces coordinate i by an independent copy. A variance analogue of McDiarmid needing no uniform bound, and the source of the jackknife's validity (Lecture 149).

Matrix concentration

Theorem — matrix Bernstein

S=∑iZi with Zi independent, mean-zero, symmetric d×d, ‖Zi‖≤b, v=‖∑i𝔼Zi2‖. Then ℙ(‖S‖≥t)≤2dexp(−t2/2v+bt/3).

The 2d prefactor costs only √logd in the resulting rate. Consequence for covariance: for sub-Gaussian rows, ‖Σˆ−Σ‖≲‖Σ‖(√d/n+d/n). The dimension enters as d/n, not 1/n: you cannot estimate a covariance in operator norm unless n≫d. Module 4 is largely about what to do when that fails.

Talagrand's inequality on product spaces sharpens McDiarmid for suprema of empirical processes by replacing the crude ∑ci2 with a variance term (the “Talagrand variance”), giving Bernstein-type behaviour for suph(Rˆn−R) and thereby fast rates in Lecture 38.

Where it breaks

McDiarmid's constant ci is a worst-case sensitivity. For unbounded losses (squared loss with Gaussian noise) ci=∞ and the bound is empty; you need truncation plus a tail argument, or a moment-based inequality. Reporting a McDiarmid bound for squared loss without a boundedness assumption is a common referee catch.

Linear Algebra I: Geometry of Estimation

Day 5 · 85 min

Why is nearly every classical estimator a projection, and what does the spectrum of the projected operator tell you?

Theorem — orthogonal projection

Let V⊆ℝn be a subspace with basis matrix A of full column rank. Then PV=A(A⊤A)−1A⊤ is the unique matrix with PV2=PV=PV⊤ and col(PV)=V; it satisfies ‖y−PVy‖≤‖y−v‖ for all v∈V, with equality iff v=PVy. Its eigenvalues are 1 (dimV times) and 0; hence tr(PV)=dimV — the identity behind “degrees of freedom”.

Theorem — spectral theorem

Every symmetric A∈ℝd×d satisfies A=UΛU⊤ with U orthogonal and Λ real diagonal.

proof sketchThe map x↦x⊤Ax on the compact unit sphere attains its maximum at some u1; a Lagrange-multiplier argument gives Au1=λ1u1. Restrict to u1⊥, which A preserves by symmetry, and induct.

Theorem — Courant–Fischer min–max

For symmetric A with λ1≥…≥λd, λk=maxdimV=kmin0≠x∈Vx⊤Axx⊤x=mindimW=d−k+1max0≠x∈Wx⊤Axx⊤x.

Consequences used later: PCA (Lecture 122) is max‖u‖=1u⊤Σˆu; the Rayleigh quotient formulation makes the top-k subspace a maximizer of trace; spectral clustering (Lecture 125) relaxes a combinatorial cut to a Rayleigh quotient over a subspace; Weyl's inequality |λk(A+E)−λk(A)|≤‖E‖ is an immediate corollary and is the first step of every perturbation argument.

Loewner order. A⪯B iff B−A⪰0. It is a partial, not total, order — A⪯B implies λk(A)≤λk(B) for all k and trA≤trB, but not A2⪯B2. Matrix monotone functions (those preserving ⪯) include t↦−t−1 and t↦logt, which is why Σ↦Σ−1 reverses the order and why log-det appears as the natural matrix concave function in Lectures 8, 54 and 116.

Where it breaks

(A⊤A)−1 requires full column rank. Near-collinearity does not break the formula, it breaks its numerics: κ(A⊤A)=κ(A)2, so forming the normal equations squares the condition number and halves the number of accurate digits. Use QR or SVD (next lecture), never inv(X.T@X).

Linear Algebra II: SVD and Perturbation

Day 6 · 85 min

One factorization underlies PCA, ridge, matrix completion, spectral clustering and sketching. Why, and when does it fail to be stable?

Theorem — SVD

Every A∈ℝn×p factors as A=UΣV⊤ with U∈ℝn×n, V∈ℝp×p orthogonal and Σ diagonal with σ1≥…≥σmin(n,p)≥0. The σi are unique; the singular vectors are unique up to sign within simple singular values and up to rotation within repeated ones.

Theorem — Eckart–Young–Mirsky

For any unitarily invariant norm, and in particular for ‖⋅‖2 and ‖⋅‖F, minrank(B)≤k‖A−B‖=‖A−Ak‖,Ak=∑i≤kσiuivi⊤, with values σk+1 and (∑i>kσi2)1/2 respectively.

proof sketch (spectral norm)If rank(B)≤k then ker(B) has dimension ≥p−k and so intersects span(v1,…,vk+1) nontrivially; take a unit w in the intersection, then ‖A−B‖2≥‖(A−B)w‖2=‖Aw‖2≥σk+12.

Theorem — Davis–Kahan sinΘ

Let A and Aˆ=A+E be symmetric, V,Vˆ the top-k eigenspaces, and δ=λk(A)−λk+1(A)>0 the eigengap. Then ‖sinΘ(V,Vˆ)‖≤2‖E‖δ. (A version with δ replaced by λk−λk+1 of the perturbed matrix holds too, which is what you can actually compute.)

Every consistency proof for a spectral method runs through this: PCA (122), spectral clustering (125), spectral methods for latent variable models (119), Isomap and Laplacian eigenmaps (126). The eigengap in the denominator is the fragile object — when eigenvalues cluster, subspace estimation is ill-posed even though the matrix estimate is fine.

Where it breaks

No gap, no guarantee. With δ≈0 the individual eigenvectors are unidentifiable but the subspace spanned by the tied block is still stable — so state results about subspaces, not vectors. A second trap: sinΘ controls the subspace, so any claim about the sign or ordering of loadings is unsupported (relevant to every “interpretation of PC2” in applied papers).

Convex Analysis I: Convexity, Smoothness, Curvature

Day 7 · 85 min

Which two numbers determine every convergence rate you will meet in Module 5?

Definitions

f is convex if f(λx+(1−λ)y)≤λf(x)+(1−λ)f(y); differentiably, iff f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)⊤(y−x); twice-differentiably, iff ∇2f⪰0.
f is L-smooth if ∇f is L-Lipschitz, equivalently ∇2f⪯LI, equivalently the descent lemma f(y)≤f(x)+∇f(x)⊤(y−x)+L2‖y−x‖2.
f is μ-strongly convex if f−μ2‖⋅‖2 is convex, equivalently ∇2f⪰μI, equivalently f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)⊤(y−x)+μ2‖y−x‖2.

Operations preserving convexity

Nonnegative combinations; pointwise supremum (hence max-type losses and every dual function); composition g∘A with affine A; h(f1,…,fk) with h convex and nondecreasing in arguments where fi is convex; partial minimization infyf(x,y) over a convex joint f; perspective tf(x/t). The supremum rule is the one to remember: it explains why the hinge, the max-margin objective, robust/DRO objectives and the Lagrange dual are all convex regardless of how ugly the inner object is.

Jensen: f convex ⇒f(𝔼X)≤𝔼f(X). Non-negativity of KL, the ELBO, Rao–Blackwell and the AM–GM inequality are all Jensen.

Quasi-convexity ({x:f(x)≤α} convex for all α) is much weaker: sublevel sets are convex but there is no descent lemma and no useful rate. It shows up in ratio objectives (Fisher discriminant, Rayleigh quotients) which are solved by eigen-decomposition, not by gradient methods.

Where it breaks

Strong convexity of the empirical objective is what regularization buys you, and it is λ that appears as μ — so rates like O(κlog(1/ϵ)) blow up exactly as λ→0, which is exactly the regime that is statistically optimal when n is large. Rate statements that ignore this coupling between λ and κ are misleading; the honest comparison fixes the statistical target and asks for compute to reach it (Lecture 61).

Convex Analysis II: Subgradients and Duality

Day 8 · 85 min

The lasso objective is not differentiable at zero — which is the whole point. What replaces the gradient, and what does the dual buy?

Definition — subdifferential

∂f(x)={g:f(y)≥f(x)+g⊤(y−x)∀y}. For convex f it is nonempty, compact and convex on the relative interior of the domain, and reduces to {∇f(x)} where f is differentiable. Optimality: x⋆∈argminf⟺0∈∂f(x⋆).

∂|x|={sign(x)} for x≠0 and [−1,1] at 0. Hence for 12(x−z)2+λ|x|, optimality reads x−z+λs=0, s∈∂|x|, giving the soft-thresholding operator x⋆=Sλ(z)=sign(z)(|z|−λ)+ — the single most reused formula in Modules 3–5. The interval-valued subdifferential at 0 is exactly why an interval of z maps to exactly zero: sparsity is a consequence of non-differentiability, not of the constraint's shape alone.

Fenchel conjugate

f*(y)=supx{y⊤x−f(x)}. Always convex (a sup of affine functions). Fenchel–Young: f(x)+f*(y)≥x⊤y with equality iff y∈∂f(x). Biconjugate: f**=f iff f is closed convex — so f** is the convex envelope in general, which is why the nuclear norm is “the” convex relaxation of rank on the spectral ball (Lecture 55).

Theorem — Lagrangian duality & KKT

For minf0(x) s.t. fi(x)≤0, Ax=b, set L(x,λ,ν)=f0+∑λifi+ν⊤(Ax−b) and g(λ,ν)=infxL. Then g is concave and g≤p⋆ always (weak duality). Under Slater's condition (a strictly feasible point for the nonlinear constraints, with the problem convex), d⋆=p⋆ (strong duality) and the dual optimum is attained. At optimum the KKT conditions hold: primal feasibility, dual feasibility λ⪰0, complementary slackness λifi(x)=0, and stationarity 0∈∂xL.

proof sketchWeak duality is one line: g(λ,ν)≤L(x⋆,λ,ν)≤f0(x⋆). Strong duality is a separating-hyperplane argument on the set {(u,t):∃x,fi(x)≤ui,f0(x)≤t}; Slater guarantees the separating hyperplane is non-vertical, which is exactly where it can fail.

SVM preview. The hard-margin primal min12‖w‖2 s.t. yi(w⊤xi+b)≥1 has dual maxα∑αi−12∑αiαjyiyjxi⊤xj s.t. α⪰0,∑αiyi=0. Complementary slackness says αi>0 only for points on the margin: support vectors are the complementary slackness condition. The inner product xi⊤xj is the only appearance of the data, which is the kernel trick (Lecture 74).

Where it breaks

Slater fails for equality-constrained-only nonconvex problems and for problems whose feasible set has empty relative interior; then a duality gap can be positive and KKT is neither necessary nor sufficient. Also, KKT is sufficient only under convexity — for nonconvex problems a KKT point can be a saddle (Lecture 71).

Information Theory for Learning

Day 8 · 85 min

Every minimax lower bound in this series is one of three inequalities. Here they are.

Definitions

H(X)=−∑plogp; differential h(X)=−∫plogp (not scale invariant, can be negative). KL(P‖Q)=∫logdPdQdP (require P≪Q). I(X;Y)=KL(PXY‖PXPY). Total variation ‖P−Q‖TV=supA|P(A)−Q(A)|=12∫|p−q|.

KL is non-negative by Jensen applied to −log; it is zero iff P=Q; it is not symmetric and violates the triangle inequality, so it is not a metric. Its chain rule KL(PXY‖QXY)=KL(PX‖QX)+𝔼XKL(PY|X‖QY|X) gives tensorization: KL(P⊗n‖Q⊗n)=nKL(P‖Q), the source of every √1/n in a lower bound.

Theorem — Pinsker

‖P−Q‖TV≤√12KL(P‖Q).

Direction matters: a small KL forces a small TV, but not conversely (KL can be infinite while TV is tiny, when supports differ slightly). For lower bounds we want the opposite direction — to show two hypotheses are hard to distinguish we upper-bound TV via KL, which Pinsker does.

Theorem — Fano

Let V→X→Vˆ be a Markov chain with V uniform on {1,…,M}. Then ℙ(Vˆ≠V)≥1−I(V;X)+log2logM. Combined with I(V;X)≤maxj,kKL(Pj‖Pk) (or the tighter 1M2∑j,kKL), this is the engine of Lecture 23.

proof sketchChain-rule the entropy H(V∣Vˆ) through the error indicator E=𝟙[Vˆ≠V]: H(V∣Vˆ)≤H(E)+ℙ(E)log(M−1), then use H(V∣Vˆ)=H(V)−I(V;Vˆ)≥logM−I(V;X) by data processing.

The recipe. To show no estimator beats rate rn: build M parameter values pairwise separated by 2rn in the loss metric (a packing), whose distributions are within KL≲logM of each other; Fano then says any estimator errs with constant probability. Estimation is thereby reduced to testing. The two hyperparameters — packing size and KL radius — trade off, and finding the sweet spot is the lower-bound proof.

f-divergences and variational forms. Df(P‖Q)=∫qf(p/q) recovers KL (f=tlogt), TV (f=12|t−1|), χ2 (f=(t−1)2), Hellinger. Each has a variational representation Df=supg{𝔼Pg−𝔼Qf*(g)}, which is where Donsker–Varadhan (KL(P‖Q)=supg{𝔼Pg−log𝔼Qeg}) comes from. DV is used verbatim in the PAC-Bayes proof (Lecture 35) and in mutual-information estimation.

MDL. Two-part codes: minM{L(M)+L(D∣M)}. With Kraft's inequality, code lengths are −log probabilities, so MDL is MAP with a specific prior; the normalized maximum likelihood refinement gives the minimax-regret code and reproduces BIC to O(1) (Lecture 111).

Where it breaks

Fano gives a bound in probability of error over the packing set. It bounds the minimax risk from below, not the risk at any particular θ; a specific estimator may beat the minimax rate on a large subset of the parameter space (this is the entire content of adaptivity, Lecture 59, and of superefficiency). Reporting a Fano bound as “no method can do better here” is a category error.

Exponential Families

Day 9 · 85 min

One parametric form covers Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson, gamma, categorical, MRFs and GLMs. What does that form buy?

Definition

pη(x)=h(x)exp{η⊤T(x)−A(η)}, with natural parameter η, sufficient statistic T, base measure h, and log-partition A(η)=log∫h(x)eη⊤T(x)dx on the natural parameter space 𝒩={η:A(η)<∞}, which is convex.

Theorem — A generates cumulants

On int(𝒩), A is C∞ and ∇A(η)=𝔼η[T(X)]=:μ,∇2A(η)=Covη[T(X)]⪰0. Hence A is convex, and strictly convex iff the family is minimal (no affine dependence among the Tj).

proofDifferentiate ∫heη⊤T−A=1 under the integral (justified by dominated convergence on the interior of 𝒩).

Consequences you will use constantly

  • Sufficiency. T(X1:n)=∑iT(Xi) is sufficient of fixed dimension — the Pitman–Koopman–Darmois theorem says (under regularity) exponential families are the only families with this property.
  • MLE. The likelihood is concave in η with ∇ℓ=T−∇A(η): a unique solution when T lies in the interior of the mean space, and no solution when it lands on the boundary (complete separation in logistic regression, Lecture 48).
  • Fisher information. I(η)=∇2A(η)=Cov(T): information is curvature is covariance. The CRLB is attained exactly here (Lecture 19).
  • Conjugacy. Prior p(η)∝exp{τ⊤η−νA(η)} gives a posterior in the same family with (τ,ν)→(τ+∑T(xi),ν+n): conjugate priors are pseudo-observations (Lecture 109).
  • Max entropy. Among all distributions with 𝔼T=μ, the entropy maximizer is the exponential family member with that mean — the constrained-optimization derivation of Gaussian, Boltzmann and Ising models.
Where it breaks

Curved exponential families (parameters constrained to a nonlinear submanifold of 𝒩) lose concavity of the likelihood. And the whole apparatus rests on A(η)<∞: for MRFs the partition function is #P-hard to evaluate (Lecture 99), so “A is convex and smooth” is true and computationally useless — hence pseudo-likelihood, contrastive divergence and score matching in Lecture 105.

Empirical Process Theory, Primer

Day 9 · 85 min

We need supf∈ℱ|ℙnf−ℙf| to be small. What controls a supremum over an infinite class?

Setup

ℙn=1n∑iδXi is the empirical measure; 𝔾nf=√n(ℙn−ℙ)f is the empirical process indexed by f∈ℱ. ℱ is Glivenko–Cantelli if ‖ℙn−ℙ‖ℱ→0 a.s., and Donsker if 𝔾n⇒ a Gaussian process in ℓ∞(ℱ).

Classical GC: for ℱ={𝟙(⋅≤t)}, supt|Fn(t)−F(t)|→0 a.s. Generalizing this from one indicator family to arbitrary ℱ is the technical content of Module 2.

Lemma — symmetrization

With εi i.i.d. Rademacher, independent of the data, 𝔼supf∈ℱ|ℙnf−ℙf|≤2𝔼supf∈ℱ|1n∑iεif(Xi)|=2ℜn(ℱ).

proofIntroduce a ghost sample X′ with the same law, write ℙf=𝔼′ℙn′f, pull the sup inside the expectation by Jensen, then note f(Xi)−f(Xi′) is symmetric so multiplying by εi leaves the law unchanged; split by the triangle inequality.

The payoff: the unknown ℙ has vanished. Rademacher complexity is computable from data, which is what makes the resulting bounds usable (Lecture 31).

Theorem — Dudley's entropy integral

For a sub-Gaussian process (Xf) with ‖Xf−Xg‖ψ2≤d(f,g), 𝔼supf∈ℱXf≲∫0diam(ℱ)√logN(ℱ,d,ε)dε. Applied to the Rademacher process with d= empirical L2: ℜn(ℱ)≲1√n∫0∞√logN2(ℱ,ε)dε.

Covering vs packing: M(2ε)≤N(ε)≤M(ε), so the two are interchangeable up to constants. Metric entropy logN is the currency: logN(𝔹d,ε)≍dlog(1/ε) for a Euclidean ball (parametric, gives √d/n), whereas logN≍ε−d/β for a β-Hölder ball (nonparametric, gives n−β/(2β+d) — Lecture 59). The rate is read off the entropy exponent.

Where it breaks

Dudley's integral diverges when logN(ε)≳ε−2, i.e. for classes with entropy exponent ≥2 — such classes are non-Donsker and the √n-rate genuinely fails; you get slower rates and must use local (rather than global) complexity. Localization — restricting to the ball where the excess risk actually lives — is what produces fast rates and is the modern refinement of everything on this page.

Function Spaces and Smoothness Classes

Day 10 · 75 min

“Assume f is smooth” is not a hypothesis until you say in which space. Which spaces, and what rate does each buy?

Definitions

Banach: complete normed space. Hilbert: Banach with an inner product inducing the norm — hence orthogonal projection, orthonormal bases, and Parseval. L2(μ) is the canonical example, and the only one in which “projection” means what you want it to.
Hölder Cβ: ⌊β⌋ derivatives with the top one (β−⌊β⌋)-Hölder. Sobolev Wm,2: weak derivatives to order m in L2; norm ∑k≤m‖f(k)‖22. Besov Bsp,q: a two-index refinement measuring smoothness in Lp with fine-tuning q; it is the natural home of wavelet thresholding because Besov norms are sequence norms on wavelet coefficients.

Note the last two rows: the only escapes from the curse of dimensionality are structural assumptions (additivity, low intrinsic dimension, sparsity). No estimator escapes it by cleverness alone — that is the content of the minimax lower bound.

Where it breaks

Sobolev and Hölder are global smoothness classes: a single kink anywhere puts f outside C1 and collapses the assumed rate, even if f is analytic elsewhere. Besov classes and wavelet methods exist precisely to handle spatially varying smoothness, and this is the real reason wavelet thresholding beats kernel smoothing on piecewise-smooth signals.

Five worked problems

  1. Sharpen the union bound. Show that for M sub-Gaussian variables with proxy σ2, 𝔼maxiXi≤σ√2logM, and that this is tight for i.i.d. Gaussians. Move: Jensen on the MGF of the max, then optimize λ — the same Chernoff step as Lecture 4.
  2. An ε-net for the sphere. Show N(Sd−1,‖⋅‖,ε)≤(1+2/ε)d by a volume argument, and deduce ‖A‖≤2supx∈𝒩1/2‖Ax‖. Move: volumetric packing plus the 12-net trick that recovers the sup from the net.
  3. Efron–Stein for the median. Bound the variance of the sample median and compare with the mean's. Move: bounding the effect of a single replacement.
  4. A non-implication. Construct Xn with Xn→0 in probability, 𝔼Xn→∞. Move: mass 1/n at height n2.
  5. Fano end-to-end. Prove the minimax rate for estimating a Gaussian mean in ℝd is σ√d/n, using a packing of the sphere from problem 2.
Common errors in published proofs
  • Applying a bound for a fixed h to the data-dependent hˆ — the single most common error. If hˆ depends on the sample, you need uniformity or stability, not a pointwise bound.
  • Union-bounding over a continuum without discretizing.
  • Treating an expectation bound as a high-probability bound (needs concentration) or vice versa (needs integration of the tail).
  • Hiding a dimension-dependent constant inside “≲”, then claiming dimension-free.
  • Using McDiarmid on an unbounded loss.
  • Reporting an asymptotic normality result at a sample size where the Berry–Esseen remainder O(n−1/2) dominates the quantity of interest.
