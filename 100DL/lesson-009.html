Deriving gradient descent in two lines

First-order Taylor expansion around \(\theta\): \(L(\theta+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx L(\theta)+\nabla L(\theta)\T\boldsymbol\delta\). To make this as small as possible with a step of fixed length \(\eta\), choose \(\boldsymbol\delta\) anti-parallel to the gradient, because the dot product is minimised at \(\cos\theta=-1\):

\[ \boldsymbol\delta = -\eta\,\frac{\nabla L}{\lVert\nabla L\rVert} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{in practice }\; \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t) \]

Two things are worth noticing. The approximation is only valid locally, so \(\eta\) must be small enough that you have not left the region where the linear model holds — that is the entire reason a learning rate exists. And we drop the normalisation, which means step size is proportional to gradient magnitude: steep regions take big steps, flat regions take tiny ones.

The largest stable learning rate has a formula

For a quadratic \(L(\theta)=\frac12\theta\T H\theta\), gradient descent gives \(\theta_{t+1}=(I-\eta H)\theta_t\). Along the eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the error is multiplied by \((1-\eta\lambda)\) every step. Convergence needs \(\lvert1-\eta\lambda\rvert\lt1\) for every eigenvalue, i.e.

\[ 0 \lt \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \]

and the slowest direction converges at rate \(1-\eta\lambda_{\min}\). Optimal \(\eta\) makes these balance, giving a convergence rate governed by the condition number \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\). Ill-conditioned problems are slow no matter what learning rate you pick — which is why normalisation (Days 32–33) and adaptive methods (Day 28) are not luxuries.

Convex vs non-convex, and why nobody panics

A function is convex if the line between any two points on the graph lies above it. Convex problems have a single global minimum and are solvable. Neural network losses are wildly non-convex: permuting hidden units gives you an exponential number of equivalent minima before you even start.

Empirically this matters much less than theory feared. In high dimensions, most critical points are saddles rather than poor local minima, and the many minima that do exist tend to have similar loss values. Mini-batch gradient noise also acts as a natural escape mechanism from shallow traps. The practical enemies are plateaus, ravines, and bad initialisation, not local minima.

Reading the loss-landscape pictures

Those beautiful 3D loss surface visualisations are 2D slices through a space with millions of dimensions, chosen along two random or specially-selected directions. They are useful intuition pumps and terrible evidence. When someone shows you a "flat minimum generalises better" plot, remember it is a projection.
