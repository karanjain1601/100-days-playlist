What deep learning actually is

A deep learning model is a function \(f_\theta:\R^{n}\to\R^{m}\) built by composing many simple differentiable pieces, whose parameters \(\theta\) are chosen by minimising a loss with gradient descent. That is the whole idea. Three claims are packed into it:

  • Composition beats hand-design. Stack enough simple transforms and the intermediate representations become useful features you never had to specify.
  • Differentiability is the price of admission. Every piece must have a derivative, because that is how credit gets assigned backwards through the stack.
  • Data replaces rules. You supply examples and an objective, not an algorithm.

AI vs ML vs DL, without the hand-waving

AI is the goal (systems that do things which look intelligent) and includes search, planning and hand-written expert systems. ML is the subset that learns from data. DL is the subset of ML using deep composed differentiable models trained end to end. Generative AI and foundation models are not a fourth ring — they are large DL models trained self-supervised on broad data and then adapted.

The history you should be able to tell in 90 seconds

The three-ingredient story

Every breakthrough on that line is one of: a better inductive bias (conv for images, attention for sequences), a trainability fix (ReLU, BatchNorm, residuals, Adam), or more scale (data, params, compute). When you meet a new architecture, ask which of the three it is. Most are one of them wearing a new name.

What you will be able to build by Day 100

  • An autodiff engine and an MLP that hits ~98% on MNIST, written from scratch in NumPy (Day 24).
  • A CNN above 94% on CIFAR-10 with a full modern training recipe, plus a fine-tuned detector or segmenter on your own data (Days 48–56).
  • A GPT trained from scratch — tokenizer, attention, training loop, sampling (Days 74–75) — then fine-tuned with QLoRA and aligned with DPO (Days 84–85).
  • A diffusion model, a VAE, a GAN, and a RAG pipeline with tool use (Days 89–94, 86-b).
  • A model that is actually served behind an API with monitoring (Day 99).

An ndarray is a pointer plus a shape plus strides

A Python list is an array of pointers to boxed objects scattered across the heap. A NumPy array is one contiguous block of raw numbers, plus metadata: shape, dtype, and strides (how many bytes to step to move one element along each axis). This is why NumPy is 50–200× faster, and it explains almost every surprising behaviour you will meet.

Broadcasting, as an algorithm

Broadcasting lets arrays of different shapes combine without explicit tiling. The rule is mechanical — run it right-to-left:

  1. Right-align the two shapes.
  2. Pad the shorter one on the left with 1s.
  3. For each axis: dimensions are compatible if they are equal, or one of them is 1.
  4. The output takes the max of the two along each axis. A 1 is "stretched" for free — no memory is allocated.
(3, 1, 5)+(4, 5)→ pad → (3, 1, 5)+(1, 4, 5)=(3, 4, 5)
Try it · broadcasting resolver
Pitfall · the silent broadcast

Subtracting predictions of shape (N, 1) from targets of shape (N,) gives you an (N, N) matrix, not an (N,) error vector. No exception is raised. Your loss becomes the mean of \(N^2\) numbers, it trains, it just never works. This bug is so common it gets its own line in the Day 36 checklist. Defend with assert pred.shape == target.shape before every loss.

Axis semantics — the thing to internalise

axis=k means "the axis that disappears" for reductions, and "the axis you move along" for everything else. For a batch of images (N, C, H, W): x.mean(axis=(2,3)) collapses height and width and leaves (N, C) — that is global average pooling, which you will meet again on Day 43.

import numpy as np

x = np.random.randn(8, 3, 32, 32).astype(np.float32)   # N, C, H, W

x.mean(axis=(2, 3)).shape        # (8, 3)   global average pool
x.mean(axis=(0, 2, 3)).shape     # (3,)     per-channel dataset mean
x.mean(axis=(0,2,3), keepdims=True).shape   # (1,3,1,1) -> broadcasts back

# keepdims is what makes normalisation a one-liner
mu  = x.mean(axis=(0,2,3), keepdims=True)
std = x.std(axis=(0,2,3), keepdims=True)
xn  = (x - mu) / (std + 1e-5)    # this is exactly BatchNorm's statistic (Day 32)

Vectorisation, measured

import numpy as np, time
A = np.random.randn(512, 512).astype(np.float32)
B = np.random.randn(512, 512).astype(np.float32)

t = time.perf_counter()
C = np.zeros((512, 512), dtype=np.float32)
for i in range(512):
    for j in range(512):
        C[i, j] = A[i] @ B[:, j]        # ~2.5 s
print("loops   ", time.perf_counter() - t)

t = time.perf_counter()
C2 = A @ B                              # ~5 ms  -> ~500x
print("vectored", time.perf_counter() - t)
print(np.allclose(C, C2, atol=1e-3))

The gap is BLAS: a tuned, cache-blocked, SIMD matmul running on all cores. Your job for the rest of the course is to phrase computations so BLAS (or CUDA) does the work. Whenever you find yourself writing a Python loop over a batch, stop and ask what array operation expresses it.

The dot product is the whole course in miniature

Algebraically \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\sum_i a_i b_i\). Geometrically \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta\). Read the second form as: how much of \(\mathbf a\) points along \(\mathbf b\), scaled by both lengths. Every neuron computes a dot product between its weight vector and its input, so a neuron is literally a template matcher — it fires most when the input points the same way as its weights.

Where you will meet this again
  • Day 15 — a neuron is \(\sigma(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x+b)\).
  • Day 42 — a convolution is a dot product slid across an image.
  • Day 60 — word similarity is a cosine between embeddings.
  • Day 70 — attention scores are \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k/\sqrt{d_k}\).
Drag the vectors · dot product, angle, projection

A matrix is a function, not a table

\(\mathbf y = W\mathbf x\) with \(W\in\R^{m\times n}\) sends \(\R^n\to\R^m\). Two equally valid readings, and you need both:

  • Row view: \(y_i=\mathbf w_{i,:}\cdot\mathbf x\). Output \(i\) is one dot product — one neuron, one template.
  • Column view: \(\mathbf y=\sum_j x_j\,\mathbf w_{:,j}\). The output is a weighted sum of \(W\)'s columns. The columns are where the input can reach; their span is the image of the transform.

The column view is the one that explains rank, bottlenecks and embeddings; the row view is the one that explains neurons. When you get confused about a layer, switch views.

Shape bookkeeping, once and for all

Pitfall · PyTorch stores weights transposed

nn.Linear(d_in, d_out) holds weight with shape (d_out, d_in) and computes x @ W.T + b. Everyone trips on this the first time they hand-initialise a weight or read one out. Print layer.weight.shape before you assume.

Norms, and which one to reach for

NormDefinitionWhere it shows up
\(L_1\)\(\sum_i \lvert x_i\rvert\)Sparsity-inducing penalty; MAE loss; pruning criteria
\(L_2\)\(\sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}\)Weight decay; gradient clipping by norm; distances
\(L_\infty\)\(\max_i \lvert x_i\rvert\)Adversarial perturbation budgets (Day 56); max-norm constraints
Frobenius\(\sqrt{\sum_{ij} W_{ij}^2}\)\(L_2\) for matrices; measuring how far a fine-tune moved the weights

\(L_1\) has a constant-magnitude gradient \(\operatorname{sign}(x)\) that does not vanish near zero, which is exactly why it drives coordinates to zero. \(L_2\)'s gradient \(2x\) shrinks as \(x\) shrinks, so it squashes everything but zeroes nothing. That single difference is the whole L1-vs-L2 sparsity story (Day 30).

Cosine similarity

\(\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\dfrac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}\in[-1,1]\). It throws away magnitude and keeps direction. Use it when vector length encodes something you do not care about — document length in TF-IDF, word frequency in embeddings, confidence in a retrieval index. Note that for unit vectors, \(\lVert\mathbf a-\mathbf b\rVert^2 = 2-2\cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\), so ranking by cosine and ranking by Euclidean distance are identical once you normalise. That identity is why vector databases normalise on insert.

Rank is the number of independent directions that survive

\(\operatorname{rank}(W)\) is the dimension of the span of \(W\)'s columns — the dimension of the output space the layer can actually reach. A \(1000\times1000\) matrix of rank 5 has a million numbers but only carries five directions' worth of information. This is not a curiosity: measured weight-update matrices in fine-tuning turn out to be close to low rank, which is the entire premise of LoRA (Day 84-b).

Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix does not rotate

\(A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v\). Applying \(A\) to \(\mathbf v\) only stretches it. Applying \(A\) repeatedly, \(A^k\mathbf v=\lambda^k\mathbf v\), so the largest \(\lvert\lambda\rvert\) dominates. That is exactly the mechanism behind exploding and vanishing gradients through a deep stack (Day 26): repeated multiplication by a Jacobian whose spectral radius is \(>1\) or \(\lt 1\).

If \(A\) has \(n\) independent eigenvectors, \(A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\), and \(A^k=Q\Lambda^k Q^{-1}\) — powers become trivial. For symmetric \(A\), \(Q\) is orthogonal and \(A=Q\Lambda Q\T\), the spectral theorem.

SVD: every matrix, no conditions attached

Eigendecomposition needs a square, diagonalisable matrix. SVD needs nothing:

\[ A = U\Sigma V\T,\qquad A\in\R^{m\times n},\; U\in\R^{m\times m},\;\Sigma\in\R^{m\times n},\;V\in\R^{n\times n} \]

with \(U,V\) orthogonal and \(\Sigma\) diagonal with \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0\). The geometric reading: every linear map is a rotation, then an axis-aligned scaling, then another rotation. That is all a matrix can ever do.

Low-rank approximation, and why it matters more than it looks

Truncate to the top \(k\) singular values: \(A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf v_i\T\). The Eckart–Young theorem says this is the best possible rank-\(k\) approximation in both Frobenius and spectral norm. You store \(k(m+n+1)\) numbers instead of \(mn\).

A (4096×4096)≈ Uk (4096×8)·VkT (8×4096) →16.7M → 65K params (0.4%)
Rank slider · low-rank reconstruction of an image

PCA falls out of SVD

Centre your data matrix \(X\in\R^{N\times d}\) so each column has mean zero. The covariance is \(C=\frac{1}{N-1}X\T X\). Take the SVD \(X=U\Sigma V\T\); then

\[ X\T X = V\Sigma\T U\T U\Sigma V\T = V\Sigma^2 V\T \]

so the columns of \(V\) are the eigenvectors of the covariance (the principal directions) and the eigenvalues are \(\sigma_i^2/(N-1)\) (the variance along each). Projecting is \(Z=XV_k\). You never need to form \(C\) — doing SVD directly on \(X\) is both faster and numerically better, because squaring the data squares the condition number.

Sanity check

Run PCA on MNIST. About 150 of the 784 components hold ~95% of the variance, and the top components look like blurry stroke templates. Keep this number in mind: real data lives on a much lower-dimensional manifold than its ambient dimension, which is why learning is possible at all.

Then plot the singular value spectrum on a log scale — the shape of that curve is the same diagnostic you will use to choose a LoRA rank on Day 84-b.

One rule matters more than the rest

The chain rule. \((f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\). Backpropagation is the chain rule applied to a graph, evaluated in a specific order, with intermediate results cached. If you are fluent in the chain rule, Day 21 is easy. If you are not, Day 21 is impossible. Practise until it is automatic.

The gradient

For \(f:\R^n\to\R\), \(\nabla f=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\right]\T\). The directional derivative along a unit vector \(\mathbf u\) is \(D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=\lVert\nabla f\rVert\cos\theta\), which is maximised at \(\theta=0\). So the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest slope. Gradient descent steps along \(-\nabla f\) for exactly this reason — it is a one-line consequence of the dot-product formula from Lesson 3.

Derive it yourself — sigmoid

\(\sigma(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\). Write it as \((1+e^{-x})^{-1}\) and apply power + chain:

\[ \sigma'(x) = -(1+e^{-x})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-x}) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2} \]

Now the trick that makes it cheap. Note \(\frac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}} = 1-\sigma(x)\), so

\[ \boxed{\;\sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)\bigl(1-\sigma(x)\bigr)\;} \]

The derivative is expressible in the output. Every framework exploits this: the backward pass reuses the cached forward activation instead of recomputing exponentials. Same trick for tanh: \(\tanh'(x)=1-\tanh^2(x)\).

Pitfall · the number 0.25

\(\sigma'\) peaks at \(x=0\) with value \(0.25\), and decays towards 0 in both tails. Chain 10 sigmoid layers and the best case gradient factor is \(0.25^{10}\approx 10^{-6}\). This single number is why deep sigmoid networks were untrainable for two decades. It is the whole content of Day 26.

ReLU and the derivative that does not exist

\(\relu(x)=\max(0,x)\), so \(\relu'(x)=1\) for \(x\gt0\), \(0\) for \(x\lt0\), and undefined at exactly \(x=0\). Frameworks pick a subgradient — PyTorch returns 0 — and it does not matter, because hitting exactly 0.0 in float arithmetic has measure zero. The important property is that the gradient is 1, not 0.25: it neither shrinks nor grows the signal as it passes backwards. That is why ReLU unlocked depth.

Derive it yourself — MSE for one linear neuron

\(L=\frac{1}{2}(y-\hat y)^2\) with \(\hat y=\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf x+b\). Chain rule:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}=(\hat y-y),\qquad \frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial w_j}=x_j \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_j}=(\hat y - y)\,x_j \]

In vector form \(\nabla_{\mathbf w}L=(\hat y-y)\mathbf x\) and \(\partial L/\partial b=(\hat y-y)\). Read it as: error × input. Large input features get large updates; that is why feature scaling matters so much.

Gradient descent · watch the learning rate decide everything

The Jacobian

For \(\mathbf f:\R^n\to\R^m\), the Jacobian \(J\in\R^{m\times n}\) has \(J_{ij}=\partial f_i/\partial x_j\). Row \(i\) is the gradient of output \(i\). It is the best linear approximation of \(\mathbf f\) near a point: \(\mathbf f(\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx \mathbf f(\mathbf x)+J\boldsymbol\delta\).

Composition is matrix multiplication: for \(\mathbf z=\mathbf g(\mathbf y)\), \(\mathbf y=\mathbf f(\mathbf x)\),

\[ J_{\mathbf z\mathbf x}=J_{\mathbf z\mathbf y}\,J_{\mathbf y\mathbf x} \]

An \(L\)-layer network is therefore a product of \(L\) Jacobians. Whether that product explodes or vanishes depends on the singular values of each factor — Lesson 4 meets Lesson 6, and together they explain Day 26.

The vector-Jacobian product is why autodiff is cheap

Nobody ever materialises a Jacobian in deep learning. For a network with \(P=10^9\) parameters and a scalar loss, the Jacobian of the loss w.r.t. an intermediate activation of size \(n\) is \(1\times n\) — a row vector. Backprop propagates such row vectors:

\[ \underbrace{\bar{\mathbf x}\T}_{1\times n} = \underbrace{\bar{\mathbf y}\T}_{1\times m}\underbrace{J_{\mathbf y \mathbf x}}_{m\times n} \]

where \(\bar{\mathbf v}\) denotes \(\partial L/\partial \mathbf v\). Each layer only needs a rule for "given the gradient flowing in, produce the gradient flowing out" — it never builds \(J\). For most layers that rule is a cheap closed form:

ForwardJacobian (conceptually)VJP actually computed
\(\mathbf y=W\mathbf x\)\(W\)gx = gy @ W and gW = gy.T @ x
\(\mathbf y=\mathbf a\odot\mathbf x\)\(\diag(\mathbf a)\)gx = gy * a
\(\mathbf y=\relu(\mathbf x)\)\(\diag(\mathbb 1[x\gt0])\)gx = gy * (x > 0)
\(\mathbf y=\softmax(\mathbf x)\)\(\diag(\mathbf y)-\mathbf y\mathbf y\T\) (dense!)gx = y * (gy - (gy*y).sum())

Note the softmax row. Its Jacobian is a full \(K\times K\) matrix, but the VJP is a two-line elementwise computation. That gap — dense Jacobian, cheap VJP — is the entire performance argument for reverse-mode autodiff.

Worked: ∂loss/∂W for a 2-layer network

Forward, for a single example, with \(\mathbf x\in\R^{d}\):

\[ \mathbf z^{(1)} = W^{(1)}\mathbf x + \mathbf b^{(1)},\quad \mathbf a = \relu(\mathbf z^{(1)}),\quad \mathbf z^{(2)} = W^{(2)}\mathbf a + \mathbf b^{(2)},\quad L=\tfrac12\lVert \mathbf z^{(2)}-\mathbf y\rVert^2 \]

Backward, right to left, naming each intermediate gradient:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar{\mathbf z}^{(2)} &= \mathbf z^{(2)} - \mathbf y \\[2pt] \bar W^{(2)} &= \bar{\mathbf z}^{(2)}\,\mathbf a\T &&\text{outer product: } (m\times1)(1\times h)=m\times h\;\checkmark\\[2pt] \bar{\mathbf b}^{(2)} &= \bar{\mathbf z}^{(2)} \\[2pt] \bar{\mathbf a} &= {W^{(2)}}\T\,\bar{\mathbf z}^{(2)} &&\text{transpose sends the signal back}\\[2pt] \bar{\mathbf z}^{(1)} &= \bar{\mathbf a}\odot\mathbb 1[\mathbf z^{(1)}\gt 0] &&\text{gate through the ReLU mask}\\[2pt] \bar W^{(1)} &= \bar{\mathbf z}^{(1)}\,\mathbf x\T,\qquad \bar{\mathbf b}^{(1)} = \bar{\mathbf z}^{(1)} \end{aligned} \]
The pattern to memorise

Three motifs repeat for every linear layer in every architecture you will ever write: weight gradient = outer product of (gradient out, input in); input gradient = \(W\T\) times gradient out; elementwise nonlinearity = multiply by its local derivative. Learn these three and you can hand-derive backprop for anything.

Hessians and curvature — what you need, no more

\(H_{ij}=\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j\). Its eigenvalues describe curvature: all positive is a local minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle. Two facts carry over into everything that follows:

  • The condition number \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) controls how badly gradient descent zig-zags. A long thin valley has huge \(\kappa\), and plain SGD bounces across it instead of running down it. That is what momentum fixes (Day 28).
  • In high dimensions, saddles vastly outnumber local minima. For a random critical point, needing all \(P\) eigenvalues to share a sign is exponentially unlikely. So the classical fear of "getting stuck in a local minimum" is mostly misplaced; plateaus around saddles are the real problem.

Full Newton steps need \(H^{-1}\), which is \(O(P^3)\) — impossible at \(P=10^9\). Adam's per-parameter scaling is a cheap diagonal caricature of this idea, and that is as close to second order as mainstream deep learning gets.

Why a probabilist's vocabulary

Every classifier you build outputs a distribution, not a label. Every loss you use is a negative log-likelihood in disguise. Every regulariser is a prior. Getting comfortable here makes the rest of the course feel like one idea instead of fifty.

DistributionParametersWhere it appears in DL
Bernoulli\(p\)Binary classification output; dropout masks; MNIST-style binarised pixels
Categorical\(\mathbf p\), \(\sum p_k=1\)Softmax output; next-token distribution in every LLM
Gaussian\(\mu,\sigma^2\)Weight init; VAE latents; diffusion noise; the reason MSE is a likelihood
Uniform\(a,b\)Random augmentation params; some inits; sampling without a preference

Expectation and variance, and their two useful identities

\(\E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x)\) or \(\int x\,p(x)\,\d x\). Linearity holds unconditionally: \(\E[aX+bY]=a\E[X]+b\E[Y]\), even for dependent variables. Variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=\E[X^2]-\E[X]^2\), and it does not add unless the variables are uncorrelated:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(aX)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X),\qquad \operatorname{Var}\!\Big(\sum_i X_i\Big)=\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(X_i)\ \ \text{if independent} \]
Why these two lines matter on Day 25-b

A neuron computes \(z=\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j x_j\). If the \(w_j\) are independent with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(w)\) and the \(x_j\) are independent with variance \(\operatorname{Var}(x)\), then \(\operatorname{Var}(z)=n\operatorname{Var}(w)\operatorname{Var}(x)\). To keep activation variance constant through depth you need \(\operatorname{Var}(w)=1/n\). That is Xavier initialisation, derived from these two identities and nothing else.

Joint, marginal, conditional, Bayes

\[ p(x,y)=p(x\mid y)\,p(y),\qquad p(x)=\sum_y p(x,y),\qquad p(y\mid x)=\frac{p(x\mid y)\,p(y)}{p(x)} \]

Read Bayes' rule as an update: the posterior \(p(y\mid x)\) is the prior \(p(y)\) reweighted by how well each hypothesis explains the evidence, the likelihood \(p(x\mid y)\). The denominator is only a normaliser.

Pitfall · base rates

A test with 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity for a disease with prevalence 0.1% gives a positive predictive value of about 9%. \(p(\text{sick}\mid+)=\frac{0.99\times0.001}{0.99\times0.001+0.01\times0.999}\approx0.09\). Every time you evaluate a model on a rare class, this arithmetic is what is really going on. It is the reason accuracy is a useless metric on imbalanced data (Day 39).

The law of large numbers is the reason mini-batching works

The sample mean of i.i.d. draws converges to the true mean, with standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{B}\). A mini-batch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full-dataset gradient, and its noise shrinks only as \(1/\sqrt{B}\). Doubling batch size costs 2× compute and buys \(\sqrt2\) less noise — that diminishing return is precisely why gigantic batches stop helping (Day 27).

Maximum likelihood, and where every loss comes from

Given data \(\mathcal D=\{(\mathbf x_i,y_i)\}\) and a model \(p_\theta(y\mid\mathbf x)\), the likelihood is \(\prod_i p_\theta(y_i\mid \mathbf x_i)\). Products underflow, so we maximise the log, and by convention minimise its negative:

\[ \theta^\star=\argmin_\theta\; -\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log p_\theta(y_i\mid\mathbf x_i) \]

Now instantiate the model and watch familiar losses appear:

Assumed output distribution\(-\log p_\theta(y\mid x)\) becomesEveryone calls it
Gaussian, fixed \(\sigma\)\(\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}(y-\hat y)^2 + \text{const}\)MSE
Laplace\(\frac{1}{b}\lvert y-\hat y\rvert + \text{const}\)MAE / L1
Bernoulli\(-[y\log\hat p+(1-y)\log(1-\hat p)]\)Binary cross-entropy
Categorical\(-\log \hat p_{y}\)Cross-entropy / NLL
The unlock

Losses are not arbitrary design choices you memorise. Choosing a loss is choosing a noise model. MSE assumes Gaussian errors, which is why it is so sensitive to outliers — a Gaussian assigns them near-zero probability, so they dominate the log-likelihood. Switching to MAE is switching to a heavier-tailed Laplace assumption. That is the real content of Day 20.

MAP adds a prior — and that prior is your regulariser

\[ \theta_{\text{MAP}}=\argmax_\theta\; p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta) = \argmin_\theta\Big[-\log p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)-\log p(\theta)\Big] \]

Put a zero-mean Gaussian prior \(p(\theta)=\mathcal N(0,\tau^2 I)\) on the weights. Then \(-\log p(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\theta\rVert_2^2+\text{const}\), and you have just derived L2 regularisation with \(\lambda=1/(2\tau^2)\). A Laplace prior gives L1. Weight decay is not a hack; it is a prior.

Entropy, cross-entropy, KL — three related quantities

QuantityFormulaIn one sentence
Entropy \(H(p)\)\(-\sum_x p(x)\log p(x)\)The average bits needed to encode samples from \(p\), using the best code for \(p\).
Cross-entropy \(H(p,q)\)\(-\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)\)The average bits when you encode samples from \(p\) using a code built for \(q\).
KL \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\)\(\sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\)The excess bits you waste for being wrong. \(=H(p,q)-H(p)\ge0\).
Why we minimise cross-entropy and not KL

Training minimises \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\Vert p_\theta) = H(p_{\text{data}},p_\theta) - H(p_{\text{data}})\). The second term does not depend on \(\theta\) — it is a property of the dataset. So minimising KL and minimising cross-entropy give identical gradients. We use cross-entropy because it is one term shorter.

For one-hot labels, \(p_{\text{data}}\) puts all mass on the true class \(c\), so \(H(p,q)=-\log q_c\). Cross-entropy loss for a single example is just the negative log of the probability you assigned to the correct answer. Assign 0.9 → loss 0.105. Assign 0.1 → loss 2.303. Assign 0.001 → loss 6.9. It punishes confident mistakes brutally, which is exactly what you want.

Forward vs reverse KL — mode covering vs mode seeking

KL is not symmetric, and the asymmetry has real consequences:

  • Forward, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\) — the expectation is over \(p\). Wherever \(p\) has mass and \(q\) does not, the term blows up. So \(q\) is forced to cover every mode of \(p\), smearing over the gaps. Mode-covering. This is what maximum likelihood does, and why an under-trained generative model produces blurry averages.
  • Reverse, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)\) — the expectation is over \(q\). \(q\) is only penalised where it puts mass, so it can safely ignore modes and pile onto one. Mode-seeking. This is what variational inference optimises, and it is the KL penalty in RLHF (Day 85) keeping the policy from wandering off the reference model.

Jensen–Shannon symmetrises: \(\mathrm{JS}(p\Vert q)=\frac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert m)+\frac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert m)\) with \(m=\frac12(p+q)\). It is bounded and always finite, which is why the original GAN objective lands on it (Day 90) — and why its flat gradients when \(p\) and \(q\) do not overlap motivated Wasserstein GANs.

Deriving gradient descent in two lines

First-order Taylor expansion around \(\theta\): \(L(\theta+\boldsymbol\delta)\approx L(\theta)+\nabla L(\theta)\T\boldsymbol\delta\). To make this as small as possible with a step of fixed length \(\eta\), choose \(\boldsymbol\delta\) anti-parallel to the gradient, because the dot product is minimised at \(\cos\theta=-1\):

\[ \boldsymbol\delta = -\eta\,\frac{\nabla L}{\lVert\nabla L\rVert} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{in practice }\; \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla L(\theta_t) \]

Two things are worth noticing. The approximation is only valid locally, so \(\eta\) must be small enough that you have not left the region where the linear model holds — that is the entire reason a learning rate exists. And we drop the normalisation, which means step size is proportional to gradient magnitude: steep regions take big steps, flat regions take tiny ones.

The largest stable learning rate has a formula

For a quadratic \(L(\theta)=\frac12\theta\T H\theta\), gradient descent gives \(\theta_{t+1}=(I-\eta H)\theta_t\). Along the eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the error is multiplied by \((1-\eta\lambda)\) every step. Convergence needs \(\lvert1-\eta\lambda\rvert\lt1\) for every eigenvalue, i.e.

\[ 0 \lt \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \]

and the slowest direction converges at rate \(1-\eta\lambda_{\min}\). Optimal \(\eta\) makes these balance, giving a convergence rate governed by the condition number \(\kappa=\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\). Ill-conditioned problems are slow no matter what learning rate you pick — which is why normalisation (Days 32–33) and adaptive methods (Day 28) are not luxuries.

Convex vs non-convex, and why nobody panics

A function is convex if the line between any two points on the graph lies above it. Convex problems have a single global minimum and are solvable. Neural network losses are wildly non-convex: permuting hidden units gives you an exponential number of equivalent minima before you even start.

Empirically this matters much less than theory feared. In high dimensions, most critical points are saddles rather than poor local minima, and the many minima that do exist tend to have similar loss values. Mini-batch gradient noise also acts as a natural escape mechanism from shallow traps. The practical enemies are plateaus, ravines, and bad initialisation, not local minima.

Reading the loss-landscape pictures

Those beautiful 3D loss surface visualisations are 2D slices through a space with millions of dimensions, chosen along two random or specially-selected directions. They are useful intuition pumps and terrible evidence. When someone shows you a "flat minimum generalises better" plot, remember it is a projection.

The four formats, and the one number that matters for each

Machine epsilon for float32 is \(\approx1.19\times10^{-7}\): the smallest \(\varepsilon\) with \(1+\varepsilon\neq1\). Consequently np.float32(1.0) + np.float32(1e-8) == 1.0. In float16, \(\varepsilon\approx10^{-3}\), so adding a learning-rate-sized update to a weight can be a complete no-op — which is why optimizer states and master weights are kept in fp32 even when the forward pass is half precision.

The log-sum-exp trick

Softmax needs \(e^{z_k}\). With \(z=1000\), \(e^{1000}=\infty\) in float32 and you get NaN. Exploit the shift invariance of softmax — subtracting a constant from all logits leaves the result unchanged:

\[ \softmax(\mathbf z)_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = \frac{e^{z_k-c}}{\sum_j e^{z_j-c}},\qquad \text{choose } c=\max_j z_j \]

Now the largest exponent is \(e^0=1\) — no overflow ever. The underflowing terms become \(e^{\text{large negative}}\to0\), which is harmless. Similarly:

\[ \log\sum_j e^{z_j} = c + \log\sum_j e^{z_j-c} \] 
import numpy as np

def softmax_naive(z):
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

def softmax_stable(z):
    z = z - z.max()               # the entire fix
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

z = np.array([1000., 1001., 1002.], dtype=np.float32)
softmax_naive(z)     # array([nan, nan, nan])
softmax_stable(z)    # array([0.09003057, 0.24472848, 0.66524094])
Break it yourself · naive vs stable softmax

Never compute log(softmax(x)) as two steps

If softmax returns exactly 0.0 for some class (perfectly possible after underflow) then \(\log 0=-\infty\) and the loss is inf, then NaN after multiplication by a zero label. Fuse the operations instead:

\[ \log\softmax(\mathbf z)_k = z_k - c - \log\sum_j e^{z_j-c} \]

No division, no log of a possibly-zero number. This is why every framework ships a fused version and why the API surface looks the way it does:

Use thisFeed itNot this
nn.CrossEntropyLoss()raw logits + integer class indicessoftmax then NLLLoss then log
nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()raw logits + 0/1 targetssigmoid then BCELoss
F.log_softmax(z, dim=-1)raw logitstorch.log(F.softmax(z))
Pitfall · the double softmax

Applying softmax in your model's forward and using CrossEntropyLoss is the most common bug of Phase 2. It does not crash. It squashes the effective logits into \([0,1]\), flattens the gradients, and your model trains to about 60% on MNIST and stops. If accuracy plateaus embarrassingly low, check this first.

The BCE-with-logits stabilisation

\(-\log\sigma(x) = \log(1+e^{-x})\), which overflows for very negative \(x\). The stable form used inside every framework is

\[ \operatorname{BCE}(x,y)=\max(x,0)-x\,y+\log\!\left(1+e^{-\lvert x\rvert}\right) \]

Verify it by hand for \(x\gg0\) and \(x\ll0\): in both cases the exponential argument is negative, so it can never overflow. Small, elegant, and it is doing work for you on every binary classification you will ever run.

Catastrophic cancellation

Subtracting two nearly-equal floats destroys significant digits. The classic example is variance: \(\E[X^2]-\E[X]^2\) is algebraically correct and numerically terrible when the mean is large relative to the spread. Use a two-pass or Welford algorithm instead. The same issue is why \(\varepsilon\) inside a normalisation layer goes inside the square root, \(\frac{x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\varepsilon}}\), not outside.

einsum: the one API worth memorising

Index notation, executed. Repeated indices are summed; indices absent from the output are summed away.

torch.einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B)          # matmul
torch.einsum('bij,bjk->bik', A, B)       # batched matmul
torch.einsum('bi,bi->b', a, b)           # per-row dot product
torch.einsum('bi,bj->bij', a, b)         # batched outer product
torch.einsum('bhqd,bhkd->bhqk', q, k)    # ATTENTION SCORES (Day 70)
torch.einsum('bhqk,bhkd->bhqd', attn, v) # attention output

Those last two lines are the core of every transformer you will build. Writing them in einsum rather than a stack of transpose/reshape calls makes the shapes self-documenting, which matters enormously once you have four dimensions in play.

q (B, H, Q, D)×k (B, H, K, D) → sum over D →scores (B, H, Q, K)

In-place ops and the two ways they bite

Methods ending in an underscore (add_, relu_, clamp_) modify memory directly and save allocation. They are safe when the overwritten value is not needed by the backward pass — relu_ is fine because ReLU's derivative depends on the sign of its output too. They are unsafe otherwise, and PyTorch will tell you loudly:

RuntimeError: one of the variables needed for gradient computation
has been modified by an inplace operation

The second bite is silent aliasing: b = a[0] is a view, so b += 1 changes a. If you meant a copy, say a[0].clone().

The one axis that explains the differences

Eager execution runs operations immediately, so you can print tensors and use a debugger. Graph execution builds a symbolic graph first, then compiles and optimises it — faster, but harder to inspect. PyTorch is eager with optional compilation (torch.compile). The Dataset / DataLoader contract

A Dataset answers two questions: how many items are there (__len__), and give me item \(i\) (__getitem__). A DataLoader handles everything else: shuffling, batching, parallel workers, and pinned memory. Keep the split of responsibility clean and the rest is easy.

from torch.utils.data import Dataset, DataLoader

class ImageCSV(Dataset):
    def __init__(self, df, root, transform=None):
        self.df, self.root, self.transform = df.reset_index(drop=True), root, transform

    def __len__(self):
        return len(self.df)

    def __getitem__(self, i):
        row = self.df.iloc[i]
        img = Image.open(f"{self.root}/{row.filename}").convert("RGB")
        if self.transform:
            img = self.transform(img)          # augmentation happens HERE, per worker
        return img, int(row.label)

loader = DataLoader(
    ds, batch_size=64, shuffle=True,
    num_workers=8,          # CPU processes decoding + augmenting in parallel
    pin_memory=True,        # page-locked staging buffer -> faster host->GPU copy
    persistent_workers=True,# don't respawn every epoch
    prefetch_factor=4,      # batches queued per worker
    drop_last=True,         # keeps BatchNorm happy; avoids a size-1 final batch
)

collate_fn: for when items are not the same size

The default collate stacks samples, which requires identical shapes. Variable-length text or audio needs padding, and padding needs a mask so the model can ignore it:

def pad_collate(batch):
    seqs, labels = zip(*batch)
    lens = torch.tensor([len(s) for s in seqs])
    padded = nn.utils.rnn.pad_sequence(seqs, batch_first=True, padding_value=0)
    mask = torch.arange(padded.size(1))[None, :] < lens[:, None]   # True = real token
    return padded, mask, torch.tensor(labels)

Carry that mask everywhere. Forgetting it in an attention layer means the model attends to padding, which is a bug that shows up as mysteriously worse validation loss with longer sequences.

Splitting, and the four ways to leak

Pitfall · leakage, in order of how often it happens
  1. Normalising before splitting. Computing mean/std on the full dataset lets test statistics into training. Fit scalers on train only, then apply to val and test.
  2. Group leakage. Multiple photos of the same patient, product or person split across train and test. Your model memorises the entity, not the task. Use GroupKFold.
  3. Temporal leakage. Random splits on time series let the model see the future. Always split by time, and never shuffle before a time-based split (Day 67-b).
  4. Test-set tuning. Choosing hyperparameters on the test set. That is now a validation set and your reported number is optimistic. Three splits exist for a reason.

Use stratification for classification so class proportions are preserved in each split — critical when a class has 30 examples and a random split could give the val set two of them.

When the data does not fit in RAM

  • Memory-map it. np.memmap or np.load(..., mmap_mode='r') reads pages on demand.
  • Shard it. Pack into chunked formats — WebDataset tar shards, Parquet, HDF5, or LMDB. Sequential reads beat millions of small file opens by an order of magnitude.
  • Stream it. Use an IterableDataset with a shuffle buffer (the same trick TFRecord/WebDataset use) when the dataset genuinely never lands on local disk.

Bias and variance, decomposed

For squared loss, expected test error at a point decomposes exactly:

\[ \E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}\!\big(\hat f(x)\big)}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}} \]

Bias is systematic error from a model too simple to represent the truth (underfitting). Variance is sensitivity to which training set you happened to draw (overfitting). The third term is noise in the labels themselves, and no model can beat it — which is why knowing your task's human error rate matters before you chase another 1%.

Gradient boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM)Tabular data, full stopStill beats deep nets on most tables — see Day 96-b
When NOT to use deep learning
  • Tabular data with fewer than ~50k rows. Gradient boosting will win, train in 30 seconds, and be easier to explain.
  • You have <1000 labelled examples and no pretrained model. Either find a pretrained backbone or use classical ML.
  • Hard interpretability requirements. Credit, medical triage, anything with a regulator. A 5-node decision tree you can defend beats a black box you cannot.
  • The relationship is genuinely linear. Do not bring a transformer to a regression fight.
  • Latency budget in the low milliseconds on CPU. Possible with deep nets, but check whether you need one at all.

Cross-validation and how to report honestly

k-fold splits the data into \(k\) parts, trains \(k\) times, and averages. Use it when data is scarce and training is cheap — which describes classical ML, not deep learning, where a single fixed split plus multiple seeds is the norm. Whatever you do, report variance: a single number with no error bar is not a result. Three seeds and a standard deviation is the minimum bar for a claim.
