Sequence Data & Why Feedforward Nets Fail

Three properties an MLP cannot handle

Variable length. A dense layer has a fixed input dimension. Sentences do not. You can pad to a maximum, but then a 5-word sentence and a 200-word sentence occupy the same tensor and the model must learn to ignore the difference.

Order matters. "The dog bit the man" and "The man bit the dog" contain identical words. Bag-of-words representations cannot tell them apart, and a flattened MLP treats position 3 and position 8 as unrelated coordinates — exactly the problem from Lesson 47, one dimension down.

Long-range dependency. In "The keys that I left on the table in the kitchen next to the bowl of fruit are missing", the verb agrees with a noun eleven words back. Any model with a fixed local window simply cannot see it.

ShapeInput → outputExample
One-to-manyFixed → sequenceImage captioning
Many-to-oneSequence → fixedSentiment classification
Many-to-many, aligned\(T\) → \(T\)Part-of-speech tagging, named-entity recognition
Many-to-many, unaligned\(T\) → \(T'\)Translation, summarisation, speech recognition

The last row is the hard one and it is what Lesson 74 exists to solve. When input and output lengths differ and the correspondence between them is unknown, you need an architecture that can decide for itself when to stop reading and start writing.

Padding, masking, packing

Batching requires a rectangular tensor, so short sequences get padded. The mask that records which positions are real is not optional bookkeeping — forget it and it corrupts your results in ways that look like a modelling problem.

from torch.nn.utils.rnn import pad_sequence, pack_padded_sequence

seqs = [torch.tensor(s) for s in batch]                 # varying lengths
lens = torch.tensor([len(s) for s in seqs])
x = pad_sequence(seqs, batch_first=True, padding_value=0)   # (B, T_max)
mask = (torch.arange(x.size(1))[None, :] < lens[:, None])   # (B, T_max) True = real

# masked mean pooling — the version that is actually correct
def masked_mean(h, mask):                                # h (B,T,D)
    m = mask.unsqueeze(-1).float()
    return (h * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1).clamp(min=1e-9)

# packing lets cuDNN skip padded steps entirely
packed = pack_padded_sequence(x_emb, lens.cpu(), batch_first=True, enforce_sorted=False)
Pitfall · three ways padding corrupts a model

Mean pooling over padding. h.mean(dim=1) averages in the pad vectors, so a 5-token sentence padded to 200 has its signal diluted 40×. Short examples then perform mysteriously worse than long ones.

Loss on pad positions. The model gets gradient for predicting the pad token, and since padding is often the majority of the tensor, it dominates the loss. Use ignore_index=pad_id in CrossEntropyLoss.

Attention over padding. In Phase 6 this becomes the single most common transformer bug — every real token attends to padding and the softmax redistributes probability mass onto positions that carry nothing. Mask the scores to \(-\infty\) before the softmax, never zero them after.

Unicode first, because it bites silently

"café" can be five code points (é as U+00E9) or six (e followed by a combining accent U+0301). They render identically and compare unequal. Without normalisation your vocabulary contains both and your model learns them as unrelated tokens. Apply NFC (canonical composition) or NFKC (which also folds compatibility forms like ﬁ → fi and full-width characters) before anything else.

The three levels, and why subword won

LevelVocabularySequence lengthThe fatal problem
WordUnbounded — every inflection, typo and name is distinctShortOut-of-vocabulary. Every unseen word becomes <unk>, which is exactly the rare word carrying the meaning.
Character~1005–8× longerNo OOV ever, but the model must relearn morphology from scratch and attention cost is quadratic in length.
SubwordFixed, 30k–256kModerateNone fatal. Common words stay whole; rare words decompose into known pieces.

Subword tokenization gives you a closed vocabulary with no OOV: "unbelievability" becomes un + believ + ability, all of which the model has seen thousands of times in other words.

Byte-Pair Encoding, by hand

Start with characters. Repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair and merge it into a new symbol. Corpus: low ×5, lower ×2, newest ×6, widest ×3.

Step 1. Count pairs. e s appears 6+3=9 times — the most frequent. Merge → es.
Step 2. Now es t appears 9 times. Merge → est.
Step 3. l o appears 5+2=7 times. Merge → lo.
Step 4. lo w appears 7 times. Merge → low.

Four merges and the frequent unit low is a single token, while est is available as a reusable suffix. Continue for 30,000 merges on a real corpus and you have a vocabulary that spends short tokens on common material and composes everything else. The merge list is ordered, and applying it in the same order at inference is what makes the tokenizer deterministic.

The three algorithms in use

AlgorithmMerge criterionUsed by
BPEHighest raw pair frequencyGPT-2/3/4, Llama, most decoder-only models
WordPieceHighest \(\dfrac{P(ab)}{P(a)P(b)}\) — pointwise mutual information, not raw countBERT, DistilBERT
UnigramStart with a large vocabulary and prune the tokens whose removal costs least likelihoodT5, ALBERT, XLNet, most SentencePiece models

WordPiece's ratio criterion prefers pairs that co-occur more than chance would predict, so it avoids merging a rare token onto a very common one purely because the common one is everywhere. Unigram is the only one that is genuinely probabilistic, which lets it produce multiple segmentations with probabilities — the basis of subword regularisation, a real augmentation technique for low-resource translation.

SentencePiece is orthogonal to all three: it is the tooling that treats the raw string, spaces included, as the input, encoding space as the visible marker ▁. That means the tokenizer is fully reversible and needs no language-specific pre-tokenizer — essential for Chinese, Japanese and Thai, which do not delimit words with spaces.

Byte-level BPE (GPT-2 onward) runs BPE over raw UTF-8 bytes rather than characters. The base vocabulary is exactly 256 symbols, so any possible input — emoji, corrupted bytes, code, binary — is representable and nothing is ever OOV, at the cost of unusual characters costing several tokens each.

Tokenization decides things you would not expect

Cost and fairness. The same sentence takes roughly 1.0× tokens in English, 2× in Spanish, and 3–5× in Hindi, Thai or Burmese, because the vocabulary was fit on English-heavy data. Since APIs bill per token and context windows are counted in tokens, speakers of those languages pay several times more for the same content and get proportionally less context. This is a real, measurable equity problem baked into a preprocessing step.

Arithmetic. If "127" tokenizes as 127 but "128" tokenizes as 12+8, the model sees no consistent digit structure. Llama's tokenizer splits every number into single digits specifically to fix this, and it measurably improves arithmetic.

Glitch tokens. Strings like SolidGoldMagikarp got their own token because they were frequent in the tokenizer's training data (a Reddit username list) but were then filtered out of the model's training data. The embedding was never trained, so prompting it produced bizarre, unstable behaviour. A tokenizer/corpus mismatch, showing up as an apparent model defect.

Pitfall · adding special tokens without resizing embeddings

You add <|user|> and <|assistant|> to a tokenizer for fine-tuning. The tokenizer now emits IDs 32000 and 32001; the embedding matrix still has 32000 rows. You get an index error if you are lucky, and silent garbage if the model was built with a padded vocabulary. Always call model.resize_token_embeddings(len(tokenizer)), and remember that the new rows are randomly initialised — they need training before they mean anything.

The problem with one-hot

Represent a vocabulary of 50,000 words as 50,000-dimensional indicator vectors and every pair is orthogonal:

\[ \mathbf v_{\text{cat}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{dog}}=0=\mathbf v_{\text{cat}}\cdot\mathbf v_{\text{democracy}} \]

The representation asserts that cat is exactly as unrelated to dog as to democracy. Everything the model knows about word similarity must be learned from scratch in the layers above, from data, per task. Dense embeddings exist to put that knowledge in the representation itself.

TF-IDF

Raw counts over-weight words that are everywhere. TF-IDF discounts a term by how many documents contain it:

\[ \text{tfidf}(t,d)=\underbrace{\log\big(1+\text{count}(t,d)\big)}_{\text{term frequency}}\times\underbrace{\log\frac{N}{1+\text{df}(t)}}_{\text{inverse document frequency}} \]

A word in every document has \(\text{df}=N\) and IDF near zero — it is contributing nothing discriminative. A word in three documents out of a million gets a large weight. The information-theoretic reading is that IDF is roughly \(-\log P(t\in d)\), the surprisal of seeing that term at all, so TF-IDF weights each term by how informative its presence is.

The distributional hypothesis

Firth, 1957: you shall know a word by the company it keeps. If two words appear in the same contexts, they mean similar things. This one assumption underwrites TF-IDF, LSA, Word2Vec, GloVe, BERT and every language model since — the entire field of learned semantics is an engineering elaboration of it. It is also the reason these models absorb every association present in the corpus, including the ones you did not want (Lesson 69).

From counts to dense vectors: LSA

Build a term–document (or term–context) matrix \(X\), then take its truncated SVD from Lesson 4:

\[ X\approx U_k\Sigma_k V_k\T \]

The rows of \(U_k\Sigma_k\) are \(k\)-dimensional word vectors. Because the SVD finds directions of maximal variance, words that co-occur with similar contexts land near each other even if they never co-occur with each other — which is exactly how "car" and "automobile" end up close despite rarely appearing in the same sentence. That is latent semantic analysis, and it predates neural embeddings by two decades.

Do not skip the classical baseline

TF-IDF with a linear SVM or logistic regression is a genuinely strong text classifier. On many document-classification tasks it is within a couple of points of a fine-tuned transformer, trains in seconds on a laptop, needs no GPU, and is fully interpretable — you can read off which words drove each decision. Run it first. If a 400M-parameter model beats it by 0.6 points, that is useful information about whether the model is worth deploying.

Skip-gram

Given a centre word, predict its context words. Maximise, over the corpus:

\[ \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{-c\le j\le c,\ j\ne0}\log p\big(w_{t+j}\mid w_t\big), \qquad p(w_O\mid w_I)=\frac{\exp\big(\mathbf v'_{w_O}\!\cdot\mathbf v_{w_I}\big)}{\sum_{w=1}^{V}\exp\big(\mathbf v'_{w}\!\cdot\mathbf v_{w_I}\big)} \]

Note that each word has two vectors: \(\mathbf v\) when it is the centre, \(\mathbf v'\) when it is the context. The trained embedding you export is usually \(\mathbf v\), sometimes their sum.

Negative sampling — why the softmax had to go

That denominator sums over the whole vocabulary. With \(V=10^6\) and billions of training pairs, computing it once per pair is hopeless. Negative sampling replaces the multi-class problem with a set of binary ones: distinguish the true context word from \(k\) randomly drawn "negatives".

\[ \log\sigma\big(\mathbf v'_{w_O}\!\cdot\mathbf v_{w_I}\big)+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\E_{w_i\sim P_n}\Big[\log\sigma\big(-\mathbf v'_{w_i}\!\cdot\mathbf v_{w_I}\big)\Big] \]

Cost drops from \(O(V)\) to \(O(k)\) with \(k=5\)–20. The noise distribution matters: \(P_n(w)\propto U(w)^{3/4}\), the unigram frequency raised to the ¾ power. That exponent flattens the distribution so that rare words are sampled as negatives more often than their raw frequency would allow — it was found empirically and it works substantially better than either \(U(w)\) or uniform. This same contrastive shape reappears as InfoNCE in Lesson 63 and in CLIP in Lesson 109.

Subsampling handles the opposite end: words like "the" appear so often they dominate the updates while carrying little information. Each occurrence of word \(w\) is discarded with probability \(1-\sqrt{t/f(w)}\), \(t\approx10^{-5}\). This both speeds training and improves the vectors, because it effectively widens the context window past the function words.

From counts to a semantic map · co-occurrence, then SVD, then geometry

GloVe and FastText

GloVe makes the count connection explicit. Rather than sliding a window and predicting, it factorises the global co-occurrence matrix directly, fitting \(\mathbf w_i\T\tilde{\mathbf w}_j+b_i+\tilde b_j\approx\log X_{ij}\) with a weighting function that damps very frequent pairs. The insight is that the ratio of co-occurrence probabilities encodes meaning: \(P(\text{solid}\mid\text{ice})/P(\text{solid}\mid\text{steam})\) is large, \(P(\text{water}\mid\cdot)\) is near 1 for both. Differences of vectors capture ratios of probabilities — which is precisely why vector arithmetic works at all.

FastText represents a word as the sum of its character n-gram vectors, so running shares structure with runner and runs. This gives it embeddings for words never seen in training — the same OOV fix as subword tokenization, one level up — and makes it markedly better for morphologically rich languages like Finnish or Turkish.

Pitfall · the analogy result is weaker than its reputation

\(\mathbf v_{\text{king}}-\mathbf v_{\text{man}}+\mathbf v_{\text{woman}}\approx\mathbf v_{\text{queen}}\) is the most-quoted result in NLP. Two caveats that are almost always omitted. First, the standard evaluation excludes the three input words from the candidate set — without that exclusion the nearest neighbour is usually king itself, because the arithmetic barely moves the vector. Second, accuracy is high on geographic and morphological analogies and much weaker on the abstract relational ones people find impressive. The geometry is real; the demonstration is stage-managed.

Bias is not a bug in the method

Embeddings trained on human text reproduce human associations, including \(\mathbf v_{\text{man}}-\mathbf v_{\text{woman}}\approx\mathbf v_{\text{programmer}}-\mathbf v_{\text{homemaker}}\). The WEAT test quantifies this by measuring differential association between target and attribute word sets, and it recovers documented human-implicit-association results from the corpus alone. Debiasing by projecting out a "gender direction" reduces the measured score but has been shown to mostly hide the structure rather than remove it — the clusters persist and are recoverable. Treat bias measurement as diagnostic, and handle it at the decision layer of your system rather than assuming a geometric patch solved it. Lesson 118 returns to this properly.

Why static embeddings had to end

Word2Vec assigns one vector per word type. But "bank" in river bank and bank account are different words that happen to be spelled the same, and a single vector must average them — landing somewhere useless to both. Every polysemous word is a compromise, and English is full of them. Contextual embeddings, where the vector is a function of the whole sentence, are the fix; that is what ELMo started and BERT completed, and it is where Phase 6 goes.

One equation

\[ \mathbf h_t=\tanh\big(W_{hh}\mathbf h_{t-1}+W_{xh}\mathbf x_t+\mathbf b\big),\qquad \mathbf y_t=W_{hy}\mathbf h_t \]

The hidden state \(\mathbf h_t\) is a fixed-size summary of everything seen so far. The same \(W_{hh}\) and \(W_{xh}\) are applied at every timestep — weight sharing across time, exactly as a CNN shares across space (Lesson 47). That is what lets one model handle any sequence length, and what lets a pattern learned at position 3 be recognised at position 300.

Backpropagation through time

Unrolling turns the RNN into a deep feedforward network, so BPTT is ordinary backprop applied to it — with one twist. Because \(W_{hh}\) is used at every step, its gradient accumulates across all of them (the fan-out rule from Lesson 22):

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{hh}}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial L_t}{\partial W_{hh}},\qquad \frac{\partial L_t}{\partial \mathbf h_k}=\frac{\partial L_t}{\partial \mathbf h_t}\prod_{i=k+1}^{t}\frac{\partial \mathbf h_i}{\partial \mathbf h_{i-1}} \] \[ \frac{\partial \mathbf h_i}{\partial \mathbf h_{i-1}}=W_{hh}\T\,\diag\!\big(1-\tanh^2(\cdot)\big) \]
Why it vanishes — and why it is worse than in a plain deep net

That product contains \(W_{hh}\T\) raised to the power \(t-k\). If the largest singular value of \(W_{hh}\) is \(\lambda\), the gradient magnitude scales roughly as \(\lambda^{t-k}\), further damped by \(\tanh'\le1\). Over 50 steps with \(\lambda=0.9\): \(0.9^{50}\approx0.005\). With \(\lambda=1.1\): \(1.1^{50}\approx117\).

There is no safe setting. \(\lambda<1\) vanishes, \(\lambda>1\) explodes, and \(\lambda=1\) is a measure-zero knife edge that training will not stay on. In a plain deep network each layer has its own matrix, so the products are of different matrices and errors partly cancel. Here it is one matrix compounded, so its spectral radius decides the fate of the whole sequence. This is the single reason the vanilla RNN was abandoned.

Gradient flow through time · how far back can the signal actually reach?

The cell, written out

class RNNCell(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_hid):
        super().__init__()
        self.Wxh = nn.Linear(d_in,  d_hid, bias=False)
        self.Whh = nn.Linear(d_hid, d_hid, bias=True)
        # orthogonal init keeps every singular value at exactly 1 (Lesson 27)
        nn.init.orthogonal_(self.Whh.weight)

    def forward(self, x, h):
        return torch.tanh(self.Wxh(x) + self.Whh(h))

def run_sequence(cell, xs, h0):
    h, outs = h0, []
    for t in range(xs.size(1)):          # this Python loop is why RNNs are slow:
        h = cell(xs[:, t], h)            # no parallelism across time, ever
        outs.append(h)
    return torch.stack(outs, dim=1), h
Pitfall · not detaching the hidden state between batches

When training on long text you carry the hidden state from one batch to the next so the model retains context. If you pass it forward without h.detach(), autograd keeps the entire graph from the start of the epoch alive — memory grows without bound and the backward pass gets slower every step until it dies. This is truncated BPTT: keep the state as a value, discard its history. One .detach(), and it is one of the most common bugs in RNN code.

What actually helps a vanilla RNN

Gradient clipping by norm (Lesson 28) fixes explosion completely and costs one line — it is non-negotiable for any recurrent model. Orthogonal initialisation of \(W_{hh}\) starts you on the knife edge. ReLU with identity initialisation (the IRNN) helps somewhat. None of it fixes vanishing, because the problem is structural rather than numerical: information has to survive repeated multiplication. The actual fix is to build a path where it does not have to, which is Lesson 71.

The idea in one sentence

Add a second state that is updated by addition rather than matrix multiplication, and let the network learn when to write to it, when to erase it and when to read from it.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf f_t&=\sigma\big(W_f[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]+\mathbf b_f\big) &&\text{forget: what to erase}\\[2pt] \mathbf i_t&=\sigma\big(W_i[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]+\mathbf b_i\big) &&\text{input: how much to write}\\[2pt] \tilde{\mathbf c}_t&=\tanh\big(W_c[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]+\mathbf b_c\big) &&\text{candidate: what to write}\\[2pt] \mathbf c_t&=\mathbf f_t\odot\mathbf c_{t-1}+\mathbf i_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf c}_t &&\text{the cell state update}\\[2pt] \mathbf o_t&=\sigma\big(W_o[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]+\mathbf b_o\big) &&\text{output: what to expose}\\[2pt] \mathbf h_t&=\mathbf o_t\odot\tanh(\mathbf c_t) &&\text{the visible hidden state} \end{aligned} \]
Why the gradient survives

Differentiate the cell-state update along the direct path:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathbf c_t}{\partial \mathbf c_{t-1}}=\diag(\mathbf f_t) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial \mathbf c_T}{\partial \mathbf c_k}=\prod_{t=k+1}^{T}\diag(\mathbf f_t) \]

Compare with the RNN's \(\prod W_{hh}\T\diag(\tanh')\). Here the product is of diagonal gate values the network controls, not of a fixed weight matrix. If the model decides a piece of information matters, it drives \(f_t\to1\) for that coordinate and the product stays at 1 for hundreds of steps — the gradient arrives undiminished. Note the structural resemblance to the residual connection in Lesson 52: both work by creating a path where the Jacobian is (close to) the identity. That is the same idea twice, discovered eighteen years apart.

Pitfall · the forget-gate bias, and why it must not start at zero

With \(\mathbf b_f=0\), the sigmoid outputs 0.5, so the cell state is halved at every step: after 20 steps only \(2^{-20}\approx10^{-6}\) survives. The LSTM begins life forgetting, and it has to climb out of that before it can learn anything long-range. Initialise \(\mathbf b_f\) to 1.0 (or 2.0) so gates start near 0.73–0.88 and the default behaviour is remembering. This is one of the highest-value one-line changes in the whole of recurrent modelling, it was documented in 2015, and PyTorch still does not do it for you — nn.LSTM initialises all biases uniformly and you must fix it yourself.

class LSTMCell(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_hid):
        super().__init__()
        # one matrix producing all four gates at once: 1 GEMM instead of 4
        self.W = nn.Linear(d_in + d_hid, 4 * d_hid)
        self.d = d_hid
        with torch.no_grad():
            self.W.bias.zero_()
            self.W.bias[d_hid:2 * d_hid].fill_(1.0)      # forget-gate bias = 1

    def forward(self, x, state):
        h, c = state
        gates = self.W(torch.cat([h, x], dim=-1))
        i, f, g, o = gates.chunk(4, dim=-1)              # order: input, forget, candidate, output
        i, f, o = torch.sigmoid(i), torch.sigmoid(f), torch.sigmoid(o)
        c = f * c + i * torch.tanh(g)                    # the highway
        h = o * torch.tanh(c)
        return h, (h, c)

Parameter count: \(4\big((d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{hid}})d_{\text{hid}}+d_{\text{hid}}\big)\) — four times a vanilla RNN cell, because there are four gate matrices. A 512-unit LSTM over 512-dimensional inputs holds about 2.1M parameters per layer.

What the gates actually learn

Karpathy's analysis of a character-level LSTM found individual cells that had learned interpretable jobs with no supervision: one tracked whether the model was inside quotation marks, another counted position within a line to predict newlines, another switched on inside brackets. These are exactly the long-range, discrete state variables that a vanilla RNN cannot hold, and finding them is convincing evidence that the cell-state highway is doing what the derivation says. Log your gate activations over a sequence; it is one of the more rewarding things you can plot.

GRU: the same idea with two gates

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf z_t&=\sigma\big(W_z[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]\big) &&\text{update gate}\\ \mathbf r_t&=\sigma\big(W_r[\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]\big) &&\text{reset gate}\\ \tilde{\mathbf h}_t&=\tanh\big(W[\mathbf r_t\odot\mathbf h_{t-1},\mathbf x_t]\big)\\ \mathbf h_t&=(1-\mathbf z_t)\odot\mathbf h_{t-1}+\mathbf z_t\odot\tilde{\mathbf h}_t \end{aligned} \]

The GRU merges cell state and hidden state into one, and ties forgetting to writing: whatever fraction \(\mathbf z_t\) you write is exactly the fraction you erase. That coupling is why it needs three matrices instead of four and runs about 25% faster.

LSTMGRU
Gates3 (forget, input, output)2 (update, reset)
States2 (\(\mathbf h\) and \(\mathbf c\))1
Parameters\(4d(d+n)\)\(3d(d+n)\) — 25% fewer
EmpiricallyWithin noise of each other on most tasks. LSTM has a slight edge on very long dependencies and on language modelling; GRU on small datasets and where speed matters.

Do not spend time choosing. Pick one, tune the learning rate properly, and move on — the decision is worth far less than one round of LR tuning.

Bidirectional

Run one RNN forward and a second backward, then concatenate their states: \(\mathbf h_t=[\overrightarrow{\mathbf h}_t;\overleftarrow{\mathbf h}_t]\). Now every position sees the whole sentence, which matters enormously for tagging and classification — resolving "bank" needs the words after it as much as before.

Pitfall · bidirectional models cannot generate, and cannot forecast

A backward pass requires the future. That is legal when the entire input is available at once (classification, tagging, encoding a source sentence for translation) and illegal whenever you predict the next element: language modelling, streaming speech recognition, and time-series forecasting. Using a BiLSTM on a forecasting task leaks the answer into the features, produces a spectacular validation score, and fails completely in production. This is a genuinely common and genuinely costly mistake — if your time-series model looks too good, check for bidirectionality first.

Depth, and the dropout that works

Stacking RNN layers helps up to a point — 2 to 4 layers is the normal range, and beyond 4 you need residual connections between layers, exactly as in Lesson 52. Google's GNMT used 8 layers with residuals for precisely this reason.

Standard dropout applied to a recurrent connection samples a new mask at every timestep, which injects fresh noise into the memory at each step and destroys long-range information. Variational (locked) dropout samples one mask per sequence and reuses it at every timestep, so the same units are dropped throughout — regularising without corrupting memory.

rnn = nn.LSTM(input_size=300, hidden_size=512,
              num_layers=2,          # stacked
              bidirectional=True,    # doubles the output width to 1024
              batch_first=True,      # (B, T, D) instead of the default (T, B, D)
              dropout=0.3)           # applied BETWEEN layers only, not within the recurrence

out, (h_n, c_n) = rnn(x)             # out: (B, T, 2*512)
# h_n is (num_layers*num_directions, B, H) — ordered [L0_fwd, L0_bwd, L1_fwd, L1_bwd]

With padding, out[:, -1] is the state after consuming pad tokens, not after the real final word. For the forward direction you want out[i, lens[i]-1]; for the backward direction you want position 0. Using pack_padded_sequence makes h_n correct automatically, which is the main reason to bother with packing. The task

Predict the next character given all previous ones. It is the smallest complete language model: no tokenizer to train, a vocabulary of about 65 symbols, and a target that comes free from the data — the input shifted by one.

x (B, T)→embed (B, T, D)→LSTM (B, T, H)→logits (B, T, V) 
text = open("input.txt").read()
chars = sorted(set(text)); V = len(chars)
stoi = {c: i for i, c in enumerate(chars)}
data = torch.tensor([stoi[c] for c in text])

def get_batch(data, B, T):
    ix = torch.randint(len(data) - T - 1, (B,))
    x = torch.stack([data[i:i+T]     for i in ix])
    y = torch.stack([data[i+1:i+T+1] for i in ix])    # the target IS the input, shifted
    return x.cuda(), y.cuda()

class CharLM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, V, d=256, h=512, layers=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(V, d)
        self.rnn = nn.LSTM(d, h, layers, batch_first=True, dropout=0.2)
        self.head = nn.Linear(h, V)
        for name, p in self.rnn.named_parameters():
            if "bias_ih" in name:
                p.data[h:2*h].fill_(1.0)               # forget-gate bias (Lesson 71)

    def forward(self, x, state=None):
        z, state = self.rnn(self.emb(x), state)
        return self.head(z), state

for step in range(20000):

Sampling, and what temperature does

\[ p_i=\frac{\exp(z_i/\tau)}{\sum_j\exp(z_j/\tau)} \]

\(\tau\to0\) gives argmax; \(\tau=1\) is the model's own distribution; \(\tau>1\) flattens it. The reason low temperature is not simply "better" is that greedy decoding on a character model produces immediate repetition loops — the the the the — because the single most likely continuation of a repeated phrase is usually more repetition. Lesson 76 covers this properly.

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(model, prompt, n=500, temp=0.8):
    model.eval()
    idx = torch.tensor([[stoi[c] for c in prompt]]).cuda()
    state, out = None, list(prompt)
    logits, state = model(idx, state)                  # consume the prompt once
    for _ in range(n):
        probs = F.softmax(logits[:, -1] / temp, dim=-1)
        nxt = torch.multinomial(probs, 1)
        out.append(chars[nxt.item()])
        logits, state = model(nxt, state)              # feed ONE character, carry the state
    return "".join(out)

Perplexity

\[ \text{PPL}=\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}-\log p(x_i\mid x_{<i})\right)=\exp(\text{cross-entropy}) \]

Perplexity is the exponential of the average cross-entropy, and it reads as an effective branching factor: a perplexity of 4 means the model is as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 4 characters at each step. Uniform over 65 characters gives PPL 65; a decent character LSTM on Shakespeare reaches 3–4. Because it is a monotone function of the loss, it adds no information — but it is the number the field reports, and it is comparable across models only if they share a tokenization.

What good looks like, by loss value

Start: \(\log 65\approx4.17\). After a few hundred steps: ~2.5, output is character soup with correct letter frequencies. Around 1.9: real words, no grammar. Around 1.5: grammatical phrases, correct quotation and bracket matching, plausible-looking names. Below 1.4 on Shakespeare you are likely overfitting a small corpus. Sample text at every checkpoint — the qualitative jump from "letter soup" to "words" is far more informative about progress than the loss curve.

Exercise

Train the same model three ways — vanilla RNN, GRU, LSTM — with identical width, depth and learning rate, on 1MB of text. Plot all three loss curves. The vanilla RNN will plateau visibly higher. Then increase the sequence length from 64 to 256 and rerun: the gap should widen, because that is the regime where the cell-state highway earns its parameters.

Seq2Seq & Encoder-Decoder

74
Chapters

  2. The context vector bottleneck
  3. Teacher forcing and exposure bias

The framework

Two RNNs. The encoder reads the source sequence and compresses it into a fixed-size vector \(\mathbf c\) — usually its final hidden state. The decoder is a language model over the target, initialised with \(\mathbf c\) and generating one token at a time until it emits <eos>. This is the first architecture in the course that handles unaligned many-to-many, and it is what made neural machine translation work in 2014.

Teacher forcing and exposure bias

During training the decoder is fed the ground-truth previous token rather than its own prediction. This makes training parallel across timesteps and keeps it stable — without it, one early mistake corrupts every subsequent step and the gradient signal is noise.

But it creates a train/inference mismatch. At training time the model only ever conditions on correct prefixes; at inference it conditions on its own output, including its own mistakes, which it has never seen. That is exposure bias, and it is why generation can start well and then degrade into incoherence.

Scheduled sampling, and the honest verdict

The obvious fix is to gradually feed the model its own predictions during training, ramping the probability from 0 to some fraction. It helps on some tasks. But it also makes the training objective inconsistent — the model is now conditioned on a mixture it will never see at test time either — and a later analysis showed it can push the model towards degenerate solutions. In practice, modern systems mostly keep pure teacher forcing and address the symptoms at decoding time instead (Lesson 76). Worth knowing that a clean-sounding fix did not survive scrutiny; it happens often.

class Decoder(nn.Module):
    def forward(self, tgt_in, enc_state):
        state, outputs = enc_state, []
        emb = self.emb(tgt_in)                          # (B, T, D) — all targets at once
        z, _ = self.rnn(emb, state)                     # teacher forcing: parallel over T
        return self.head(z)

loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.reshape(-1, V),
                       tgt_out.reshape(-1),

Pitfall · getting the shift wrong

The decoder input is [<bos>, w1, w2, w3] and the target is [w1, w2, w3, <eos>] — offset by one. Feed the same sequence as both and the model learns the identity function: it copies its input to its output, training loss goes to near zero within a few hundred steps, and generation produces nothing but the prompt. A training loss that collapses implausibly fast on a seq2seq task almost always means the shift is missing.

The Attention Mechanism

The mechanism

Instead of one context vector, compute a fresh one at every decoder step, as a weighted average of all encoder states:

\[ e_{tj}=\text{score}(\mathbf s_{t-1},\mathbf h_j),\qquad \alpha_{tj}=\frac{\exp(e_{tj})}{\sum_{k=1}^{T_x}\exp(e_{tk})},\qquad \mathbf c_t=\sum_{j=1}^{T_x}\alpha_{tj}\mathbf h_j \]

Three steps, and they are the same three steps in every attention mechanism you will ever meet: score, normalise, average. Everything from Bahdanau to FlashAttention differs only in how the score is computed and how the sum is made efficient.

VariantScore functionNote
Bahdanau (additive)\(\mathbf v\T\tanh\big(W_1\mathbf s+W_2\mathbf h\big)\)A small MLP. Works when \(\mathbf s\) and \(\mathbf h\) have different dimensions. More parameters, slower.
Luong (general)\(\mathbf s\T W\mathbf h\)A bilinear form. One matrix.
Luong (dot)\(\mathbf s\T\mathbf h\)No parameters at all. Requires matching dimensions.
Scaled dot-product\(\dfrac{\mathbf q\T\mathbf k}{\sqrt{d_k}}\)What the transformer uses. Lesson 81 derives the \(\sqrt{d_k}\).

Dot-product won because it is a single matrix multiply over the whole batch and sequence, which maps perfectly onto GPU hardware. Additive attention is marginally better at small scale and hopeless at large scale — a recurring pattern in this field.

Alignment · scores, softmax, context — and what sharpness costs

Why the alignment matrix mattered so much

Plot \(\alpha_{tj}\) as a heatmap and you can read the model's word alignment directly. On French→English it is mostly diagonal, with a visible off-diagonal crossing exactly where adjective and noun order swaps. Nobody supervised this — it emerged from optimising translation quality alone.

That was the first widely persuasive demonstration that a neural network had learned an interpretable, linguistically meaningful internal structure. It also established attention weights as an interpretability tool, which turned out to be an overclaim: later work showed you can often find very different attention distributions giving identical predictions, so attention weights are not a faithful explanation of what the model used. Lesson 117 revisits this. Read alignment plots as suggestive, not as evidence.

class BahdanauAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_enc, d_dec, d_attn):
        super().__init__()
        self.Wh = nn.Linear(d_enc, d_attn, bias=False)
        self.Ws = nn.Linear(d_dec, d_attn, bias=False)
        self.v  = nn.Linear(d_attn, 1, bias=False)

    def forward(self, s, H, mask):
        # s (B,d_dec) · H (B,Tx,d_enc) · mask (B,Tx) True = real token
        e = self.v(torch.tanh(self.Wh(H) + self.Ws(s).unsqueeze(1))).squeeze(-1)   # (B,Tx)
        e = e.masked_fill(~mask, float("-inf"))       # BEFORE softmax, not after
        a = torch.softmax(e, dim=-1)
        return torch.bmm(a.unsqueeze(1), H).squeeze(1), a                          # context, weights
Pitfall · masking after the softmax

Zeroing the weights of padded positions after softmax leaves the remaining weights summing to less than 1, so the context vector is systematically shrunk by however much padding a given example carries — short sentences get quiet contexts, long ones get loud. Set the scores to \(-\infty\) before the softmax so the normalisation excludes them properly. And use -inf rather than a large negative number: with fp16, \(-10^9\) overflows. Use torch.finfo(dtype).min if you need a finite value.

The sentence that ended the phase

Attention was introduced as a patch: the RNN still did the sequential work, and attention just fixed its bottleneck. Then someone asked what happens if the encoder attends to itself — if every source position builds its representation by attending to every other source position, with no recurrence anywhere. That removes the sequential dependency entirely, so the whole sequence computes in parallel, and the path length between any two positions drops from \(O(T)\) to \(O(1)\). Everything in Phase 6 follows from that question.

Decoding & Generation Strategies

The model gives you a distribution, not a sentence

A trained language model defines \(p(x_t\mid x_{<t})\). Turning that into text is a search problem, entirely separate from training, and the choice you make here changes the output more than another epoch of training would. Two models with identical perplexity can produce wildly different text under different decoders.

Decoding strategies · the same distribution, five different sentences

Search-based: greedy and beam

Greedy takes the argmax at each step. Beam search keeps the \(B\) highest-probability partial sequences and extends all of them, which finds higher-likelihood sequences — the greedy path is often not the most probable overall.

Beam search needs length normalization

Sequence probability is a product of terms below 1, so longer sequences are always less probable. Unnormalised beam search therefore has a systematic bias towards short output — in translation it truncates sentences. Divide by a length penalty:

\[ \text{score}(\mathbf y)=\frac{\log p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)}{\big((5+|\mathbf y|)/6\big)^{\alpha}},\qquad \alpha\approx0.6\text{–}1.0 \]

The odd constants are GNMT's, tuned empirically. A coverage penalty is often added too, rewarding hypotheses whose attention weights have covered every source word — which directly penalises the dropped-clause failure mode.

Why beam search is wrong for open-ended text

Beam search is excellent for translation and summarisation, where there is roughly one correct output. For open-ended generation it produces flat, repetitive, oddly generic text — and the reason is counterintuitive. Human text is not high-probability. Measure the per-token probability of real human writing and it fluctuates constantly, including many low-probability choices; that variation is what makes it interesting. Beam search searches for the highest-probability sequence, which is precisely the boring one. The fix is not a better search: it is to stop searching and start sampling.

Sampling-based

Top-p's adaptivity is the key property: it keeps 1 token when the model is sure and 200 when it is not, which is exactly what fixed-\(k\) truncation cannot do.

def top_p_filter(logits, p=0.9):
    sorted_logits, sorted_idx = torch.sort(logits, descending=True)
    probs = torch.softmax(sorted_logits, dim=-1)
    cum = probs.cumsum(dim=-1)
    # keep everything up to and INCLUDING the token that crosses p
    remove = cum - probs > p
    sorted_logits[remove] = float("-inf")
    return sorted_logits.scatter(-1, sorted_idx.argsort(-1), sorted_logits)

# order matters: temperature, then truncation, then renormalise
logits = logits / temperature
logits = top_p_filter(logits, p=0.92)
next_id = torch.multinomial(torch.softmax(logits, dim=-1), 1)
Pitfall · applying temperature after truncation

Truncate first and you have already thrown tokens away based on the untempered distribution, so raising the temperature can no longer bring them back — the two controls stop composing and high-temperature output becomes strangely rigid. Apply temperature first, then top-k/top-p, then renormalise. Related: repetition penalties that divide logits by a factor break for negative logits, where dividing makes them larger. Use the subtractive form (a frequency/presence penalty) or handle the sign explicitly; this bug is present in a surprising amount of shipped code.

Settings that work

Factual answering, code, extraction: temperature 0 (greedy) or 0.2. You want determinism and there is a right answer. Translation, summarisation: beam search, 4–5 beams, length penalty ~0.6. Chat, creative writing: temperature 0.7–1.0 with top-p 0.9–0.95. Brainstorming: temperature 1.0–1.2 with min-p. Always report your decoding parameters alongside any generation result — an unreported temperature makes a comparison meaningless, and this omission is rampant in papers and blog posts alike.

Causality by padding

A 1D convolution over a sequence is Lesson 48 with one spatial dimension. To use it for prediction you must prevent it from seeing the future: pad \(k-1\) zeros on the left only, and drop the last \(k-1\) outputs. Then output \(t\) depends on inputs \(\le t\) and nothing later — a causal convolution.

The problem is receptive field. Stacking causal 3-taps grows it linearly: \(L\) layers see \(2L+1\) steps. Reaching 1000 timesteps needs 500 layers. Dilation fixes it by doubling the spacing each layer, so the receptive field grows exponentially:

\[ r=1+\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}(k-1)\,2^{l}=1+(k-1)\big(2^{L}-1\big) \]

With \(k=2\) and \(L=10\): \(r=1024\) from ten layers.

RNN / LSTMDilated causal CNNTransformer

Path length between distant positions\(O(T)\)\(O(\log T)\)\(O(1)\)

Look at the first row. The RNN's fatal weakness is not accuracy — it is that timestep \(t\) cannot start until \(t-1\) finishes, so a GPU with thousands of cores runs one step at a time. Both alternatives compute every position simultaneously during training. That is what decided the field, and the last row is the price the transformer pays for it.

TCNs are still worth reaching for

A Temporal Convolutional Network — dilated causal convs, residual blocks, weight normalisation — matches or beats LSTMs on many sequence benchmarks, trains far faster, and has stable gradients. For time series and moderate-length signals it is often a better default than either an RNN or a transformer: fewer hyperparameters than attention, no quadratic cost, and a receptive field you can compute exactly from the architecture rather than hope for.

Framing

Slide a window of length \(L\) over the series and predict the next \(H\) values. That turns forecasting into ordinary supervised learning, with two decisions that matter more than the model: the lookback \(L\) (long enough to contain a full seasonal cycle) and whether you forecast the \(H\) steps directly in one shot or recursively, feeding predictions back as inputs. Recursive is more flexible and accumulates error; direct is more robust and needs \(H\) separate outputs. Direct multi-step is the modern default.

Pitfall · the three leaks that make forecasting results fictional

Random splitting. Shuffling a time series and splitting randomly puts future points in the training set. The model interpolates rather than forecasts and reports a beautiful score. Always split chronologically.

Scaling on the whole series. Computing mean and std across train and test before splitting leaks future statistics into training. Fit the scaler on the training window only, and refit as you roll forward.

Features that were not available at prediction time. Using a daily aggregate to predict an hour inside that same day; using a revised figure that was only published two weeks later. Every feature needs a timestamp for when it became known, not when it refers to. This one destroys real production systems, not just benchmarks.

The baselines you must beat

BaselineWhat it doesWhy it matters
Naive\(\hat y_{t+h}=y_t\)On many financial and high-frequency series, nothing beats it. If your model does not, you have learned that.
Seasonal naive\(\hat y_{t+h}=y_{t+h-m}\)Last week's Tuesday predicts this Tuesday. Astonishingly hard to beat on retail and traffic data.

The uncomfortable state of the field

A 2022 paper ("Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?") showed that a single linear layer on the flattened lookback window beat several published transformer forecasters on their own benchmarks. The response was not that transformers are useless — PatchTST subsequently did better by treating patches of the series as tokens, much as ViT does with images — but the episode is instructive. Time series benchmarks are small, evaluation is easy to get wrong, and complexity is rewarded by reviewers. Run the linear baseline. Run seasonal naive. Publish the comparison honestly.

Predict a distribution, not a number

Most decisions made from a forecast are asymmetric: stocking too little costs a lost sale, too much costs spoilage. A point forecast cannot express that. Quantile (pinball) loss trains a model to output any quantile you ask for:

\[ \mathcal L_q(y,\hat y)=\max\big(q(y-\hat y),\ (q-1)(y-\hat y)\big) \]

Train heads for \(q\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}\) and you get a median plus an 80% interval directly. DeepAR takes the alternative route and outputs the parameters of a likelihood (Gaussian, or negative binomial for counts), trained by maximum likelihood exactly as in Lesson 8.

Backtest with rolling origin

A single train/test split gives one number from one point in history, and its variance is enormous. Instead, slide the cutoff forward: train on \([0,t)\), evaluate on \([t,t+H)\), advance \(t\), repeat. Report the mean and spread across all folds. Use MASE (error relative to seasonal naive) rather than raw RMSE, so results are comparable across series with different scales — and avoid MAPE, which is undefined at zero and asymmetrically punishes over-forecasting.

Perplexity, and its one hard rule

\(\text{PPL}=\exp(\text{cross-entropy per token})\), as in Lesson 73. The rule: perplexity is only comparable between models sharing a tokenizer. A character model and a BPE model computing "perplexity" are measuring per-character and per-token uncertainty, which are different quantities by a factor of several. To compare across tokenizers, normalise to bits per character: \(\text{BPC}=\frac{\text{total NLL in bits}}{\text{number of characters}}\). Papers that compare raw perplexities across tokenizations are making an error, and it happens often.

BLEU, by hand

BLEU is modified n-gram precision with a brevity penalty:

\[ \text{BLEU}=\text{BP}\cdot\exp\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{4}w_n\log p_n\right),\qquad \text{BP}=\begin{cases}1 & c>r\\ e^{1-r/c} & c\le r\end{cases} \]

where \(p_n\) is the fraction of candidate n-grams appearing in the reference, clipped so a repeated word cannot score more times than it appears in the reference. That clipping exists to stop the degenerate "the the the the the" from scoring perfect unigram precision. The brevity penalty exists because precision alone rewards emitting one confident word — BLEU has no recall term, and BP is the patch that stands in for it.

A worked example

Reference: the cat sat on the mat (6 tokens). Candidate: the cat sat on mat (5 tokens).

Unigrams: all 5 candidate tokens appear in the reference → \(p_1=5/5=1.0\). Bigrams: the cat ✓, cat sat ✓, sat on ✓, on mat ✗ → \(p_2=3/4\). Trigrams: 2/3. 4-grams: 1/2. Geometric mean \(=(1\cdot0.75\cdot0.667\cdot0.5)^{1/4}=0.717\). Brevity: \(c=5<r=6\), so \(\text{BP}=e^{1-6/5}=0.819\). BLEU \(=0.717\times0.819=\mathbf{0.587}\). One dropped article costs 41 points — which tells you something about the metric's sensitivity.

Pitfall · four ways these metrics lie to you

Paraphrase is punished. "The film was excellent" and "the movie was superb" share almost no n-grams and score near zero against each other. A perfectly correct output can score badly.

Fluent nonsense is rewarded. Copying phrases from the source into a summary scores well on ROUGE while being extractive and possibly wrong.

Scores are not comparable across setups. BLEU depends on tokenization, casing, and number of references. Two papers reporting "BLEU 34" may differ by several points purely in preprocessing — which is exactly why sacreBLEU exists and why you should report its version string.

Factual errors are invisible. A summary that inverts a number or attributes a quote to the wrong person can score as well as a correct one. No n-gram metric measures truth. This is the failure that matters most in deployment and the one these metrics are least able to see.

How to actually evaluate generation

Use automatic metrics for regression testing — catching when a change makes things clearly worse — not for ranking systems that are close. For real judgements, run human evaluation: at least 3 annotators per item, a written rubric with separate axes (fluency, adequacy, factuality), pairwise comparison rather than absolute scoring, and report inter-annotator agreement. LLM-as-judge (Lesson 100) is now the practical middle ground, but it has its own biases — towards longer answers, towards its own outputs, and towards whichever option is presented first — so randomise position and calibrate against a human-labelled subset before trusting it.
