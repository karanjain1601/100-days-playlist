A constant learning rate never converges

This is the fact that makes schedules mandatory rather than optional. SGD does not descend on the true loss — it descends on a noisy estimate from one mini-batch. Near a minimum the true gradient goes to zero but the noise does not, so each step is pure jitter of size roughly \(\eta\) times the gradient noise.

The noise ball

Model the mini-batch gradient as the true gradient plus zero-mean noise, \(\hat{\mathbf g}=\mathbf g+\boldsymbol\xi\) with \(\E[\boldsymbol\xi\boldsymbol\xi\T]=\Sigma\). Near a quadratic minimum with curvature \(\lambda\), the stationary distribution of SGD has

\[ \E\!\left[\|\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta^\ast\|^2\right] \;\approx\; \frac{\eta\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{2\lambda-\eta\lambda^2} \;\xrightarrow[\ \eta\ \text{small}\ ]{}\; \frac{\eta\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{2\lambda} \]

The parameters orbit the minimum inside a ball whose radius is proportional to \(\eta\). Halving the learning rate halves the radius. The only way to actually land at the bottom is to shrink \(\eta\) towards zero — which is exactly what a schedule does. Every drop you see in a loss curve immediately after an LR decay is this ball contracting.

Warmup — and why it is not optional for Adam

Warmup ramps the learning rate linearly from (near) zero to \(\eta_0\) over the first few hundred to few thousand steps:

\[ \eta_t=\eta_0\cdot\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t}{T_{\text{warm}}}\right) \]

There are two independent reasons, and people usually only know the second one.

Reason 1 — Adam's second moment is garbage at step 1

Recall from Lesson 32 that Adam divides by \(\sqrt{\hat v_t}\). At small \(t\), \(\hat v_t\) is an average of one or two squared gradients, so its variance is enormous. Some coordinates get a denominator that happens to be tiny and take a wildly oversized step. This is not a scaling problem you can fix with a smaller \(\eta\) — it is an estimator-variance problem, and it goes away only once \(\hat v_t\) has averaged over \(\sim 1/(1-\beta_2)=1000\) steps. Warmup is a cheap way to survive that window. (RAdam attacks the same problem analytically.)

Reason 2 — large batches want a large \(\eta\) the model cannot yet take

The linear scaling rule from Lesson 29 says batch 8192 wants an LR 32× larger than batch 256. That LR is correct once the weights are in a sane region, but at initialisation the loss surface is steep and badly conditioned, and a 32× step diverges immediately. Warmup buys the network a few hundred steps to reach a region where the big step is safe.

Rules of thumb: 2–5% of total steps for vision, ~2000 steps for transformer pretraining regardless of run length, and 0 for small-batch SGD on a small model, where warmup does nothing but waste steps.

Stepping the scheduler correctly

import math, torch
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LambdaLR

def warmup_cosine(optimizer, warmup_steps, total_steps, min_ratio=0.0):
    def f(step):
        if step < warmup_steps:                       # linear ramp
            return step / max(1, warmup_steps)
        p = (step - warmup_steps) / max(1, total_steps - warmup_steps)
        p = min(1.0, p)                                # clamp: never go past the floor
        return min_ratio + (1 - min_ratio) * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * p))
    return LambdaLR(optimizer, f)

steps_per_epoch = len(train_loader)
total = epochs * steps_per_epoch
sched = warmup_cosine(opt, warmup_steps=int(0.03 * total), total_steps=total)

for epoch in range(epochs):
    for xb, yb in train_loader:
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        loss = criterion(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        sched.step()                                   # PER BATCH, because total is in batches
Pitfall · three ways to break a scheduler

1. Wrong granularity. You compute total in batches but call sched.step() once per epoch. Your cosine then completes 0.3% of its curve and the LR never decays. The loss looks "stuck" and you blame the model. Decide once whether your schedule is per-step or per-epoch and make the denominator match.

2. Wrong order. sched.step() must come after opt.step(). PyTorch warns about this but the warning fires once and scrolls away.

3. Resuming without the scheduler. Checkpoints must save sched.state_dict() too. Restore the model and optimizer but not the scheduler and you restart training at the peak LR, which undoes hours of annealing in about ten steps.

Reading the two curves together

Always log LR on the same x-axis as loss. A loss drop that lines up exactly with an LR decay is the noise ball contracting — normal and healthy. A loss drop that happens between decays is real learning. If every improvement in your run is glued to a decay step, your LR was too high for the whole run and the model was never learning, only orbiting.

Train the same CIFAR model three times for exactly 20 epochs: constant \(\eta\), step decay, and warmup+cosine. Plot all three loss curves on one axis. L2, and what it actually does to a weight

Add a penalty on squared weight magnitude to the objective:

\[ \tilde L(\mathbf w)=L(\mathbf w)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf w\|_2^2 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \nabla\tilde L=\nabla L+\lambda\mathbf w \]

Substitute into the SGD update and the effect becomes obvious:

\[ \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta(\nabla L+\lambda\mathbf w)=\underbrace{(1-\eta\lambda)}_{\text{shrink}}\mathbf w-\eta\nabla L \]

Every step multiplies the weight by \((1-\eta\lambda)\) before the gradient is applied. That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the technique is called weight decay: absent any gradient signal, weights decay geometrically towards zero. A weight only stays large if the loss gradient keeps pushing it back out. L2 is therefore a running vote: justify your magnitude or lose it.

Why L2 is a Gaussian prior

From Lesson 8, MAP estimation maximises \(\log p(\mathcal D\mid\mathbf w)+\log p(\mathbf w)\). Take an isotropic Gaussian prior \(\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma_p^2 I)\):

\[ \log p(\mathbf w)=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_p^2}\|\mathbf w\|_2^2+\text{const} \]

Negate to get a loss and the penalty is exactly \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\mathbf w\|^2\) with \(\lambda=1/\sigma_p^2\). A large \(\lambda\) is a narrow prior — a strong prior belief that weights are small. Swap in a Laplace prior \(p(w)\propto e^{-|w|/b}\) and you get L1 instead. The two penalties are not tricks; they are two different statements about what you believe before seeing data.

L1 and why it produces exact zeros

The L1 gradient is \(\lambda\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\) — a constant pull towards zero regardless of how small \(w\) already is. L2's pull is \(\lambda w\), which vanishes as \(w\to0\). So L2 makes weights small; L1 makes them exactly zero and keeps them there, because at \(w=0\) the subgradient interval \([-\lambda,\lambda]\) can cancel the data gradient entirely. That is sparsity, and it is why L1 is used for feature selection and structured pruning rather than for general deep-network regularisation.

The AdamW distinction, restated because it bites everyone

Lesson 32 derived this; here is the practical form. Adding \(\lambda\mathbf w\) to the gradient means the penalty passes through Adam's per-parameter denominator \(\sqrt{\hat v}\). A weight with historically large gradients gets a large \(\hat v\) and therefore a weaker decay — the exact opposite of what you intended. Decoupled decay applies the shrink directly to the parameter, outside the adaptive machinery:

\[ \text{L2 in Adam:}\quad \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\frac{\hat m+\lambda\mathbf w}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon} \qquad\qquad \text{AdamW:}\quad \mathbf w\leftarrow\mathbf w-\eta\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon}-\eta\lambda\mathbf w \]

Use torch.optim.AdamW. If you find yourself typing Adam(..., weight_decay=...), you almost certainly meant AdamW.

Pitfall · decaying your normalization gains

A LayerNorm gain \(\gamma\) is initialised to 1 and is supposed to sit near 1. Apply weight decay to it and you are actively pulling it towards 0, which scales the entire layer's output towards zero. On a transformer this shows up as a run that trains for a while and then quietly degrades. The same applies to biases: a bias has no capacity to overfit in the way a weight matrix does, and shrinking it just adds bias in the statistical sense. The p.ndim < 2 test above catches all of these in one line.

Early stopping

Track a validation metric, keep the best weights, and stop when it has not improved for patience evaluations. It is the cheapest regulariser in existence — it costs nothing and it is the only one that adapts to the run in front of you.

class EarlyStopping:
    def __init__(self, patience=10, min_delta=1e-4, mode="min"):
        self.patience, self.min_delta, self.mode = patience, min_delta, mode
        self.best, self.count, self.best_state = None, 0, None

    def step(self, value, model):
        better = (self.best is None or
                  (value < self.best - self.min_delta if self.mode == "min"
                   else value > self.best + self.min_delta))
        if better:
            self.best, self.count = value, 0
            # keep on CPU so a long run does not hold a second model on the GPU
            self.best_state = {k: v.detach().cpu().clone()
                               for k, v in model.state_dict().items()}
        else:
            self.count += 1
        return self.count >= self.patience        # True -> stop

    def restore(self, model):
        if self.best_state is not None:
            model.load_state_dict(self.best_state)
Early stopping is L2 in disguise

For a linear model trained by gradient descent from \(\mathbf w=0\), stopping after \(t\) steps with learning rate \(\eta\) gives a solution provably close to L2-regularised regression with \(\lambda\approx 1/(\eta t)\). Train longer and you weaken the regularisation. This is why "train longer" and "reduce weight decay" so often produce the same symptom, and why tuning both at once is confusing — they are pulling the same lever from two ends.

Sane starting values

CNN from scratch with SGD: \(\lambda=5\times10^{-4}\). Vision transformer or ConvNeXt with AdamW: \(\lambda=0.05\)–\(0.1\). LLM pretraining: \(\lambda=0.1\). Fine-tuning a pretrained model: \(\lambda=0.01\) or lower — the pretrained weights are already a strong prior and you do not want to erase it. Note the two orders of magnitude between the SGD and AdamW numbers; they are not comparable quantities, because decoupled decay is not scaled by the gradient magnitude.

The mechanism

At training time, sample a Bernoulli mask and zero out units:

\[ m_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p),\qquad \tilde h_i=\frac{m_i}{1-p}\,h_i \]

The \(1/(1-p)\) factor is inverted dropout, and it is doing something specific: it makes \(\E[\tilde h_i]=h_i\), so the expected input to the next layer is unchanged. That means inference needs no adjustment at all — you simply stop dropping. (The original 2012 formulation instead multiplied by \((1-p)\) at test time; inverted dropout moved the cost into training, where it is free, and is what every framework implements today.)

Why it regularises

Two complementary stories, both true. Ensembling: a layer with \(n\) units has \(2^n\) possible masks, so training is approximately training \(2^n\) subnetworks with massively shared weights, and inference approximates averaging them. Co-adaptation: a unit cannot rely on any particular partner being present, so it must learn a feature that is independently useful. Fragile committee-of-two features get broken up.

The honest caveat: dropout's importance has fallen sharply. Modern convnets use it barely or not at all, because BatchNorm plus heavy augmentation already regularise. It remains standard in transformer FFN and attention blocks, and in the classifier head of a fine-tuned model.

Pitfall · the dropout–BatchNorm variance shift

BatchNorm records running statistics during training, when dropout is active and the variance of its input is inflated by the \(1/(1-p)\) rescaling. At evaluation dropout switches off, the true variance drops, and BatchNorm is now normalising with statistics that no longer match its input. The result is a model whose validation accuracy is mysteriously worse than its training accuracy in a way that is not overfitting. Fix: put dropout only after the last BatchNorm in the network — conventionally in the classifier head — or drop it entirely and lean on augmentation.

Stochastic depth / DropPath

Instead of dropping units, drop entire residual branches:

\[ \mathbf x_{l+1}=\mathbf x_l+\frac{b_l}{1-p_l}\,F_l(\mathbf x_l),\qquad b_l\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1-p_l) \]

When \(b_l=0\) the block becomes the identity, so the network trains at a random shallower depth each step. The drop probability is normally ramped linearly with depth, \(p_l=\frac{l}{L}p_{\max}\), because early layers extract features everything else depends on while late layers are more redundant. \(p_{\max}\) runs 0.1 for a small ViT up to 0.5 for a very deep one — it is one of the two or three hyperparameters that actually matter when training a ViT from scratch.

Label smoothing

Replace the one-hot target with a slightly softened one:

\[ y_k^{\text{LS}}=(1-\varepsilon)\,y_k+\frac{\varepsilon}{K} \]
What it does to the logits

With hard targets, cross-entropy is minimised only as \(\hat p_{\text{correct}}\to1\), which requires the correct logit to run away to \(+\infty\). Gradient descent will happily spend the entire run pushing one logit larger. With smoothing, the loss is minimised at a finite gap. For a uniform smoothing distribution, the optimal logit gap satisfies

\[ z_{\text{correct}}-z_{\text{other}}=\log\frac{(K-1)(1-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon} \]

With \(K=1000\) and \(\varepsilon=0.1\) that is a gap of about 9.1 — large, but bounded. The model stops burning capacity on unbounded confidence, which improves calibration (Lesson 43) and typically adds a few tenths of a percent of accuracy. The cost: smoothed models are worse teachers for knowledge distillation, because the smoothing destroys the fine structure in the non-target logits that distillation feeds on.

# PyTorch has it built in — no need to hand-roll the targets
criterion = torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss(label_smoothing=0.1)

# DropPath, the version used in timm / ViT / ConvNeXt
class DropPath(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, p=0.0):
        super().__init__()
        self.p = p

    def forward(self, x):
        if self.p == 0.0 or not self.training:
            return x
        keep = 1 - self.p
        # one Bernoulli sample PER EXAMPLE, broadcast over all other dims
        shape = (x.shape[0],) + (1,) * (x.ndim - 1)
        mask = x.new_empty(shape).bernoulli_(keep)
        return x * mask / keep
Pitfall · forgetting model.eval()

Evaluate with dropout still active and your validation numbers become noisy and pessimistic, and they change every time you run them. The signature is a validation loss that is higher than the training loss from the very first epoch, plus non-reproducible eval results. The mirror-image bug is forgetting model.train() after evaluating, which silently disables dropout and BatchNorm updates for the rest of training. Wrap both in your loop so neither can be forgotten.

The formula, exactly

For a mini-batch \(\mathcal B\) of size \(m\), per channel (or per feature):

\[ \mu_{\mathcal B}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m x_i,\qquad \sigma^2_{\mathcal B}=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m(x_i-\mu_{\mathcal B})^2 \] \[ \hat x_i=\frac{x_i-\mu_{\mathcal B}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\mathcal B}+\epsilon}},\qquad y_i=\gamma\,\hat x_i+\beta \]

Two things are easy to miss. First, \(\gamma\) and \(\beta\) are learnable, one pair per channel — the layer can undo its own normalisation if that is what the loss wants, so BN adds capacity rather than removing it. Second, \(\mu_{\mathcal B}\) and \(\sigma_{\mathcal B}\) are functions of the batch, so they are part of the computational graph: the backward pass has three terms, one flowing through \(\hat x\) and two more through the mean and variance. This is why BN makes each example's gradient depend on the other examples in its batch — a fact with consequences everywhere below.

The story, and the rebuttal

The 2015 paper explained BN as reducing "internal covariate shift" — the drift in each layer's input distribution as the layers below it update. It is an appealing story and it is largely wrong. Santurkar et al. (2018) injected deliberate distribution shift after BN layers and training was unaffected; they also measured covariate shift directly and found BN did not reliably reduce it.

The better explanation is that BN smooths the loss landscape. It reduces the Lipschitz constant of both the loss and its gradient, which means the gradient direction stays trustworthy over a larger step. That is a precise statement of the thing you actually observe: with BN you can raise the learning rate by an order of magnitude and it still converges. Know both stories — the original will be quoted at you in interviews, and the correction is the part that tells you what BN is good for.

Train and eval are different functions

At training time BN uses the batch statistics. At evaluation it must be deterministic and must work for a single example, so it uses running estimates accumulated during training:

\[ \mu_{\text{run}}\leftarrow(1-\alpha)\,\mu_{\text{run}}+\alpha\,\mu_{\mathcal B} \]

with \(\alpha=0.1\) in PyTorch (note: PyTorch's momentum argument is \(\alpha\), the weight on the new value — the opposite convention to TensorFlow and to optimizer momentum. This trips people up constantly).

The small-batch failure

The variance of a variance estimate from \(m\) samples scales as \(1/m\). At \(m=64\) the batch statistics are a decent proxy for the population; at \(m=2\) they are noise, and worse, the normalisation is dominated by whichever two examples happened to land together. Detection and segmentation models, which often run batch size 1–2 per GPU because the images are huge, were the original motivation for GroupNorm.

Pitfall · three BN bugs you will meet

Gradient accumulation does not simulate a big batch for BN. Accumulating 8 micro-batches of 4 gives you the gradient of batch 32 but the normalisation of batch 4, eight times over. BN statistics cannot be accumulated. This is a real and frequently missed limitation.

Fine-tuning with a tiny batch corrupts the running stats. You load a pretrained ResNet, fine-tune with batch size 4, and accuracy on the source domain collapses — because 200 steps of batch-4 statistics have overwritten estimates that took an ImageNet run to build. Fix: model.apply(lambda m: m.eval() if isinstance(m, nn.BatchNorm2d) else None) to freeze the BN layers in eval mode while still training the weights.

Bias before BN is dead weight. Conv2d(..., bias=True) followed by BN: the bias is added and then immediately subtracted by \(\mu_{\mathcal B}\). It contributes nothing but occupies parameters and an optimizer state. Always bias=False on a conv that feeds a BN.

Folding BN into the convolution for inference

At inference BN is a fixed affine map, and an affine map composed with a convolution is just another convolution. Fold it and you delete the layer entirely — typically a 20–30% latency win on mobile:

\[ W'=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\text{run}}+\epsilon}}\,W,\qquad b'=\beta-\frac{\gamma\,\mu_{\text{run}}}{\sqrt{\sigma^2_{\text{run}}+\epsilon}} \] 
@torch.no_grad()
def fold_bn(conv, bn):
    """Return a single Conv2d equivalent to conv followed by bn, at inference."""
    w = conv.weight.clone()
    scale = bn.weight / torch.sqrt(bn.running_var + bn.eps)   # gamma / sigma
    fused = torch.nn.Conv2d(conv.in_channels, conv.out_channels,
                            conv.kernel_size, conv.stride,
                            conv.padding, conv.dilation, conv.groups, bias=True)
    fused.weight.copy_(w * scale.reshape(-1, 1, 1, 1))
    b = conv.bias if conv.bias is not None else torch.zeros(conv.out_channels)
    fused.bias.copy_((b - bn.running_mean) * scale + bn.bias)
    return fused
Verify the fold

Put both versions in eval(), push the same random tensor through, and assert the max absolute difference is below \(10^{-5}\). If it is not, you almost certainly forgot eval() on the original — in train mode BN uses batch statistics and the two will never match.

They differ in exactly one thing: which axes you average over

Every normalization layer computes a mean and variance and rescales. The only thing separating BatchNorm, LayerNorm, InstanceNorm and GroupNorm is the set of axes the statistics are taken over. Fix that picture and the whole family collapses into one idea.

LayerNorm, and why sequences demand it

\[ \text{LN}(\mathbf x)=\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta,\qquad \mu=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d x_j,\quad \sigma^2=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d (x_j-\mu)^2 \]

Statistics are computed over the feature dimension of a single token. Nothing crosses the batch, so: batch size 1 works, train and eval are the same function, there are no running statistics, gradient accumulation is exact, and no cross-GPU synchronisation is needed. For variable-length padded sequences this is not a convenience but a requirement — batch statistics over a tensor that is 40% padding are meaningless.

RMSNorm — drop the mean

\[ \text{RMSNorm}(\mathbf x)=\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x}{\sqrt{\frac1d\sum_j x_j^2+\epsilon}} \]

No mean subtraction, no \(\beta\). The claim, borne out empirically, is that the re-centring in LayerNorm contributes almost nothing and the re-scaling does all the work. You save one pass over the vector and a parameter tensor, which is worth roughly 7–10% of layer latency — meaningful when you run it twice per block across 80 blocks. Llama, T5, Gemma, Mistral and most current open-weight models use RMSNorm.

class RMSNorm(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, eps=1e-6):
        super().__init__()
        self.eps = eps
        self.weight = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.ones(d))

    def forward(self, x):
        # compute in fp32 even under autocast: the sum of squares overflows fp16 easily
        dtype = x.dtype
        x = x.float()
        rms = torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + self.eps)
        return (x * rms).to(dtype) * self.weight

Pre-norm vs post-norm

The 2017 transformer put the norm after the residual add. Every modern model puts it before the sublayer:

\[ \text{post:}\ \ \mathbf x_{l+1}=\text{LN}\big(\mathbf x_l+F(\mathbf x_l)\big) \qquad\qquad \text{pre:}\ \ \mathbf x_{l+1}=\mathbf x_l+F\big(\text{LN}(\mathbf x_l)\big) \]

In the pre-norm form there is a clean identity path from the loss to every layer — nothing sits between \(\mathbf x_l\) and \(\mathbf x_{l+1}\) but an addition, exactly the condition from Lesson 28 that makes gradients flow. Post-norm puts a LayerNorm on that path, and its Jacobian shrinks the gradient at every one of the \(L\) layers.

Post-norm (2017)Pre-norm (modern)
WarmupRequired — diverges without itOptional, still usually used

Residual stream growthBounded by the normGrows with depth — needs a final norm before the head
Pitfall · the missing final norm

In a pre-norm stack, nothing normalises the residual stream on its way out of the last block, so its magnitude grows roughly like \(\sqrt L\). Forget the final LayerNorm before the output projection and the logits come out scaled wrong — training still works, but slowly and with a strange loss curve. Every reference implementation has this layer; it is easy to omit when writing your own, and you will meet it again in Lesson 85.

Choosing, in one line each

Conv net, batch ≥ 32 → BatchNorm. Conv net, tiny batch (detection, segmentation, 3D medical) → GroupNorm, \(G=32\). Anything sequential (transformer, RNN) → LayerNorm, or RMSNorm if you are writing it yourself. Style transfer / image-to-image → InstanceNorm, because you specifically want to erase per-image contrast statistics. Tiny batch on a transformer → nothing changes; LayerNorm never cared about your batch size.

What you are really doing

Augmentation is usually taught as "more data". That framing is misleading — a horizontally flipped cat carries almost no new information. The accurate framing is that you are declaring an invariance: you are telling the model that a specific transformation must not change the label. Every augmentation is a statement of prior knowledge about your task, and the statement can be false.

The test for whether an augmentation is legal

Apply it and ask: would a human expert still give the same label? Horizontal flip on ImageNet — yes. Horizontal flip on MNIST — no, a flipped 2 is not a 2, and a flipped 6 is definitely not a 9's problem you want. Vertical flip on satellite imagery — yes. Vertical flip on street scenes — no. Colour jitter on flowers — dangerous, since colour may be the class. Getting this wrong does not throw an error; it just costs you accuracy and you will never know why.

mixup

Take two examples and blend both inputs and labels with the same coefficient:

\[ \lambda\sim\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha),\qquad \tilde{\mathbf x}=\lambda\mathbf x_i+(1-\lambda)\mathbf x_j,\qquad \tilde{\mathbf y}=\lambda\mathbf y_i+(1-\lambda)\mathbf y_j \]

The blended image looks like a double exposure and means nothing to a human, yet it works consistently. The reason is that it forces the model's response to be linear between training points, which suppresses the wildly confident predictions that networks otherwise make in the empty space between classes. That is why mixup improves calibration (Lesson 43) at least as much as it improves accuracy, and why it also reduces vulnerability to adversarial examples.

CutMix is the same idea with a spatial cut rather than a blend: paste a rectangular patch of image \(j\) into image \(i\), and set \(\lambda\) to the fraction of area retained. It preserves local statistics that mixup destroys, and it usually beats mixup on convnets.

Pitfall · four ways augmentation goes wrong

Augmenting the validation set. Your validation number now measures performance on distorted images and is not comparable to anything. Validation transforms must be deterministic. This bug makes your model look worse than it is, so people rarely investigate.

Ordering RandomErasing before Normalize. Erasing to 0 before normalisation means erasing to black, a real and unusual pixel value. After normalisation, 0 is the dataset mean — a genuinely uninformative value. The order matters.

Augmenting so hard the model cannot fit. If training accuracy never rises above validation accuracy, your augmentation is stronger than your model. Turn it down. Heavy augmentation is for big models on small datasets.

Assuming text works like images. Synonym replacement and random deletion frequently hurt modern NLP models, because a pretrained language model already knows what a synonym is and your edit mostly introduces ungrammatical noise. Back-translation is the one that reliably survives. Be sceptical here in a way you need not be with images.

Why random search beats grid search

Pitfall · sampling learning rates uniformly

uniform(1e-5, 1e-1) puts 90% of its samples above \(10^{-2}\) — a region where almost every model diverges. You will run 50 trials and see 45 NaNs. Learning rates, weight decay and \(\epsilon\) are scale parameters and must be sampled log-uniformly: 10 ** uniform(-5, -1), or trial.suggest_float("lr", 1e-5, 1e-1, log=True). This single mistake wastes more compute than any other in tuning.

Beyond random: spend less on bad trials

Random search is embarrassingly parallel and hard to beat on a small budget, but it is wasteful in one specific way — it runs every trial to completion, including the obviously terrible ones. The two fixes are orthogonal and compose:

  • Model where to sample next — Bayesian optimization fits a surrogate over the objective and picks points with high expected improvement. TPE (the Optuna default) instead models \(p(\text{config}\mid\text{good})\) and \(p(\text{config}\mid\text{bad})\) and maximises their ratio, which handles conditional and categorical spaces gracefully.
  • Kill bad trials early — Successive Halving runs \(n\) configs for a small budget, keeps the top \(1/\eta\), multiplies their budget by \(\eta\), repeats. Hyperband wraps this in an outer loop over the aggressiveness of the halving; ASHA makes it asynchronous so no worker ever idles waiting for a rung to fill. This is usually the bigger win of the two.
  • Population-based training — train a population in parallel, and periodically have poor performers copy a good performer's weights and perturb its hyperparameters. Uniquely, this produces a schedule rather than a fixed value, which is why it works well for RL and for augmentation strength.

Tune small, transfer up

You cannot afford 60 trials of your largest model. The standard move is to tune on a smaller proxy — fewer layers, narrower, fewer steps — and transfer the result. Naively this fails, because the optimal learning rate shifts as width grows. Maximal Update Parametrization (μP) fixes the parametrization so that the optimal LR is width-invariant, letting you tune a 40M model and use the same LR at 6B. If you are ever going to train something large, this idea saves more compute than anything else in this lesson.

Pitfall · tuning on the test set

Run 200 trials, select the best by test accuracy, and report that number. It is inflated, and the amount of inflation grows with the number of trials — you have used the test set as a training signal 200 times. Use a validation split for all selection, and touch the test set exactly once, at the end, after every decision is frozen. If your dataset is too small to give up a validation split, use nested cross-validation and accept the cost. Report the search budget alongside the number; "best of 200 trials" and "best of 3" are not the same claim.

The single most useful test in deep learning

Before anything else: take one mini-batch, turn off augmentation, dropout and weight decay, and train on that batch alone for a few hundred steps. A correct network with a correct training loop will drive the loss to essentially zero — it has more than enough capacity to memorise 32 examples. If it cannot, you have a bug, and it is in the code rather than in the hyperparameters.

def overfit_one_batch(model, batch, criterion, steps=300, lr=1e-3):
    model.train()
    xb, yb = batch
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=lr)   # no weight decay
    for i in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        loss = criterion(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        if i % 50 == 0 or i == steps - 1:
            print(f"step {i:4d}  loss {loss.item():.6f}")
    assert loss.item() < 1e-3, "cannot overfit one batch -> there is a bug, not a tuning problem"
What the failure modes tell you

Loss is flat from step 0 → gradients are not reaching the parameters. Check requires_grad, check that you did not detach() something, check that the optimizer was given the right parameter list. Loss falls then plateaus well above zero → the labels are not deducible from the inputs, i.e. label misalignment. Loss goes to NaN → see the NaN section. Loss goes to zero → your model and loop are fine, and every remaining problem is data, regularisation or optimisation.

Check the initial loss

Before a single step, a correctly initialised classifier should predict close to uniform, so the loss should be \(\log K\). For 10 classes that is 2.303; for 1000 classes, 6.908. For binary cross-entropy it is \(\log 2=0.693\). If your run starts at 8.5 on a 10-class problem, the output layer has been initialised with a large scale, or you double-applied a softmax, or your labels are out of range. This costs five seconds and catches an enormous class of bugs.

Pitfall · the double softmax

This is the most common silent bug in the whole of deep learning, because nothing crashes. You write return F.softmax(self.fc(x), dim=1) because it feels tidy, then pass that to CrossEntropyLoss, which softmaxes it again. The model still trains — just badly, converging to maybe 85% where it should reach 98%. The tell is that your logits, once printed, all sit inside \([0,1]\). Your model's forward should return raw logits, full stop; apply softmax only in the code that displays a probability to a human.

Hunting NaNs

NaNs come from a short list of places: \(\log(0)\), \(0/0\), \(\sqrt{0}\) in the backward pass (the gradient of \(\sqrt x\) at 0 is infinite), overflow in fp16 (Lesson 44), and a learning rate high enough to blow the weights up in one step. Find the first one, not the thousandth:

# 1. Which layer produces the first non-finite value?
def nan_hook(name):
    def hook(module, inp, out):
        t = out[0] if isinstance(out, tuple) else out
        if torch.is_tensor(t) and not torch.isfinite(t).all():
            raise RuntimeError(f"non-finite output in {name} ({module.__class__.__name__})")
    return hook

for name, m in model.named_modules():
    m.register_forward_hook(nan_hook(name))

# 2. Or let autograd find it in the BACKWARD pass (slow — debug runs only)
with torch.autograd.detect_anomaly():
    loss.backward()

# 3. Standing guard in the real loop: skip the step, do not poison the weights
if not torch.isfinite(loss):
    print("non-finite loss, skipping batch")
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    continue

The checklist

Twenty things to verify, in order
  1. Can you overfit one batch to ~0 loss?
  2. Is the initial loss \(\log K\)?
  3. Have you looked at 20 raw training examples after the full transform pipeline, with their labels, plotted?
  4. Do the label indices actually fall in \([0,K)\)?
  5. Are inputs normalised with statistics computed on the training split only?
  6. Does forward return raw logits?
  7. Is the loss paired correctly with the output (table above)?
  8. Is zero_grad called every step?
  9. Is model.train() / model.eval() set in the right places?
  10. Is the optimizer holding the parameters of the model you are actually training?
  11. Do all parameters have non-None, non-zero .grad after a backward?
  12. Is the update-to-weight ratio near \(10^{-3}\) (Lesson 28)?
  13. Does the validation set overlap the training set?
  14. Do X and y stay aligned through every shuffle and every split?
  15. Is the scheduler stepped at the right granularity?
  16. Is augmentation applied to train only?
  17. Does the loss curve respond at all to a 10× learning-rate change?
  18. Is the model on the same device as the data, and in the expected dtype?
  19. Does a random-guess baseline actually score what you expect?
  20. Does a trivial baseline (majority class, or nearest neighbour on raw pixels) beat you? If so, stop tuning and go read your data.
Pitfall · debugging the model when the bug is in the data

A large majority of "the model won't learn" reports are label bugs, leakage, or a transform that destroys the signal. Item 3 on that list — plot the images after the transform pipeline, with their labels — finds more bugs than any amount of architecture tinkering, and almost nobody does it first. If your normalisation constants are wrong, the plotted images will look obviously wrong. If your labels are shifted by one, you will see it instantly. What to log

Loss and accuracy are the obvious ones and the least diagnostic. The list that actually saves you:

  • Learning rate at every step — so you can align loss drops with schedule events.
  • Gradient norm, global and per-layer group. A spike here precedes a loss spike; a norm collapsing to \(10^{-8}\) is a dead layer.
  • Update-to-weight ratio \(\eta\|\Delta w\|/\|w\|\) — should sit near \(10^{-3}\) (Lesson 28).
  • Throughput in examples/sec and the GPU utilisation. A run at 30% utilisation is a data-loading problem, and you will not notice it from the loss curve.
  • The full config, the git commit hash, and whether the working tree was dirty.
  • A sample of predictions — a grid of the worst-scoring validation examples each epoch. This is the single most informative artifact and almost nobody logs it.

Seeding — and the bug in every tutorial

import os, random, numpy as np, torch

def seed_everything(seed: int):
    os.environ["PYTHONHASHSEED"] = str(seed)
    random.seed(seed)
    np.random.seed(seed)
    torch.manual_seed(seed)

def seed_worker(worker_id):
    # torch gives each worker a distinct base seed; derive python/numpy seeds FROM it
    s = torch.initial_seed() % 2**32
    np.random.seed(s)
    random.seed(s)

loader = DataLoader(ds, batch_size=64, num_workers=8, shuffle=True,
                    worker_init_fn=seed_worker,
                    generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed))
Pitfall · every DataLoader worker generating the same "random" numbers

When PyTorch forks worker processes, each inherits the parent's NumPy random state unchanged. If your Dataset.__getitem__ uses np.random for augmentation — which is extremely common — then with 8 workers, examples 0–7 of every batch receive identical augmentation, as do 8–15, and so on. Your augmentation diversity silently drops by a factor of 8. This has been found in published codebases. The worker_init_fn above is the fix; alternatively use torch's RNG inside the dataset, which PyTorch seeds per-worker correctly.

How much determinism is worth paying for

Full bitwise determinism requires torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True) and cudnn.benchmark=False, and costs real throughput — some ops have no deterministic kernel and will simply raise. It is worth it while chasing a specific bug, and rarely worth it otherwise.

What you actually need is reproducible conclusions, not reproducible bits. That means: run every important comparison with 3 seeds and report mean ± std. If your improvement is 0.3% and your seed-to-seed standard deviation is 0.4%, you have not measured an improvement — you have measured noise, and no amount of determinism flags will change that.

Two phenomena that break the classical story

Double descent

Classical statistics predicts a U-shaped test error as model capacity grows: underfit, sweet spot, overfit. What large networks actually show is that test error rises to a peak at the interpolation threshold — the point where the model has just enough parameters to fit the training set exactly — and then falls again as you keep growing. Past that threshold, more parameters make things better. This is why "bigger model, more overfitting" is bad advice in the modern regime, and why the field kept scaling long past the point classical theory said to stop. The same shape appears along the epoch axis too, which is why a val loss that turns up at epoch 40 may turn back down by epoch 200.

Grokking

On small algorithmic tasks, a network will reach 100% training accuracy while validation accuracy sits at chance — and then, tens of thousands of steps later, with training loss already flat, validation accuracy suddenly jumps to 100%. The model memorised first and generalised much later, once weight decay had ground away the memorising solution and left the structured one. The practical lesson is narrow but real: a flat training loss does not mean nothing is happening. Watch weight norms and validation, not just training loss, before killing a run.

Before you believe any curve

Establish the baselines first. What does random guessing score? What does predicting the majority class score? What does logistic regression on raw features score? What would a competent human score? A validation accuracy of 92% means nothing until you know that the majority class is 91%. Most of the time this takes ten minutes and reframes the entire project.

Everything comes from four numbers

\[ \text{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\qquad \text{recall}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad F_\beta=(1+\beta^2)\frac{PR}{\beta^2 P+R} \]

Precision answers "when I raise an alarm, how often am I right?"; recall answers "of everything I should have caught, how much did I catch?". \(F_1\) is their harmonic mean; \(\beta>1\) weights recall higher (use \(F_2\) for disease screening, where a miss is far worse than a false alarm), \(\beta<1\) weights precision.

Pitfall · accuracy on imbalanced data

A fraud detector on a dataset with 0.2% fraud can hit 99.8% accuracy by predicting "not fraud" every single time. It has recall 0, precision undefined, and zero value. If your positive class is below about 10%, accuracy is not a metric, it is a decoration. Report precision, recall and PR-AUC instead, and always show the confusion matrix.

ROC-AUC versus PR-AUC — the distinction that matters

Both summarise performance across all thresholds, but they use different denominators, and on imbalanced data they tell very different stories:

\[ \text{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}\ \ \text{(uses positives)},\qquad \text{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN}\ \ \text{(uses negatives)} \]

When negatives vastly outnumber positives, \(TN\) is enormous, so FPR stays tiny even when the model produces a flood of false positives. ROC-AUC therefore looks flattering. Precision, \(TP/(TP+FP)\), has no \(TN\) anywhere in it — it feels every false positive directly.

A concrete case

1,000,000 negatives, 1,000 positives. Your model catches 900 positives and raises 10,000 false alarms.

TPR \(=900/1000=0.90\). FPR \(=10{,}000/1{,}000{,}000=0.01\). On an ROC curve that is a point near the top-left — excellent. But precision \(=900/10{,}900=\mathbf{8.3\%}\): twelve out of every thirteen alarms are false. Whoever has to review those alerts will tell you the model is unusable, and the PR curve agrees with them. Use PR-AUC when the positive class is rare and the cost lands on false positives.

Calibration

A model is calibrated if, among predictions made with confidence 0.8, exactly 80% are correct. Modern networks are badly overconfident — a ResNet's average confidence far exceeds its accuracy, and the gap grew as architectures got deeper. If you use the probability for anything downstream (thresholding, expected-cost decisions, abstaining, or feeding another system) this matters as much as accuracy.

Expected Calibration Error bins predictions by confidence and measures the gap:

\[ \text{ECE}=\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{|S_b|}{n}\Big|\operatorname{acc}(S_b)-\operatorname{conf}(S_b)\Big| \]

Temperature scaling fixes most of it with a single parameter. Divide the logits by \(T\) and fit \(T\) on the validation set by minimising NLL. Because it is monotonic it cannot change any prediction or any accuracy — it only rescales confidence.

class TemperatureScaler(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.log_T = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(1))    # T = exp(log_T), keeps T > 0

    def forward(self, logits):
        return logits / self.log_T.exp()

    def fit(self, val_logits, val_labels):
        opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([self.log_T], lr=0.1, max_iter=60)
        nll = torch.nn.CrossEntropyLoss()
        def closure():
            opt.zero_grad()
            loss = nll(self(val_logits), val_labels)
            loss.backward()
            return loss
        opt.step(closure)
        return self.log_T.exp().item()      # typically lands between 1.2 and 2.5
Report an interval, not a point

On a validation set of \(n\) examples, an accuracy of \(p\) has standard error \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). At \(n=1000\) and \(p=0.9\) that is 0.95%, so the 95% interval is roughly \(\pm 1.9\) points. Your "92.1% vs 91.4%" improvement is inside the noise. For anything more complex than accuracy, bootstrap: resample the validation set with replacement 1000 times, recompute, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Then add seed variance on top of that.

Range versus precision

FormatExponent / mantissaMax valueDecimal digitsVerdict
fp328 / 233.4e38~7The reference. Master weights live here.
fp165 / 1065,504~3Good precision, terrible range. Needs loss scaling.
bf168 / 73.4e38~2fp32's exponent, fp16's size. Just works. Ampere and newer.

That table is the whole story. bf16 sacrifices mantissa bits to keep fp32's exponent, so it cannot overflow where fp32 would not, and it needs no loss scaling. fp16 keeps precision but its maximum is 65,504 — and gradients routinely underflow below its minimum normal value of \(6\times10^{-5}\), silently flushing to zero. If your GPU supports bf16, use bf16.

Why fp16 needs loss scaling

Gradients are small — typically \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-7}\). Much of that range falls below fp16's smallest normal number and rounds to exactly zero. The fix is to multiply the loss by a large constant \(S\) before the backward pass. By linearity every gradient is scaled by \(S\) too, lifting it into representable range; you then divide by \(S\) before the optimizer step.

GradScaler chooses \(S\) dynamically: start at 65,536, and whenever any gradient comes back non-finite, skip that step and halve \(S\); after 2000 consecutive clean steps, double it. This is why an AMP run legitimately skips a handful of early steps — that is the scaler calibrating, not a bug.

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")          # a no-op if you use bf16
ACC = 4                                        # micro-batches per optimizer step

for i, (xb, yb) in enumerate(loader):
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        loss = criterion(model(xb), yb) / ACC   # DIVIDE — see the pitfall below

    scaler.scale(loss).backward()

    if (i + 1) % ACC == 0:
        scaler.unscale_(opt)                    # unscale BEFORE clipping, or you clip the scale
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        scaler.step(opt)
        scaler.update()
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        sched.step()
Pitfall · four ways to get accumulation wrong

Forgetting to divide the loss. Accumulating 4 micro-batches without / ACC gives you a gradient 4× too large, which is equivalent to quietly quadrupling your learning rate.

Clipping before unscaling. With fp16, gradients are inflated by \(S\approx 65{,}536\). Clip to norm 1.0 at that point and you have effectively clipped to \(1.5\times10^{-5}\), destroying the update. Always scaler.unscale_(opt) first.

Stepping the scheduler per micro-batch. The schedule then runs \(ACC\times\) too fast.

Expecting BatchNorm to be fooled. As in Lesson 36 — accumulation gives you the gradient of a large batch, but normalisation statistics of the small one. Under DDP, also wrap the non-stepping micro-batches in model.no_sync(), or you pay a full all-reduce on every micro-batch and lose most of the benefit.

Where the memory actually goes

For a model with \(P\) parameters trained in mixed precision with AdamW, the fixed cost per parameter is roughly:

\[ \underbrace{2}_{\text{bf16 weights}}+\underbrace{4}_{\text{fp32 master}}+\underbrace{4}_{\text{fp32 grad}}+\underbrace{8}_{\text{Adam }m,v} \;=\; \mathbf{18\ \text{bytes/param}} \]

A 7B model therefore needs about 126 GB before a single activation is stored — which is why an 80 GB A100 cannot fine-tune 7B naively, and why ZeRO, FSDP, LoRA and 8-bit optimizers all exist (Lessons 94 and 96). Activations are the variable part, and they scale with batch size × sequence length × depth.

Activation checkpointing

Backprop needs the forward activations (Lesson 22). Checkpointing keeps only a few and recomputes the rest during the backward pass. Storing every \(\sqrt n\)-th activation in an \(n\)-layer network cuts activation memory from \(O(n)\) to \(O(\sqrt n)\) at the cost of one extra forward pass — about 30% more compute for a memory reduction that is frequently the difference between running and not running.

from torch.utils.checkpoint import checkpoint

class Block(torch.nn.Module):
    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.mlp(self.norm(x))

# use_reentrant=False is the modern implementation; the old one breaks with some autograd features
for blk in model.blocks:
    blk.forward = lambda x, b=blk: checkpoint(b._forward, x, use_reentrant=False)
OOM triage, in order of what to try first
  1. Reduce batch size and add gradient accumulation to keep the effective batch — costs nothing but time.
  2. Turn on mixed precision if it is not already on — halves activations.
  3. Turn on gradient checkpointing — the biggest single lever for deep models.
  4. Switch to an 8-bit optimizer — saves 6 bytes/param.
  5. Check for accidental retention: are you appending loss instead of loss.item() to a list? That keeps the entire graph alive (Lesson 12).
  6. torch.cuda.memory_summary() and torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() before guessing further.
  7. Only then: a smaller model, or LoRA, or more GPUs.

Why it works

Visualise the filters of any trained convnet (you will do this in Lesson 64) and the first layer always looks the same: oriented edges, colour blobs, spatial frequencies. Nobody designed that — it is what gradient descent discovers because it is what natural images are made of. Those features are not specific to ImageNet's thousand classes; they are specific to images. Depth increases specificity: edges → textures → object parts → whole objects. So the general rule is that early layers transfer almost anywhere and late layers transfer only to similar tasks, and everything below follows from that one sentence.

Discriminative learning rates

Early layers need barely any change; the new head needs a lot. Give them different learning rates, decaying geometrically as you go down (ULMFiT used \(\eta_{l-1}=\eta_l/2.6\); most vision recipes use a layer decay of 0.65–0.9):

def param_groups_llrd(model, base_lr, decay=0.75):
    """Layer-wise LR decay: the head gets base_lr, each block below gets decay x less."""
    blocks = list(model.blocks)                 # deepest last
    groups, n = [], len(blocks)
    groups.append({"params": model.head.parameters(), "lr": base_lr})
    for i, blk in enumerate(reversed(blocks)):  # i=0 is the last block
        groups.append({"params": blk.parameters(), "lr": base_lr * (decay ** (i + 1))})
    groups.append({"params": model.stem.parameters(), "lr": base_lr * (decay ** (n + 1))})
    return groups

opt = torch.optim.AdamW(param_groups_llrd(model, base_lr=1e-3), weight_decay=0.01)

Progressive unfreezing is the discrete version: train the head alone for an epoch or two so its random weights stop sending garbage gradients into the backbone, then unfreeze one block at a time. Do the head-only warmup even if you plan to fine-tune everything — it takes two epochs and reliably improves the final number.

Pitfall · BatchNorm during fine-tuning

Two independent problems, and the second is subtle. One: as in Lesson 36, fine-tuning with a small batch overwrites carefully-learned running statistics with noise. Two: "freezing" a backbone with requires_grad=False does not freeze BatchNorm's running statistics — those are buffers, not parameters, and they keep updating on every forward pass in train mode. Your "frozen" feature extractor is therefore drifting. You must also put those modules in eval():

for p in backbone.parameters():
    p.requires_grad = False
backbone.eval()                        # stops running-stat updates AND dropout
for m in backbone.modules():           # belt and braces if you re-enter train() later
    if isinstance(m, torch.nn.modules.batchnorm._BatchNorm):
        m.eval()
Catastrophic forgetting

Fine-tune hard on a narrow task and the model loses ability on everything else — the weights that encoded the old capability are simply overwritten, because nothing in the loss is defending them. The mitigations are all versions of "stay close to where you started": low learning rate, few epochs, layer-wise decay, freezing, replaying a mix of old data, or restricting the update to a low-rank subspace (LoRA, Lesson 96). This same phenomenon reappears at the frontier as the "alignment tax" in Lesson 97 — it is not a small-model problem.

How much data you actually need

With a good pretrained backbone: 10–50 images per class gets a usable linear probe. 100–500 makes fine-tuning worthwhile. 1000+ and you are in comfortable territory. These numbers are one to two orders of magnitude below what training from scratch needs, and that gap is the entire reason transfer learning dominates practice. Before collecting more data, always run the linear probe first — it takes minutes and tells you whether your problem is data-limited or feature-limited.
