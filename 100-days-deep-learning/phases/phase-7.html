The shift

A discriminative model estimates \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)\). A generative model estimates \(p(\mathbf x)\) — or at least gives you a way to sample from it. That is a strictly harder problem: to generate a convincing photograph you must implicitly know about geometry, lighting, materials and object structure, none of which anyone labelled.

The trilemma

FamilySample qualityDiversity / coverageSampling speed
AutoregressiveHighHighSlow — one element at a time
VAEBlurryHighOne forward pass
GANVery highMode collapseOne forward pass
FlowsModerateHighFast
DiffusionVery highHighSlow — but see Lesson 108

For years the received wisdom was that you could pick two of the three. Diffusion took quality and diversity, accepted slow sampling, and then attacked that weakness with distillation and few-step methods — which is a useful strategic lesson: take the two properties that are hard to fix, and engineer your way out of the one that is merely expensive.

Why VAEs are blurry and GANs are not

It follows from the loss. A VAE minimises reconstruction error, typically MSE, which as Lesson 21 showed corresponds to a Gaussian likelihood — and the optimal prediction under MSE when the model is uncertain is the mean of the plausible outputs. Average several plausible faces and you get a blur. A GAN's loss says nothing about matching any particular target; it says "make the discriminator unable to tell". A blurry image is trivially detectable, so blur is punished directly. Different loss, different failure mode, and the difference is entirely explained by Lesson 8's maximum-likelihood framing.

Pitfall · evaluating generative models

There is no good single metric. FID compares the mean and covariance of Inception features between real and generated sets, and it is the standard — but it is sensitive to sample count, to the resizing and JPEG settings used, and to the fact that Inception was trained on ImageNet, so it measures ImageNet-like features. Inception Score is worse: it cannot detect mode collapse within a class and it ignores the real data entirely. Report Precision/Recall for generative models (quality and coverage separately), fix your sample count and preprocessing, and state them. Two papers reporting "FID 8.3" may not be comparable at all.

The setup

\[ \mathbf z=f_\phi(\mathbf x),\qquad \hat{\mathbf x}=g_\theta(\mathbf z),\qquad \mathcal L=\|\mathbf x-\hat{\mathbf x}\|^2 \]

Compress and reconstruct. With \(\dim(\mathbf z)\ll\dim(\mathbf x)\) the network must discard something, and what survives is whatever is most useful for reconstruction — a learned compression.

A linear autoencoder is PCA

With linear \(f\) and \(g\) and squared error, the optimal solution spans the same subspace as the top-\(k\) principal components (Lesson 4) — the reconstruction error is minimised by projecting onto the top-\(k\) singular directions, which is exactly Eckart–Young. The autoencoder does not recover the individual components (any rotation within the subspace is equally optimal) but the subspace is identical. Non-linear activations are what make an autoencoder more than PCA: it can then follow a curved manifold rather than a flat one.

The variants, and what each regulariser buys

VariantChangeEffect
DenoisingCorrupt the input, reconstruct the clean originalCannot copy; must learn structure. The direct ancestor of masked language modelling and of diffusion.
SparsePenalty forcing most units near zeroOvercomplete latent that is still forced to be selective. Now central to interpretability (Lesson 117).
ContractivePenalise the encoder Jacobian \(\|\partial f/\partial\mathbf x\|_F^2\)Representation is locally invariant to small input changes

Anomaly detection actually works

Train on normal data only. Anomalies reconstruct badly because the model never learned to represent them, so reconstruction error is an anomaly score. This is one of the genuinely reliable practical uses of autoencoders — industrial defect detection, network intrusion, equipment monitoring — and it needs no anomaly labels, which is usually the whole problem.

Pitfall · the threshold, and the too-powerful decoder

Set the threshold on a validation set containing known anomalies, using the precision/recall trade from Lesson 43 — picking "mean + 3σ of training error" is arbitrary and usually wrong. And keep the bottleneck genuinely narrow: a sufficiently expressive autoencoder learns to reconstruct anything, including inputs unlike its training data, at which point the anomaly score goes flat and the method silently stops working.

Why you cannot generate from a plain autoencoder

Sample a random \(\mathbf z\), decode it, and you get noise. Nothing in the objective constrained where in latent space the encoder puts things — it is free to scatter the training data into disconnected islands with vast empty regions between them, and it will, because that makes reconstruction easiest. The decoder has never seen the empty regions and produces garbage there. To generate, you need the latent distribution to be something you can sample from. That single requirement is what produces the VAE.

The model

Posit that data is generated by first drawing a latent \(\mathbf z\sim p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,I)\) and then \(\mathbf x\sim p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\). The likelihood we want to maximise is

\[ p_\theta(\mathbf x)=\int p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\,p(\mathbf z)\,\d\mathbf z \]

which is intractable — it integrates over every possible latent. And the posterior \(p_\theta(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\) we would need in order to train is equally intractable. Variational inference sidesteps both by introducing an approximate posterior \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\), which is the encoder.

The ELBO, derived

Start from the log-likelihood and insert \(q_\phi\):

\[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf x)=\log\int p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)\,\d\mathbf z =\log\,\E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)}\!\left[\frac{p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)}{q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)}\right] \]

Apply Jensen's inequality (\(\log\E[\cdot]\ge\E[\log\cdot]\), since \(\log\) is concave):

\[ \log p_\theta(\mathbf x)\ \ge\ \E_{q_\phi}\big[\log p_\theta(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\big]-\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)\ =\ \mathcal L_{\text{ELBO}} \]

The gap between \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf x)\) and the ELBO is exactly \(\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\big)\) — the error in our posterior approximation. So maximising the ELBO does two things at once: it pushes up the likelihood, and it pulls \(q_\phi\) towards the true posterior. That is the whole idea of variational inference, and it is worth sitting with.

The two terms read cleanly. Reconstruction: decode \(\mathbf z\) and match \(\mathbf x\). KL: keep the encoder's output distribution close to \(\mathcal N(0,I)\), which is exactly the constraint the plain autoencoder was missing. For Gaussian \(q_\phi=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\sigma^2)\) the KL has a closed form:

\[ \mathrm{KL}=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-\log\sigma_j^2-1\Big) \]
The ELBO trade-off · reconstruction against KL, and where the latent stops carrying information
class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_in, d_hid, d_lat):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_in, d_hid), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(d_hid, 2 * d_lat))       # mu and logvar
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d_lat, d_hid), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(d_hid, d_in))

    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.enc(x).chunk(2, dim=-1)
        # predict LOG variance, never variance: it is unconstrained and exp() keeps sigma > 0
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
        z = mu + std * torch.randn_like(std)                        # reparameterization
        return self.dec(z), mu, logvar

def elbo(x_hat, x, mu, logvar, beta=1.0):
    rec = F.mse_loss(x_hat, x, reduction="sum") / x.size(0)
    kl  = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + logvar - mu.pow(2) - logvar.exp()) / x.size(0)
    return rec + beta * kl, rec, kl
Pitfall · posterior collapse

If the decoder is powerful enough to model the data on its own — an autoregressive decoder, or a PixelCNN — the cheapest way to minimise the ELBO is to set \(q_\phi(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)=p(\mathbf z)\) exactly. The KL term goes to zero, the latent carries no information about the input, and the decoder ignores it entirely. Your "VAE" is now just a language model with a decorative unused input. The symptom is a KL term that drops to near zero early and stays there. Fixes: KL annealing (ramp \(\beta\) from 0), free bits (do not penalise KL below a floor per dimension), or weakening the decoder. Watch the KL term as a training metric, not just the total loss.

β-VAE and VQ-VAE

β-VAE simply weights the KL term. \(\beta>1\) forces a tighter information bottleneck, which empirically encourages each latent dimension to capture an independent factor of variation — rotation, size, colour. Disentanglement is real but fragile, and a later analysis showed that unsupervised disentanglement is impossible without inductive biases, and that reported successes depended heavily on hyperparameters and random seed. Treat the demos with some scepticism.

VQ-VAE replaces the continuous latent with a lookup into a learned codebook, making the latent a grid of discrete tokens. That is the idea that matters most downstream: once an image is a sequence of tokens, you can model it with a transformer. VQ-VAE underpins DALL·E 1, and its continuous cousin is the compressor inside Stable Diffusion (Lesson 108).

The game

\[ \min_G\max_D\ V(D,G)=\E_{\mathbf x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(\mathbf x)\big]+\E_{\mathbf z\sim p_{\mathbf z}}\big[\log\big(1-D(G(\mathbf z))\big)\big] \]

The discriminator learns to tell real from fake; the generator learns to fool it. No explicit density, no reconstruction target — just a learned, adaptive loss function that gets harder as the generator improves.

The optimum, and the Jensen-Shannon connection

For a fixed \(G\), the optimal discriminator is found by maximising the integrand pointwise:

\[ D^\ast(\mathbf x)=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf x)}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf x)+p_g(\mathbf x)} \]

Substitute it back into \(V\) and, after adding and subtracting \(\log 4\), you get

\[ V(D^\ast,G)=2\cdot\mathrm{JSD}\big(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g\big)-\log 4 \]

So at the optimum the generator is minimising the Jensen-Shannon divergence (Lesson 8), whose unique minimum is \(p_g=p_{\text{data}}\). Elegant — and it contains the theory's own fatal flaw, below.

Why GANs are hard to train · the optimal discriminator, and the gradient it leaves behind
Why the original loss saturates, and the one-line fix

Early in training \(G\) is terrible, so \(D(G(\mathbf z))\approx0\). The generator's gradient of \(\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))\) at that point is nearly zero — the loss is flat exactly where the generator most needs signal. This is the sigmoid saturation problem from Lesson 20, in a new costume.

The fix, in the original paper: instead of minimising \(\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))\), maximise \(\log D(G(\mathbf z))\). Same fixed point, opposite gradient behaviour — strong signal when the generator is bad, weak when it is already fooling \(D\). Every GAN implementation uses this non-saturating form, and it is a good example of two objectives being equivalent at the optimum and wildly different under gradient descent.

Pitfall · a discriminator that is too good

Here is the flaw hiding in the JS derivation. If \(p_{\text{data}}\) and \(p_g\) have disjoint support — which they essentially always do, since both are low-dimensional manifolds in a very high-dimensional pixel space — then JS divergence is constant at \(\log 2\), and its gradient is exactly zero everywhere. A perfect discriminator gives the generator nothing to learn from. This is the central instability of GAN training and it is not a tuning problem; it is the objective. It is why practitioners deliberately handicap the discriminator, and it is precisely what WGAN was designed to fix.

The failure modes, and what they look like

FailureSymptomMitigation
Mode collapseEvery sample looks the same, or cycles between a few outputsMinibatch discrimination, unrolled GAN, WGAN-GP, more diverse noise
Vanishing D gradientD loss → 0, G loss climbs, samples stop improvingWeaken D, label smoothing, noise on D's inputs, switch to Wasserstein
OscillationLosses cycle forever; no convergenceTwo time-scale update rule (different LRs for G and D), spectral norm
Checkerboard artifactsRegular grid pattern in outputsKernel size divisible by stride, or upsample + conv (Lesson 49)
There is no loss curve to read

This is the part that most disorients people coming from supervised learning. In a GAN, the losses are not a progress metric. Both networks are improving against a moving opponent, so a falling generator loss can mean the generator improved or that the discriminator got worse. The only reliable signals are looking at samples, and FID computed periodically against a held-out set. Log a fixed grid of samples from fixed noise vectors every N steps and watch it as a film; that is the actual instrument.

Wasserstein GAN

Replace JS divergence with the Earth Mover (Wasserstein-1) distance:

\[ W(p_r,p_g)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(p_r,p_g)}\E_{(x,y)\sim\gamma}\big[\|x-y\|\big] \]

The intuition is literal: the minimum cost of moving one pile of earth into the shape of the other, where cost is mass times distance. Crucially, this is finite and continuous even when the supports are disjoint — two non-overlapping distributions 3 units apart have \(W=3\), and if you move one closer, \(W\) decreases smoothly. JS would have been stuck at \(\log 2\) the whole time. That is the entire fix.

The infimum is intractable, so WGAN uses the Kantorovich-Rubinstein dual, which requires the critic \(f\) to be 1-Lipschitz:

\[ W(p_r,p_g)=\sup_{\|f\|_L\le1}\ \E_{x\sim p_r}[f(x)]-\E_{x\sim p_g}[f(x)] \]
Enforcing LipschitzHowVerdict
Weight clippingClamp all weights to \([-c,c]\)Crude. Pushes weights to the clip boundaries, wastes capacity, and \(c\) is very sensitive.
Gradient penalty (WGAN-GP)Penalise \(\big(\|\nabla_{\hat x}f(\hat x)\|_2-1\big)^2\) on points interpolated between real and fakeThe standard. Note: use no BatchNorm in the critic — GP is defined per-example and BN couples them.
Spectral normDivide each weight matrix by its largest singular value, estimated by one power iteration per stepCheapest and most robust. Bounds the Lipschitz constant by construction rather than by penalty.

Conditional and paired translation

cGAN conditions both networks on a label \(y\), so you can ask for a specific class. pix2pix is image-to-image with paired data: a U-Net generator (Lesson 59), a PatchGAN discriminator that classifies overlapping patches rather than the whole image, and an L1 term alongside the adversarial loss. The L1 gets the low frequencies roughly right; the adversarial term supplies the high-frequency detail that L1 would have blurred. That division of labour is a good general pattern.

CycleGAN — translation without pairs

Paired data is often impossible: there is no photograph of the same scene that Monet painted. CycleGAN learns \(G:X\to Y\) and \(F:Y\to X\) with two discriminators, plus a cycle-consistency loss:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{cyc}}=\E_x\big\|F(G(x))-x\big\|_1+\E_y\big\|G(F(y))-y\big\|_1 \]

Without it, \(G\) could map every input to one convincing output in \(Y\) and satisfy the discriminator completely while preserving nothing. Requiring the round trip to return the original forces \(G\) to retain enough information to invert. The known failure: the constraint can be satisfied by steganography — the network hides the input in imperceptible high-frequency noise so \(F\) can recover it, without any real semantic correspondence. Cycle consistency is a necessary but not sufficient condition, and it was demonstrated to be gameable.

StyleGAN

The architectural contribution that made GAN faces famous. Instead of feeding \(\mathbf z\) into the first layer, a mapping network transforms \(\mathbf z\) into an intermediate space \(\mathbf w\), which then modulates every layer via AdaIN. Two consequences:

  • \(\mathcal W\) is less entangled than \(\mathcal Z\). The Gaussian prior forces \(\mathcal Z\) to be curved wherever the data distribution is uneven; a learned mapping is free to unwarp it, which is why editing directions in \(\mathcal W\) (age, pose, expression) are so much cleaner.
  • Style mixing: use one \(\mathbf w\) for coarse layers and another for fine layers, and you get one face's pose with another's colouring — coarse-to-fine control that falls straight out of the architecture.

StyleGAN2 removed the droplet artifacts caused by AdaIN by replacing it with weight demodulation; StyleGAN3 fixed "texture sticking" — details clinging to pixel coordinates rather than to the object — by treating the network as a properly band-limited continuous signal and filtering correctly at every resampling step. That is a signal-processing fix to a deep learning problem, and it is one of the more satisfying papers in the area.

Where GANs stand now

Diffusion took over text-to-image almost completely, for reasons Lesson 107 makes clear: stable training, better coverage, and an easy conditioning story. But GANs are not dead. They remain the fastest high-quality generators — one forward pass, versus 20–50 for a distilled diffusion model — which matters for real-time video, super-resolution and on-device generation. And the adversarial loss survives everywhere: it is a component in neural audio codecs, in the decoders of latent diffusion models, and in diffusion distillation. The architecture lost; the idea of a learned loss did not.

Flows: exact likelihood by construction

Take a simple base distribution and push it through an invertible map \(f\). The change-of-variables formula gives the density exactly:

\[ \log p_X(\mathbf x)=\log p_Z\big(f^{-1}(\mathbf x)\big)+\log\left|\det\frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial\mathbf x}\right| \]

No bound, no approximation — unlike the VAE's ELBO. The price is severe: \(f\) must be invertible (so input and output dimensions match, no bottleneck), and you must be able to compute a \(d\times d\) determinant, which is \(O(d^3)\) in general. Flows are entirely about architectures that make that determinant cheap.

Coupling layers — a triangular Jacobian

Split \(\mathbf x\) into halves. Leave the first half untouched; transform the second using a function of the first:

\[ \mathbf y_{1:d}=\mathbf x_{1:d},\qquad \mathbf y_{d+1:D}=\mathbf x_{d+1:D}\odot\exp\big(s(\mathbf x_{1:d})\big)+t(\mathbf x_{1:d}) \]

The Jacobian is lower-triangular, so its determinant is just the product of the diagonal — \(\exp\big(\sum s(\mathbf x_{1:d})\big)\), computable in \(O(d)\). And the inverse is trivial: subtract \(t\), divide by \(\exp(s)\). Note that \(s\) and \(t\) can be arbitrarily complex neural networks and never need to be inverted themselves, because they only ever see the untouched half. That is the trick, and it is genuinely clever. Alternate which half is transformed between layers so everything eventually gets updated; Glow adds a learned invertible 1×1 convolution to permute channels rather than using a fixed split.

Autoregressive models

Factorise the joint exactly by the chain rule of probability:

\[ p(\mathbf x)=\prod_{i=1}^{D}p\big(x_i\mid x_{<i}\big) \]

This is the same objective as an LLM (Lesson 88), applied to pixels or audio samples. PixelRNN/PixelCNN generate an image pixel by pixel in raster order with a masked convolution enforcing causality; MADE achieves the same in one dense network by masking the weight matrices. WaveNet models raw audio at 16kHz with the dilated causal convolutions from Lesson 77.

Pitfall · the blind spot in masked convolutions

The naive masked convolution in PixelCNN has a blind spot: a triangular region above and to the right of the current pixel that is never visible, no matter how many layers you stack. It comes from the geometry of stacking masked kernels, and it measurably hurt sample quality. Gated PixelCNN fixed it by splitting into two stacks — a vertical stack seeing all rows above, and a horizontal stack seeing the current row to the left — that together cover the full context. A good reminder to actually plot your receptive field rather than assume it.

Generating a 256×256 RGB image autoregressively means 196,608 sequential forward passes. That is the whole story of why these models, despite having exact likelihoods and excellent theory, lost to diffusion for images — and why the same objective utterly dominates for text, where the sequence is a thousand tokens rather than a hundred thousand pixels.

Flows did not disappear

Exact invertibility is still valuable where you need a density rather than samples — variational inference, simulation-based inference in physics and cosmology, and probabilistic forecasting. And the continuous-time view of flows connects directly to flow matching and rectified flow (Lesson 108), which are now among the leading formulations for image and video generation. The family was folded into diffusion rather than defeated by it.

Destroy, then learn to undo

The forward process adds a little Gaussian noise \(T\) times, gradually turning data into pure noise. It has no parameters — it is fixed:

\[ q(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\!\Big(\mathbf x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\Big) \]
The closed form — why you never simulate the chain

Composing \(t\) Gaussian steps gives another Gaussian. Let \(\alpha_t=1-\beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s\). Then:

\[ q(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf x_0)=\mathcal N\!\Big(\mathbf x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)I\Big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbf x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon \]

This is what makes training practical: to get a training example at timestep 500 you do not run 500 noising steps. You sample \(t\) uniformly, sample \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), and compute \(\mathbf x_t\) in one line. \(\bar\alpha_t\) is the signal-to-noise schedule: it starts near 1 (almost pure data) and ends near 0 (almost pure noise).

From the ELBO to three lines of code

The full variational bound on \(\log p_\theta(\mathbf x_0)\) decomposes into a sum of KL divergences between \(q(\mathbf x_{t-1}\mid\mathbf x_t,\mathbf x_0)\) and \(p_\theta(\mathbf x_{t-1}\mid\mathbf x_t)\). Both are Gaussian, so each KL is a closed-form squared difference of means. After substituting the closed form above and dropping the time-dependent weights, everything collapses to:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{simple}}=\E_{t,\mathbf x_0,\boldsymbol\varepsilon}\Big[\big\|\boldsymbol\varepsilon-\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,t)\big\|^2\Big] \]

Predict the noise that was added. That is it. A page of variational algebra reduces to mean squared error on a noise vector, and the weighting that gets dropped is what makes it work better in practice than the exact bound would. Note also that \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta\) is, up to scaling, an estimate of the score \(\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x)\) — which is how DDPM and score-based SDE models turned out to be the same thing described twice.

betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)            # linear schedule
alphas = 1.0 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, dim=0)

def training_step(model, x0):
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (x0.size(0),), device=x0.device)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    a = abar[t].view(-1, 1, 1, 1)
    xt = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps    # the closed form — no simulation
    return F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)          # predict the noise

@torch.no_grad()
def sample(model, shape, device):
    x = torch.randn(shape, device=device)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        tt = torch.full((shape[0],), t, device=device, dtype=torch.long)
        eps = model(x, tt)
        a, ab = alphas[t], abar[t]
        mean = (x - (1 - a) / (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps) / a.sqrt()
        x = mean + (betas[t].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x) if t > 0 else 0)
    return x
Forward and reverse diffusion · with an exactly-known score, so nothing is trained or faked
Pitfall · the linear schedule destroys information too early

With the original linear \(\beta\) schedule at 256×256 resolution, \(\bar\alpha_t\) reaches near zero well before \(t=T\) — the last several hundred steps operate on what is already pure noise and contribute nothing but wasted compute. The cosine schedule keeps \(\bar\alpha_t\) meaningful for much longer, and it measurably improves sample quality. Drag the timestep slider in the demo and compare where each schedule's signal-to-noise ratio crosses 1. Related: the model must be conditioned on \(t\), normally through a sinusoidal embedding (Lesson 83) added into every residual block — omit it and the network cannot know how much noise to remove, so it learns the average and produces mush.

Classifier-free guidance

Classifier guidance needed a separate noise-aware classifier. CFG removes it entirely. Train one model that sees the condition \(c\) but randomly drops it (replacing it with a null token) about 10% of the time, so it learns both \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,c)\) and \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,\varnothing)\). At sampling time, extrapolate:

\[ \tilde{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,\varnothing)+w\big(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,c)-\boldsymbol\varepsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,\varnothing)\big) \]

At \(w=1\) this is ordinary conditional sampling. At \(w>1\) you push past the conditional prediction, away from the unconditional one — amplifying whatever makes this sample specific to the prompt. The cost is two forward passes per step, and a real trade-off: high \(w\) gives strong prompt adherence but reduced diversity and over-saturated, contrast-heavy images. Typical values are 3–8, and it is the single most consequential knob a user of these systems touches.

Guidance scale · prompt adherence bought with diversity

Faster sampling

DDIM reformulates the reverse process as a deterministic, non-Markovian ODE. Because there is no injected noise, you can take much larger steps: 1000-step DDPM quality is roughly matched in 50 DDIM steps. It also makes generation reproducible from a seed and makes the latent space interpolable, since the mapping from noise to image is now a function.

Flow matching and rectified flow reframe the whole thing: instead of a noising chain, learn a velocity field that transports noise to data along paths that are as straight as possible. Straight paths need fewer integration steps, and the training objective is simpler than the diffusion ELBO. SD3 and Flux use this formulation. Consistency models and adversarial distillation go further, training a student to jump directly from any point on the trajectory to the endpoint — 1 to 4 step generation, at some quality cost.

Why ControlNet's zero-initialised convolutions matter

ControlNet clones the U-Net encoder and links it back into the frozen original through convolutions initialised to exactly zero. At step zero the branch contributes nothing, so the model is bit-identical to the base — no random noise is injected into a carefully trained network, and there is no destructive early phase to recover from. The branch's influence grows only as the gradients justify it. This is the same reasoning as LoRA's \(B=\mathbf 0\) initialisation (Lesson 96) and as ResNet's zero-init residual \(\gamma\) (Lesson 52). Three separate papers, one principle: start new machinery at the identity.

Pitfall · text encoders and negative prompts

Two practical gotchas. First, the CLIP text encoder used by SD 1.x truncates at 77 tokens, silently — a long prompt simply loses its ending, which is why prompt weight seems to concentrate at the front. Second, "negative prompts" are not a separate mechanism: they replace the null/unconditional branch of CFG with an encoding of the unwanted text, so guidance pushes away from it. That means negative prompts only do anything at \(w>1\), and their strength is tied to the guidance scale — which explains why they seem to stop working when you turn guidance down.

CLIP

Two encoders — one for images, one for text — trained so that matching pairs are close and mismatched pairs are far. Given a batch of \(N\) pairs, compute all \(N^2\) similarities and treat the diagonal as the correct class in both directions:

\[ \mathcal L=\tfrac12\Big[\text{CE}\big(\text{sim}/\tau,\ \text{diag}\big)_{\text{rows}}+\text{CE}\big(\text{sim}/\tau,\ \text{diag}\big)_{\text{cols}}\Big] \]

This is InfoNCE (Lesson 63) with the other modality supplying the negatives. Because each example's negatives are the other \(N-1\) items in the batch, batch size is a hyperparameter of the loss — CLIP used 32,768.

Zero-shot classification, and why it is not magic

Embed the text "a photo of a {class}" for every candidate class, embed the image, take the nearest. No training on the target dataset at all. CLIP matched a fully supervised ResNet-50 on ImageNet this way, which was a genuine shock in 2021.

The limits are equally real. Performance depends heavily on prompt engineering — the phrase "a photo of a" is worth several points, and ensembling 80 prompt templates was worth several more. It is weak on fine-grained distinctions (bird species, aircraft models), on counting, and on anything requiring spatial relations, because a contrastive objective over noisy web captions never had to represent "three" or "to the left of". And its performance mirrors what is common in web text, so it is far better at Western, English-language concepts. Zero-shot is a strong baseline, not a substitute for a labelled dataset.

SigLIP replaces the softmax cross-entropy with a per-pair sigmoid loss. That removes the need for a global normalisation over the batch, so it works well at small batch sizes and shards trivially across devices — a small change that made contrastive training far more accessible.

The LLaVA result is the instructive one: a single linear layer between a frozen vision encoder and a frozen LLM, trained on modest instruction data, produces a capable vision-language model. It suggests the two representation spaces were already close enough that a linear map suffices — which says something quite deep about what large-scale pretraining converges to.

Pitfall · resolution, and where VLMs actually fail

Most VLMs downsample to 224 or 336 pixels. Any text in the image smaller than roughly 20 pixels is gone before the LLM sees anything, which is why models confidently misread charts, forms and screenshots — they are not hallucinating from ignorance, they are reading a blur. Tiling approaches (process crops at full resolution plus a global thumbnail) fix a lot of this at the cost of many more tokens. When evaluating a VLM on documents, always check what resolution it actually receives; it explains most of the failures you will see.

Audio is an image, if you let it be

Raw audio at 16kHz is 16,000 samples per second — a 10-second clip is 160,000 values with structure at every timescale. Almost every model instead works on a mel spectrogram: take the short-time Fourier transform to get frequency content over time, then warp the frequency axis to the mel scale, which spaces bins according to human pitch perception (roughly logarithmic above 500Hz). The result is a 2D array you can hand to a CNN or a transformer.

The window/hop trade is the uncertainty principle, and it is unavoidable: a short window localises events in time but smears them in frequency, a long window does the reverse.

Whisper is the third kind, scaled: an ordinary encoder-decoder transformer on log-mel spectrograms, trained on 680,000 hours of weakly-supervised multilingual audio scraped from the web. It performs multilingual transcription, translation, language ID and timestamping in one model, with special tokens selecting the task — the T5 text-to-text idea (Lesson 89) applied to audio. Its robustness came almost entirely from data scale and diversity rather than from architecture, which was the paper's actual point.

Pitfall · Whisper hallucinates on silence

Because the decoder is a language model trained on web audio, feeding it silence or non-speech produces plausible text with no acoustic basis — commonly "Thank you for watching" or subtitle credits, which were abundant in the training data. It is a well-documented failure with real consequences in medical and legal transcription. Mitigate with voice-activity detection before transcribing, check the no_speech_prob output, and be suspicious of repeated identical segments.

Self-supervised audio, and why it mattered more here

wav2vec 2.0 masks spans of a latent audio representation and solves a contrastive task over quantised targets; HuBERT instead predicts cluster assignments from an offline k-means over features, iteratively refined. Both learn phonetic structure with no transcripts. The impact was larger in speech than in vision for an economic reason: transcribing audio is slow and expensive, roughly 5–10× real time for careful work, so labelled speech is scarce while raw audio is essentially free. wav2vec 2.0 matched fully-supervised systems using 10 minutes of labelled data. When labels are expensive and raw data is abundant, self-supervision is transformative — that is the general rule, and speech is its cleanest case.

Synthesis

Modern TTS is two stages: an acoustic model producing a mel spectrogram from text (Tacotron 2, FastSpeech 2, VITS), then a vocoder turning the spectrogram back into a waveform. The spectrogram discarded phase information, so the vocoder must invent it plausibly — HiFi-GAN does this adversarially and in real time, and is why neural TTS became deployable. FastSpeech dropped autoregression in favour of a duration predictor, which removed both the latency and the attention-failure modes (skipped and repeated words) that plagued Tacotron.

Neural audio codecs — EnCodec, SoundStream — apply residual vector quantisation to compress audio into discrete tokens at a few kbps. Once audio is tokens, a transformer can model it exactly as it models text, which is how MusicGen, AudioLM and the current generation of speech language models work. It is the VQ-VAE idea from Lesson 103, and the same move that unified images with language models. Phase 8 begins on Day 95.
