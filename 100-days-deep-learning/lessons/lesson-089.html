Everything is text-to-text

T5's framing: classification, translation, summarisation, regression and question answering are all "given this string, produce that string". Prefix the input with a task description and use one model, one objective and one decoding procedure for all of them.

"translate English to German: That is good."     -> "Das ist gut."
"cola sentence: The course is jumping well."     -> "not acceptable"
"stsb sentence1: ... sentence2: ..."              -> "3.8"        # regression as a string!
"summarize: state authorities dispatched ..."     -> "six people hospitalized after ..."

Emitting "3.8" as text for a regression task is deliberately inelegant and it works. The point was to remove per-task architecture entirely so that pretraining objectives could be compared fairly — which is what T5's paper actually is: a large controlled ablation over objectives, corpora, architectures and scales.

Span corruption

BERT masks individual tokens. T5 masks contiguous spans and replaces each with a single sentinel, then asks the decoder to emit the spans in order:

input: Thank you <X> me to your party <Y> week.
target: <X> for inviting <Y> last <Z>

Two advantages over token masking. The target sequence is much shorter than the input, so training is cheaper per example. And predicting a multi-token span requires modelling the joint distribution over the span, not \(k\) independent marginals — a harder and more useful task. T5 uses 15% corruption with mean span length 3.

BART takes a different route: corrupt the input with an arbitrary noising function — token masking, deletion, span infilling, sentence permutation, document rotation — and train a full seq2seq model to reconstruct the original. Because the decoder is a standard autoregressive LM, BART is directly usable for generation in a way BERT is not. Their ablation found text infilling plus sentence shuffling worked best.

Why decoder-only won anyway

Encoder-decoder is arguably the better architecture for conditional generation, and it still holds up on translation and summarisation at matched size. It lost on operational grounds: one stack is simpler to scale, shard and serve than two; there is no split of parameters to tune; a decoder gets learning signal from every token; and in-context learning made per-task fine-tuning less necessary, which was encoder-decoder's main selling point. This is a recurring pattern worth internalising — the architecture that wins is usually the one that is easiest to scale, not the one that is best at a fixed size.

FLAN is the bridge to Phase 6's second half: take T5 or PaLM and fine-tune it on hundreds of tasks phrased as natural-language instructions. The result generalises to unseen instructions, which is the discovery that instruction tuning works at all — and that is Lesson 95.
