Depthwise separable convolution — the idea behind every mobile model

A standard conv mixes space and channels in one operation. Factor it into two:

\[ \frac{\text{depthwise separable}}{\text{standard}}=\frac{C_{\text{in}}k^2+C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}}{C_{\text{in}}C_{\text{out}}k^2}=\frac{1}{C_{\text{out}}}+\frac{1}{k^2} \]

The families, and what each contributed

ArchitectureThe one ideaWhy it mattered
DenseNet (2017)Every layer receives the concatenation of all previous feature mapsExtreme feature reuse; very parameter-efficient. Memory-hungry in practice, which limited adoption.
ResNeXt (2017)Replace the bottleneck's 3×3 with 32 parallel grouped paths ("cardinality")Showed that width and depth are not the only axes. Grouped convolution went mainstream here.
SENet (2018)Squeeze each channel to one number by global pooling, learn per-channel gates, rescaleChannel attention for ~1% extra FLOPs and a solid accuracy gain. Now a standard bolt-on.
MobileNetV2 (2018)Inverted residuals: expand channels, depthwise, project back down, skip on the narrow endsThe opposite of a bottleneck, and the right shape when the middle op is cheap.
MobileNetV3 (2019)NAS-searched, plus h-swish and SE blocksThe practical mobile default for years.
ShuffleNet (2018)Channel shuffle so grouped convolutions can exchange informationFixes grouped conv's isolation problem at zero FLOP cost.
EfficientNet (2019)Scale depth, width and resolution together by fixed ratiosSee below — the most-copied scaling result in vision.
RegNet (2020)Search over design spaces, not individual architecturesProduced simple, linear width rules that beat searched nets. A methodological argument.
ConvNeXt (2022)Take ResNet-50 and apply every ViT training trick and design choice, one at a timeMatched Swin Transformer. Strong evidence that much of ViT's advantage was the recipe, not attention.
EfficientNet's compound scaling

Given \(\phi\times\) more compute, how should you spend it? EfficientNet grid-searched the answer once on a small model and found that depth, width and resolution should scale together:

\[ \text{depth}=\alpha^\phi,\quad \text{width}=\beta^\phi,\quad \text{resolution}=\gamma^\phi \qquad\text{with } \alpha\beta^2\gamma^2\approx2 \]

The constraint holds because FLOPs scale linearly in depth but quadratically in width and in resolution. Their values were \(\alpha=1.2,\beta=1.1,\gamma=1.15\). The practical takeaway survives even if you never use EfficientNet: scaling one axis alone saturates fast. A network twice as deep at the same width and resolution wastes most of the extra compute.

Pitfall · FLOPs are not latency

Depthwise convolutions cut FLOPs by 9× and frequently deliver nothing like a 9× speedup — often under 2×. The reason is arithmetic intensity: a depthwise conv does very little maths per byte of memory it touches, so it is memory-bandwidth-bound and leaves the GPU's compute units idle. Dense 3×3 convolutions map beautifully onto tensor cores; depthwise ones do not. Always measure wall-clock latency on your actual target hardware. A model that is theoretically 5× cheaper and practically the same speed is a very common and very expensive surprise.

What to actually use in 2026

Default backbone: ResNet-50 — universally supported and a fair baseline. Best accuracy per FLOP on a GPU: ConvNeXt or EfficientNetV2. Mobile or edge: MobileNetV3 or MobileViT, chosen by measured latency. Small dataset: whatever has the best pretrained weights, which usually beats whatever has the best architecture. Reach for timm (Lesson 55) and compare three candidates on your data rather than reasoning about it from a table.
