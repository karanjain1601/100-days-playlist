A constant learning rate never converges

This is the fact that makes schedules mandatory rather than optional. SGD does not descend on the true loss — it descends on a noisy estimate from one mini-batch. Near a minimum the true gradient goes to zero but the noise does not, so each step is pure jitter of size roughly \(\eta\) times the gradient noise.

The noise ball

Model the mini-batch gradient as the true gradient plus zero-mean noise, \(\hat{\mathbf g}=\mathbf g+\boldsymbol\xi\) with \(\E[\boldsymbol\xi\boldsymbol\xi\T]=\Sigma\). Near a quadratic minimum with curvature \(\lambda\), the stationary distribution of SGD has

\[ \E\!\left[\|\boldsymbol\theta-\boldsymbol\theta^\ast\|^2\right] \;\approx\; \frac{\eta\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{2\lambda-\eta\lambda^2} \;\xrightarrow[\ \eta\ \text{small}\ ]{}\; \frac{\eta\,\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{2\lambda} \]

The parameters orbit the minimum inside a ball whose radius is proportional to \(\eta\). Halving the learning rate halves the radius. The only way to actually land at the bottom is to shrink \(\eta\) towards zero — which is exactly what a schedule does. Every drop you see in a loss curve immediately after an LR decay is this ball contracting.

The schedules worth knowing

ScheduleFormula (step \(t\) of \(T\))Where it earns its place
Step decay\(\eta_t=\eta_0\gamma^{\lfloor t/s\rfloor}\)The ImageNet classic: ×0.1 at epochs 30/60/90. Simple, still competitive, but the cliff edges are arbitrary.
Exponential\(\eta_t=\eta_0\gamma^{t}\)Smooth version of the above. Sensitive to \(\gamma\) — over a long run it either decays to nothing by epoch 10 or barely moves.
Polynomial\(\eta_t=\eta_0(1-t/T)^p\)\(p=1\) is linear-to-zero, the default in most LLM pretraining runs and in segmentation (\(p=0.9\)).
Cosine\(\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\tfrac12(\eta_0-\eta_{\min})\left(1+\cos\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\)The modern default. Slow at the start, fast in the middle, very slow at the end — spends its budget where it matters. Needs \(T\) known in advance.
One-cycleup to \(\eta_{\max}\), then down to \(\eta_0/10^4\)Superconvergence. The rising phase acts as regularisation; the long tail anneals. Excellent for short budgets.
Plateau×\(\gamma\) when val stops improvingThe only reactive option. Convenient when you have no idea how long training will take; usually beaten by a well-set cosine.

Warmup — and why it is not optional for Adam

Warmup ramps the learning rate linearly from (near) zero to \(\eta_0\) over the first few hundred to few thousand steps:

\[ \eta_t=\eta_0\cdot\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{t}{T_{\text{warm}}}\right) \]

There are two independent reasons, and people usually only know the second one.

Reason 1 — Adam's second moment is garbage at step 1

Recall from Lesson 32 that Adam divides by \(\sqrt{\hat v_t}\). At small \(t\), \(\hat v_t\) is an average of one or two squared gradients, so its variance is enormous. Some coordinates get a denominator that happens to be tiny and take a wildly oversized step. This is not a scaling problem you can fix with a smaller \(\eta\) — it is an estimator-variance problem, and it goes away only once \(\hat v_t\) has averaged over \(\sim 1/(1-\beta_2)=1000\) steps. Warmup is a cheap way to survive that window. (RAdam attacks the same problem analytically.)

Reason 2 — large batches want a large \(\eta\) the model cannot yet take

The linear scaling rule from Lesson 29 says batch 8192 wants an LR 32× larger than batch 256. That LR is correct once the weights are in a sane region, but at initialisation the loss surface is steep and badly conditioned, and a 32× step diverges immediately. Warmup buys the network a few hundred steps to reach a region where the big step is safe.

Rules of thumb: 2–5% of total steps for vision, ~2000 steps for transformer pretraining regardless of run length, and 0 for small-batch SGD on a small model, where warmup does nothing but waste steps.

Stepping the scheduler correctly

import math, torch
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import LambdaLR

def warmup_cosine(optimizer, warmup_steps, total_steps, min_ratio=0.0):
    def f(step):
        if step < warmup_steps:                       # linear ramp
            return step / max(1, warmup_steps)
        p = (step - warmup_steps) / max(1, total_steps - warmup_steps)
        p = min(1.0, p)                                # clamp: never go past the floor
        return min_ratio + (1 - min_ratio) * 0.5 * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * p))
    return LambdaLR(optimizer, f)

steps_per_epoch = len(train_loader)
total = epochs * steps_per_epoch
sched = warmup_cosine(opt, warmup_steps=int(0.03 * total), total_steps=total)

for epoch in range(epochs):
    for xb, yb in train_loader:
        opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
        loss = criterion(model(xb), yb)
        loss.backward()
        opt.step()
        sched.step()                                   # PER BATCH, because total is in batches
Pitfall · three ways to break a scheduler

1. Wrong granularity. You compute total in batches but call sched.step() once per epoch. Your cosine then completes 0.3% of its curve and the LR never decays. The loss looks "stuck" and you blame the model. Decide once whether your schedule is per-step or per-epoch and make the denominator match.

2. Wrong order. sched.step() must come after opt.step(). PyTorch warns about this but the warning fires once and scrolls away.

3. Resuming without the scheduler. Checkpoints must save sched.state_dict() too. Restore the model and optimizer but not the scheduler and you restart training at the peak LR, which undoes hours of annealing in about ten steps.

Reading the two curves together

Always log LR on the same x-axis as loss. A loss drop that lines up exactly with an LR decay is the noise ball contracting — normal and healthy. A loss drop that happens between decays is real learning. If every improvement in your run is glued to a decay step, your LR was too high for the whole run and the model was never learning, only orbiting.

Exercise

Train the same CIFAR model three times for exactly 20 epochs: constant \(\eta\), step decay, and warmup+cosine. Plot all three loss curves on one axis. Then run the cosine version for 40 epochs and confirm that the 20-epoch cosine beats the 40-epoch cosine truncated at epoch 20 — this is the reason cosine needs \(T\) known in advance.
