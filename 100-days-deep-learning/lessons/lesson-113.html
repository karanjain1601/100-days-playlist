Optimise the policy directly

Q-learning learns values and derives a policy by taking the argmax — which needs a discrete, small action space. Policy gradient methods parameterise \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) and differentiate the expected return directly:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\E_{\pi_\theta}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\Psi\Big] \]

with \(\Psi\) some measure of how good the action was. This works in continuous action spaces, handles stochastic policies naturally, and is the branch that leads to RLHF.

Why a baseline reduces variance without introducing bias

REINFORCE sets \(\Psi=G_t\), the total return, and its variance is enormous. Subtract any function of the state, \(\Psi=G_t-b(s_t)\). The bias introduced is:

\[ \E\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,b(s)\big]=b(s)\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}=b(s)\,\nabla_\theta\!\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=b(s)\,\nabla_\theta 1=0 \]

Exactly zero, because probabilities sum to one and the gradient of a constant vanishes. So you may subtract any state-dependent baseline for free. The best choice is \(b(s)=V(s)\), giving the advantage \(A(s,a)=Q(s,a)-V(s)\): "how much better than average was this action". Learning \(V\) alongside \(\pi\) is exactly what makes it an actor-critic method, and GAE interpolates between one-step and Monte-Carlo advantage estimates with a parameter \(\lambda\) that trades bias against variance.

PPO — the algorithm behind RLHF

A large policy update can be catastrophic: the policy collapses and, because it generates its own data, it may never recover. TRPO enforced a hard KL trust region, which required second-order optimisation. PPO gets most of the benefit with a first-order clip. Let \(r_t(\theta)=\dfrac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\). Then:

\[ L^{\text{CLIP}}=\E_t\Big[\min\big(r_t\hat A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big] \]

Read the \(\min\) carefully, because it is asymmetric on purpose. When \(\hat A_t>0\) (a good action) the objective is capped at \(1+\epsilon\) — no reward for pushing the probability arbitrarily high. When \(\hat A_t<0\) it is capped at \(1-\epsilon\). Either way the incentive to move far from the old policy disappears past the clip, so the gradient goes to zero there. Typically \(\epsilon=0.2\). Continuous control, and the rest of the map

AlgorithmTypeWhere it fits
PPOOn-policyThe default. Robust, simple, works nearly everywhere. Sample-inefficient.
DDPG / TD3Off-policy, deterministicContinuous control. TD3 adds twin critics and delayed updates to fix DDPG's overestimation.
SACOff-policy, maximum-entropyAdds an entropy bonus to the reward, so the policy stays stochastic and explores. Usually the strongest choice for robotics.
Model-based / world models—Learn the dynamics, then plan or train inside the learned model. Dramatically more sample-efficient; risks exploiting model errors.
Offline RLBatchLearn purely from a fixed dataset with no environment access. The hard part is that the policy must not rely on actions the data never contains.
The line from here to Phase 6

RLHF is PPO where the state is the prompt-plus-generated-prefix, the action is the next token, the policy is the language model, and the reward comes from a learned preference model at the end of the sequence. GRPO (Lesson 98) is the same setup with the value network deleted — sample \(G\) responses and use the group mean as the baseline, which the derivation above says is legitimate because any state-dependent baseline is unbiased. The most consequential application of reinforcement learning today is not games or robotics. It is making language models useful.
