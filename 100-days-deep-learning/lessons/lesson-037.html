They differ in exactly one thing: which axes you average over

Every normalization layer computes a mean and variance and rescales. The only thing separating BatchNorm, LayerNorm, InstanceNorm and GroupNorm is the set of axes the statistics are taken over. Fix that picture and the whole family collapses into one idea.

LayerNorm, and why sequences demand it

\[ \text{LN}(\mathbf x)=\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\epsilon}}+\beta,\qquad \mu=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d x_j,\quad \sigma^2=\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d (x_j-\mu)^2 \]

Statistics are computed over the feature dimension of a single token. Nothing crosses the batch, so: batch size 1 works, train and eval are the same function, there are no running statistics, gradient accumulation is exact, and no cross-GPU synchronisation is needed. For variable-length padded sequences this is not a convenience but a requirement — batch statistics over a tensor that is 40% padding are meaningless.

RMSNorm — drop the mean

\[ \text{RMSNorm}(\mathbf x)=\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x}{\sqrt{\frac1d\sum_j x_j^2+\epsilon}} \]

No mean subtraction, no \(\beta\). The claim, borne out empirically, is that the re-centring in LayerNorm contributes almost nothing and the re-scaling does all the work. You save one pass over the vector and a parameter tensor, which is worth roughly 7–10% of layer latency — meaningful when you run it twice per block across 80 blocks. Llama, T5, Gemma, Mistral and most current open-weight models use RMSNorm.

class RMSNorm(torch.nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, eps=1e-6):
        super().__init__()
        self.eps = eps
        self.weight = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.ones(d))

    def forward(self, x):
        # compute in fp32 even under autocast: the sum of squares overflows fp16 easily
        dtype = x.dtype
        x = x.float()
        rms = torch.rsqrt(x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True) + self.eps)
        return (x * rms).to(dtype) * self.weight

Pre-norm vs post-norm

The 2017 transformer put the norm after the residual add. Every modern model puts it before the sublayer:

\[ \text{post:}\ \ \mathbf x_{l+1}=\text{LN}\big(\mathbf x_l+F(\mathbf x_l)\big) \qquad\qquad \text{pre:}\ \ \mathbf x_{l+1}=\mathbf x_l+F\big(\text{LN}(\mathbf x_l)\big) \]

In the pre-norm form there is a clean identity path from the loss to every layer — nothing sits between \(\mathbf x_l\) and \(\mathbf x_{l+1}\) but an addition, exactly the condition from Lesson 28 that makes gradients flow. Post-norm puts a LayerNorm on that path, and its Jacobian shrinks the gradient at every one of the \(L\) layers.

Post-norm (2017)Pre-norm (modern)
WarmupRequired — diverges without itOptional, still usually used
Depth beyond ~12 layersHard to trainTrains to 100+ layers
Final quality at equal depthSlightly betterSlightly worse
Residual stream growthBounded by the normGrows with depth — needs a final norm before the head
Pitfall · the missing final norm

In a pre-norm stack, nothing normalises the residual stream on its way out of the last block, so its magnitude grows roughly like \(\sqrt L\). Forget the final LayerNorm before the output projection and the logits come out scaled wrong — training still works, but slowly and with a strange loss curve. Every reference implementation has this layer; it is easy to omit when writing your own, and you will meet it again in Lesson 85.

Choosing, in one line each

Conv net, batch ≥ 32 → BatchNorm. Conv net, tiny batch (detection, segmentation, 3D medical) → GroupNorm, \(G=32\). Anything sequential (transformer, RNN) → LayerNorm, or RMSNorm if you are writing it yourself. Style transfer / image-to-image → InstanceNorm, because you specifically want to erase per-image contrast statistics. Tiny batch on a transformer → nothing changes; LayerNorm never cared about your batch size.
