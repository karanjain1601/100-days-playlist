Month 11 · Days 301–330 · Lessons 301–330

Interpretability, Theory, Robustness, and Privacy

Thirty papers that ask whether the models of the previous ten months can be trusted: whether we can see what they compute, whether they generalize for the reasons we think, whether they can be attacked, and what they leak about their training data. The month moves from attribution methods and the sanity checks that undercut them, through mechanistic interpretability (circuits, superposition, sparse autoencoders, causal tracing), to the theory of overparameterized networks, adversarial robustness, calibration, poisoning and privacy. It builds on the transformer of Month 5 and the alignment questions of Month 7, and hands the final month a habit it needs for compression, systems and evaluation: ask what a measurement actually shows.

By the end of this month you can

  • Compute gradient saliency, Grad-CAM, LIME, SHAP and Integrated Gradients for one model; state the Shapley axioms and IG's completeness property and derive the latter; and run the model-randomization sanity check to show which maps ignore the model.
  • Rewrite an attention-only transformer as a residual stream with per-head QK and OV circuits, explain how K-composition produces an induction head, and compute the in-context-learning score.
  • Reproduce the toy model of superposition, train a sparse autoencoder on its activations, steer a model by clamping a feature, and explain feature splitting, L1 shrinkage and the main critiques of SAE evaluation.
  • Distinguish decodability from use and plausibility from faithfulness: build a probing control task and report selectivity, run causal tracing, and derive ROME's rank-one edit together with the ways edits fail to propagate.
  • Explain the lottery-ticket reinitialization control, the interpolation-threshold peak of double descent, grokking and its progress measures, what the constant neural tangent kernel does and does not describe, and why the flat-minima and information-bottleneck stories were disputed.
  • Derive FGSM from the linearity argument, write adversarial training as a min-max problem solved with PGD, state the certified radius of randomized smoothing, and separate empirical robustness from certified robustness; diagnose shortcut learning and fix miscalibration with temperature scaling.
  • Explain how backdoors and poisoning evade standard metrics, run a shadow-model membership-inference attack, implement DP-SGD's per-example clipping and noise and say what \(\varepsilon\) guarantees, and describe federated averaging and its local-computation knobs.

L301 · DAY 301

Deep Inside Convolutional Networks: Visualising Image Classification Models and Saliency Maps

30m videoLevel 2

Simonyan, Vedaldi & Zisserman, ICLR Workshop 2014 (arXiv:1312.6034)

One backward pass through a trained ConvNet, taken with respect to the input pixels instead of the weights, gives two tools: an image that maximizes a class score (what the class "looks like" to the network) and a per-pixel saliency map (which pixels the score is locally most sensitive to).

Cold open

The paper is a short workshop paper, and most of today's attribution work starts from it. Take a trained ImageNet classifier, pick the logit for "goose", and differentiate it with respect to the image. The gradient's magnitude, drawn as a heatmap, lights up the goose. No extra training, no labels, one backward pass. The month opens here because this is the simplest possible answer to "why did the network say that?", and the next four lessons are about how far that answer can be trusted.

The problem

By 2013 ConvNets had won ImageNet, and nobody could say what they computed. Two lines of prior work existed. Erhan et al. (2009) had visualized hidden units of deep belief nets by activation maximization: gradient ascent on the input to excite one unit. Zeiler & Fergus's deconvnet (Day 62) projected a chosen activation back to pixel space through "reversed" layers. Gradient-based explanations of individual classifier decisions also predate this paper (Baehrens et al., JMLR 2010). What was missing was a simple, model-agnostic recipe for ConvNets, for both the class as a whole and one particular image, plus a clear account of how it related to the deconvnet.

The method

Definition · class model visualization

Let \(S_c(I)\) be the unnormalized score (the logit) of class \(c\) for image \(I\). Find

$$I^\star = \argmax_I \; S_c(I) - \lambda \|I\|_2^2$$

by gradient ascent on \(I\) with the weights frozen, starting from the zero image; the training-set mean image is added back for display. The authors use the logit rather than the softmax posterior because the posterior can be raised by pushing the other class scores down, and they found the logit gave more interpretable pictures.

The second tool explains one image \(I_0\). A deep network's score is highly non-linear in \(I\), but near \(I_0\) it is approximately linear.

Derivation · first-order Taylor

Expand the score around \(I_0\):

$$S_c(I) \approx S_c(I_0) + w^\top (I - I_0) = w^\top I + b, \qquad w = \frac{\partial S_c}{\partial I}\bigg|_{I_0}.$$

For a truly linear model \(S_c = w^\top I + b\), the magnitude \(|w_k|\) says how much pixel \(k\) moves the score per unit change. The paper uses the local linear model the same way: rank pixels by \(|w_k|\). For an RGB image, take the largest magnitude over the three channels,

$$M_{ij} = \max_{ch} \big| w_{h(i,j,ch)} \big|,$$

where \(h(i,j,ch)\) indexes the element of \(w\) for pixel \((i,j)\) in channel \(ch\). Cost: one backward pass.

Saliency without segmentation labels

To show the maps carry real spatial information, the authors use them for weakly supervised localization. The network was trained only on image-level class labels, yet its saliency map can seed a segmentation.

Algorithm · saliency-seeded GraphCut
  1. Compute \(M\) for the top predicted class.
  2. Take the most salient pixels as foreground seeds and the least salient as background seeds (fixed quantile thresholds).
  3. Fit colour models (Gaussian mixtures) to the foreground and background seeds.
  4. Run GraphCut colour segmentation (Boykov & Jolly 2001) to propagate the labels to the rest of the image.
  5. Return the bounding box of the largest connected foreground component.

The authors entered this in the ILSVRC-2013 localization task. It was not competitive with methods trained on bounding boxes, and the paper does not claim it was; the point is that a classifier trained with no location labels already "knows" roughly where the object is.

Gradients and the deconvnet are nearly the same thing

Derivation · layer by layer

Write \(R_n\) for the signal being sent back to layer \(n\)'s input, \(X_n\) for that input on the forward pass.

  • Convolution. The deconvnet applies the convolution with flipped filters, which is exactly the transposed convolution the chain rule uses. Identical.
  • Max-pooling. The deconvnet routes the signal to the argmax position via stored "switches"; the gradient of max-pooling does the same. Identical.
  • ReLU. Gradient: \(R_n = R_{n+1}\cdot \mathbb 1[X_n > 0]\), gated by the forward activation. Deconvnet: \(R_n = R_{n+1}\cdot\mathbb 1[R_{n+1} > 0]\), gated by the sign of the backward signal. Different.

So the deconvnet is the gradient with a modified ReLU rule, and gradient saliency "generalizes" the deconvnet in the authors' sense: it applies to any layer, not only convolutional ones. Combine both gates and you get guided backpropagation (Springenberg et al. 2015), which returns in Day 302 and fails a sanity check in Day 305.

import torch
from torchvision.models import resnet50, ResNet50_Weights

weights = ResNet50_Weights.DEFAULT
model = resnet50(weights=weights).eval()
prep = weights.transforms()

def saliency(img, cls=None):
    x = prep(img).unsqueeze(0).requires_grad_(True)
    scores = model(x)                      # unnormalized class scores (logits)
    c = scores.argmax(1).item() if cls is None else cls
    scores[0, c].backward()                # one backward pass, w.r.t. the pixels
    return x.grad[0].abs().amax(0), c      # max |w| over channels: H x W map

Evidence

The evidence is visual. Class model images for ImageNet classes contain recognizable fragments (goose heads, dumbbell shapes, dalmatian spots) tiled across the frame. Saliency maps for individual images concentrate on the object. The localization experiment supplies one quantitative check that the maps are not noise. What the paper does not do is test whether high-saliency pixels are actually the ones the decision depends on: there is no deletion test, no comparison with occlusion, no ground-truth attribution. That gap is the subject of the rest of this block.

Limits & what broke
  • Local linearity is the whole story. The map is a statement about an infinitesimal perturbation. Saturated features get near-zero gradient however much they matter, and the gradient of a ReLU network is noisy and discontinuous across nearby inputs. SmoothGrad (Smilkov et al. 2017), Integrated Gradients and SHAP (Day 304, Day 305) are attempts to answer the question the gradient cannot.
  • The class images look adversarial. They are recognizable to a person but also full of high-frequency texture and repeated parts, the kind of image that drives a logit up without looking like a natural photo. That is an early hint of what Day 321 makes explicit: small, structured input changes can move a network's output a long way.
  • The prior makes the picture. The only regularizer here is an L2 penalty. Later work (Yosinski et al. 2015; Mahendran & Vedaldi 2016; Olah et al. 2017, "Feature Visualization") showed that blur, jitter, transformation robustness or a learned image prior change the result dramatically. These images are partly artifacts of the chosen prior, not only of the network.

Bridge

Gradient saliency is cheap, works on any differentiable model, and has fine resolution, but it is noisy and class-insensitive in ways the paper does not test. Tomorrow's Grad-CAM (Day 302) trades pixel resolution for class sensitivity by taking gradients at the last convolutional layer instead of the input. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 62 (ZFNet and the deconvnet).

L302 · DAY 302

Grad-CAM: Visual Explanations from Deep Networks via Gradient-based Localization

35m videoLevel 2

Selvaraju, Cogswell, Das, Vedantam, Parikh & Batra, ICCV 2017 (arXiv:1610.02391)

Weight each feature map of the last convolutional layer by the spatially averaged gradient of the class score, sum, and keep the positive part: the result is a coarse, class-discriminative heatmap that works for any CNN-based model without changing or retraining it.

Cold open

The red-and-blue blob over a cat's face that you have seen in a hundred papers is, most of the time, Grad-CAM. Its popularity comes from three properties together: it is class-specific (the "cat" map and the "dog" map of the same image differ), it needs no retraining, and it is about ten lines of code. It is also coarse, and the variant most often shown with it, Guided Grad-CAM, has a problem this lesson ends on.

The problem

Gradient saliency (Day 301) has pixel resolution but is noisy and only weakly class-discriminative. Class Activation Mapping (CAM; Zhou et al., CVPR 2016) produced clean class-specific maps, but only for one architecture: convolutional features \(A^k\), then global average pooling (GAP), then a single linear layer with weights \(w^c_k\). The map was \(\sum_k w^c_k A^k\). Any network with fully connected layers after the convolutions, such as VGG, or a captioning or VQA model, had to be modified and retrained to use it, usually at some cost in accuracy. GAP itself came from Network in Network (Day 63).

The method

Definition · Grad-CAM

Let \(A^k \in \R^{u\times v}\) be the \(k\)-th feature map of a chosen convolutional layer and \(y^c\) the pre-softmax score for class \(c\). Then

$$\alpha^c_k = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i}\sum_{j} \frac{\partial y^c}{\partial A^k_{ij}}, \qquad L^c_{\text{Grad-CAM}} = \operatorname{ReLU}\Big(\sum_k \alpha^c_k A^k\Big),$$

with \(Z = uv\). The map has the feature map's resolution and is upsampled bilinearly to the image.

Derivation · CAM is a special case

In a CAM architecture, \(y^c = \sum_k w^c_k \cdot \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j} A^k_{ij}\). Differentiate: \(\partial y^c/\partial A^k_{ij} = w^c_k/Z\) for every position. Averaging over the \(Z\) positions gives

$$\alpha^c_k = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_{i,j}\frac{w^c_k}{Z} = \frac{w^c_k}{Z} \propto w^c_k .$$

So Grad-CAM reproduces CAM up to a positive constant (and the ReLU) on CAM's own architecture. For any other head the gradient still defines a channel weight; it is a first-order, locally linear stand-in for the missing linear layer.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def grad_cam(model, layer, x, cls):
    store = {}
    def fwd(module, inp, out):
        store["A"] = out
        out.register_hook(lambda g: store.__setitem__("dA", g))
    h = layer.register_forward_hook(fwd)
    model.zero_grad()
    model(x)[0, cls].backward()            # gradient of the logit y^c
    h.remove()
    A, dA = store["A"][0].detach(), store["dA"][0]   # K x u x v
    alpha = dA.mean(dim=(1, 2))            # global-average-pooled gradients
    cam = F.relu((alpha[:, None, None] * A).sum(0))
    return F.interpolate(cam[None, None], size=x.shape[-2:], mode="bilinear")[0, 0]

Design choices that matter

The ReLU. The authors keep only features with a positive influence on \(y^c\): pixels whose intensity should increase to raise the class score. Without it, the map also lights up regions that belong to other classes, and the paper reports that localization gets worse. Dropping the ReLU changes the picture substantially, so a "Grad-CAM" without it is a different explanation, not a variant.

The last convolutional layer. Deeper layers carry more class-specific semantics; shallower layers carry finer spatial detail. The last conv layer is the compromise, and its resolution is the map's resolution: for VGG-16 at 224×224 input that is a 14×14 grid. The blur is therefore a structural limit of the method, not a bug to be fixed with better upsampling.

Guided Grad-CAM. To recover pixel detail, multiply the upsampled Grad-CAM map elementwise with a guided backpropagation map (the deconvnet-and-gradient ReLU rule from Day 301). The product is sharp and class-specific in appearance.

Counterfactual explanations. Negate the gradients before pooling and the map highlights regions whose removal would make the network more confident in \(c\): the evidence against the class.

Evidence

The paper mixes quantitative and human evaluations. On ILSVRC weakly supervised localization (threshold the map, take a box), the authors report Grad-CAM competitive with CAM without CAM's architecture change or its accuracy cost. In human studies on Amazon Mechanical Turk, they report that Guided Grad-CAM made it easier for people to tell which class was being explained, and helped untrained users pick out the more accurate of two models even when both made the same prediction. It also produces maps for captioning and VQA models, which CAM could not handle.

The bias case study is the most reusable result. A classifier trained to tell doctors from nurses on image-search data, which was gender-skewed, reached good validation accuracy, but Grad-CAM showed it attending to faces and hairstyles rather than to stethoscopes or coats. After the training set was rebalanced, the maps moved to task-relevant regions and the authors report the model generalized better. A heatmap here was used as a debugging tool, which is the use it is best suited for.

Limits & what broke
  • Guided backprop is partly an edge detector. Adebayo et al. (Day 305) found that guided backpropagation, and therefore Guided Grad-CAM, changes very little when the network's upper-layer weights are randomized. Nie et al. (ICML 2018) argued that guided backprop and the deconvnet largely perform image recovery. Most of Guided Grad-CAM's sharp detail comes from the image, not the model, yet such figures are still published uncritically. Plain Grad-CAM passes the same test much better.
  • Coarseness is permanent. Small objects and multiple instances of one class blur together on a 7×7 or 14×14 grid. Grad-CAM++ (Chattopadhyay et al., WACV 2018) changed the weighting to handle multiple instances.
  • Pooled gradients are still gradients. \(\alpha^c_k\) inherits saturation and noise from Day 301; gradient-free variants such as Score-CAM (Wang et al., 2020) weight channels by the score change from masking instead.
  • Localization is not faithfulness. A map that overlaps the object box can still misdescribe what the model uses; that is a separate test.

Bridge

Grad-CAM still needs gradients and convolutional feature maps. Tomorrow's LIME (Day 303) drops both and treats the model as a black box that can only be queried. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 63 (global average pooling), ⟵ Day 301 (gradient saliency and guided backprop).

L303 · DAY 303

“Why Should I Trust You?” Explaining the Predictions of Any Classifier (LIME)

35m videoLevel 2

Ribeiro, Singh & Guestrin, KDD 2016 (arXiv:1602.04938)

To explain one prediction of a model you can only query, perturb the input in a human-readable representation, record the model's outputs, and fit a sparse linear model weighted by proximity to the original input; the objective states openly that the explanation trades faithfulness for simplicity.

Cold open

A classifier separates huskies from wolves almost perfectly. Its explanation for one "wolf" prediction highlights the snow behind the animal, not the animal. The authors built that classifier on purpose, from a training set where every wolf photo had snow, and used it to test whether people could spot a model that had learned the wrong thing. It is the quickest demonstration in ML that accuracy and trustworthiness are different properties.

The problem

The previous two lessons need gradients (Day 301) or convolutional feature maps (Day 302). Many deployed models are random forests, ensembles, pipelines with preprocessing, or APIs: no gradient is available. Global surrogates (fit one interpretable model to the whole black box) exist, but a single simple model rarely matches a complex one everywhere. LIME's bet is that matching it near one input is feasible.

The method

Definition · the LIME objective

For a model \(f\), an instance \(x\), a class \(G\) of interpretable models (here sparse linear models), a proximity kernel \(\pi_x\) and a complexity penalty \(\Omega\):

$$\xi(x) = \argmin_{g \in G}\; \mathcal L(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g), \qquad \mathcal L = \sum_{z} \pi_x(z)\,\big(f(z) - g(z')\big)^2,$$

with \(\pi_x(z) = \exp(-D(x,z)^2/\sigma^2)\). \(\mathcal L\) measures local fidelity; \(\Omega\) measures interpretability (for sparse linear models, a hard limit of \(K\) non-zero weights). Both appear in one objective, so the trade between them is a stated design choice rather than a hidden one.

The interpretable representation. The explanation lives in a different space from the model's input. For text, \(x' \in \{0,1\}^{d'}\) marks which words are present; for images, which superpixels are kept. The model may see word embeddings or raw pixels, which no one can read, and perturbation needs an "off" state that makes sense: deleting a word, greying a superpixel. A coefficient then means "keeping this word raises the predicted probability by this much, near \(x\)".

Algorithm · sparse linear explanation
  1. Map \(x\) to \(x'\) (all ones: every word or superpixel present).
  2. Draw samples \(z'\) by switching off a random subset of the ones in \(x'\).
  3. Map each \(z'\) back to an input \(z\) and query the black box for \(f(z)\).
  4. Weight each sample by \(\pi_x(z)\) (cosine distance for text, L2 for images).
  5. Select \(K\) features (the paper uses the Lasso path, "K-Lasso"), then fit weighted least squares on them.
  6. Report the \(K\) coefficients.
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import Ridge

def lime_text(predict, words, n=2000, width=0.25, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    d = len(words)
    Z = np.ones((n, d), dtype=int)                   # row 0 is x itself
    for row in Z[1:]:
        row[rng.choice(d, rng.integers(1, d + 1), replace=False)] = 0
    texts = [" ".join(w for w, keep in zip(words, z) if keep) for z in Z]
    y = predict(texts)                               # black-box probability of one class
    dist = 1 - np.sqrt(Z.sum(1) / d)                 # cosine distance to the all-ones x'
    w = np.exp(-dist**2 / width**2)                  # proximity kernel
    g = Ridge(alpha=1.0).fit(Z, y, sample_weight=w)  # paper: K-Lasso, then least squares
    return sorted(zip(words, g.coef_), key=lambda t: -abs(t[1]))

SP-LIME: which explanations to show

One explanation is anecdote. To judge a whole model a user needs a few, chosen so they cover different features. Collect explanations for \(n\) instances in a matrix \(W\), give feature \(j\) a global importance \(I_j\) (for text, \(\sqrt{\sum_i |W_{ij}|}\)), and pick a set \(V\) of at most \(B\) instances maximizing the coverage

$$c(V) = \sum_j I_j \,\mathbb 1\big[\exists\, i \in V : W_{ij} > 0\big].$$

Coverage is a monotone submodular set function (each added instance helps less once its features are covered), so exact maximization is NP-hard but greedy selection has the standard \(1 - 1/e\) guarantee. The submodular-pick step is the paper's answer to "explanations of what, for whom": a budget of human attention, spent on non-redundant evidence.

Evidence

  • Simulated users. On sentiment data with classifiers that are interpretable by construction (sparse logistic regression, small decision trees), the true important features are known. The authors report LIME recovering them at high recall, above parzen-window and greedy baselines.
  • Choosing a model. In the 20 Newsgroups "Christianity vs Atheism" task, a classifier with good held-out accuracy relied on header words such as "Posting" and "Host". With explanations, non-expert users could tell which of two classifiers would generalize and could improve one by deleting such features.
  • Husky vs wolf. Shown predictions alone, many participants with ML background said they trusted the snow-biased model; after seeing explanations, the authors report that far fewer did and most named snow as the feature it used. The study was small, but the qualitative effect is the one people remember.

What the evidence does not show: that explanations of real, unbiased models are faithful in any measurable sense, or that a user who sees ten local explanations understands the global model.

Limits & what broke
  • Instability. The samples are random, so two runs can give different top features, and Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola (2018) showed LIME's explanations can change sharply between nearly identical inputs.
  • The kernel width is unprincipled. \(\sigma\) sets what "local" means, nothing in the method chooses it, and changing it can reverse which features look important. Garreau & von Luxburg (AISTATS 2020) analysed this for tabular data.
  • Local, but read globally. Users extend a local linear explanation to inputs where it no longer holds. The same authors' Anchors (AAAI 2018) responded with rules that state their own coverage.
  • Off-manifold perturbations. Inputs with words or superpixels removed are unlike training data. Slack et al. (AIES 2020) built classifiers that detect such inputs and behave innocently on them, so LIME and SHAP explanations hid a biased model.

Bridge

LIME's choices (kernel, loss, regularizer) are made by hand, and different choices give different answers. Tomorrow's SHAP (Day 304) shows that LIME's family contains exactly one member satisfying a short list of axioms, and gives the kernel that produces it. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 301, Day 302 (the white-box methods LIME replaces).

L304 · DAY 304

A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions (SHAP)

40m videoLevel 3

Lundberg & Lee, NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1705.07874)

Many attribution methods (LIME, DeepLIFT, layer-wise relevance propagation, classic Shapley estimates) explain a prediction as a sum of per-feature contributions, and within that family exactly one attribution satisfies local accuracy, missingness and consistency: the Shapley value of cooperative game theory.

Cold open

Every method so far was defined by a procedure: take a gradient, pool it, fit a local line. None said what a correct attribution should satisfy. SHAP starts from the other end: write down properties, and prove there is one answer. That move, plus a fast algorithm for tree ensembles a year later, is why SHAP plots are now the default explanation in tabular ML.

The problem

LIME's kernel and regularizer are chosen by hand (Day 303); DeepLIFT and LRP propagate relevance with rules that differ by layer type. On the same input they disagree, and there was no principled way to say which was right. Game theory had settled the analogous question in 1953: how to split a coalition's payoff among its players.

The method

Definition · additive feature attribution

An explanation model over simplified inputs \(z' \in \{0,1\}^M\) ("feature present or not") of the form

$$g(z') = \phi_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{M} \phi_i z'_i .$$

To evaluate the model with only a set \(S\) of features present, SHAP uses \(f_x(S) = \E[f(X) \mid X_S = x_S]\), in practice approximated by averaging over a background dataset for the missing features.

Definition · the Shapley value

For a set function \(v\) on players \(\{1,\dots,M\}\), player \(i\)'s value is its marginal contribution averaged over all \(M!\) orders in which the players could join:

$$\phi_i = \sum_{S \subseteq \{1..M\}\setminus\{i\}} \frac{|S|!\,(M-|S|-1)!}{M!}\,\big[v(S\cup\{i\}) - v(S)\big].$$

The weight counts orderings: \(|S|!\) ways to arrange the players before \(i\), \((M-|S|-1)!\) for those after.

Theorem · uniqueness

Shapley (1953). The Shapley value is the only attribution satisfying all four of

  • Efficiency: \(\sum_i \phi_i = v(\{1..M\}) - v(\varnothing)\).
  • Symmetry: if \(v(S\cup\{i\}) = v(S\cup\{j\})\) for every \(S\) containing neither, then \(\phi_i = \phi_j\).
  • Dummy: if \(v(S\cup\{i\}) = v(S)\) for every \(S\), then \(\phi_i = 0\).
  • Additivity: the values for \(v + w\) are the sums of the values for \(v\) and for \(w\).

Lundberg & Lee, Theorem 1. Within additive attributions, only \(\phi_i\) = Shapley values of \(v = f_x\) satisfy local accuracy (\(f(x) = g(x')\), efficiency in disguise), missingness (absent features get \(\phi_i = 0\)) and consistency (if a model changes so that \(i\)'s marginal contribution never decreases, \(\phi_i\) does not decrease). The proof rests on Young's (1985) characterization, which replaces additivity and dummy with this monotonicity.

Worked example

Three features, \(v(\varnothing)=0\), \(v(1)=10\), \(v(2)=20\), \(v(3)=0\), \(v(12)=40\), \(v(13)=10\), \(v(23)=20\), \(v(123)=40\). Feature 3 never changes anything. With \(M=3\) the weights are \(\tfrac13, \tfrac16, \tfrac16, \tfrac13\) for \(|S| = 0, 1, 1, 2\):

$$\phi_1 = \tfrac13(10) + \tfrac16(20) + \tfrac16(10) + \tfrac13(20) = 15,\qquad \phi_2 = \tfrac13(20) + \tfrac16(30) + \tfrac16(20) + \tfrac13(30) = 25,\qquad \phi_3 = 0.$$

The interaction (40 − 10 − 20 = 10) is split equally, and \(15 + 25 + 0 = 40 = v(123) - v(\varnothing)\).

The unifying class and KernelSHAP

The paper's second claim is that six existing methods are additive feature attributions: LIME, DeepLIFT, layer-wise relevance propagation, and three earlier Shapley-based estimators (Shapley regression values, Shapley sampling values, quantitative input influence). LIME is the bridge: its weighted regression can recover Shapley values if its three free choices are set correctly.

Theorem · the Shapley kernel

With \(\Omega(g) = 0\), squared loss, and weights

$$\pi_{x}(z') = \frac{M-1}{\binom{M}{|z'|}\,|z'|\,(M - |z'|)},$$

the LIME regression's solution is the Shapley values. The weight is infinite for \(|z'| = 0\) and \(|z'| = M\); implementations enforce those two as constraints (\(\phi_0\) and efficiency). Coalitions of very few or very many features get the most weight, because they are the most informative about single features.

The cost. Exact values need \(v\) on all \(2^M\) subsets, so everything practical is an approximation with its own assumptions: KernelSHAP samples coalitions; Deep SHAP propagates DeepLIFT-style multipliers with a background set; Linear SHAP is closed form under feature independence.

import itertools, math
import numpy as np

def shapley_exact(f, x, background):
    """Exact interventional SHAP: features outside S are taken from background rows."""
    M = len(x)
    def v(S):
        Z = background.copy()
        Z[:, list(S)] = x[list(S)]
        return f(Z).mean()
    phi = np.zeros(M)
    for i in range(M):
        rest = [j for j in range(M) if j != i]
        for k in range(M):
            for S in itertools.combinations(rest, k):
                w = math.factorial(k) * math.factorial(M - k - 1) / math.factorial(M)
                phi[i] += w * (v(S + (i,)) - v(S))
    return phi   # sums to f(x) - mean of f over the background

TreeSHAP is a later paper (Lundberg, Erion & Lee, 2018; Nature Machine Intelligence 2020). For tree ensembles it pushes all subsets down each tree at once, tracking for every root-to-leaf path what fraction of coalitions reach it, and cuts the cost from exponential in \(M\) to \(O(TLD^2)\) for \(T\) trees with \(L\) leaves and depth \(D\). Exact, fast values on XGBoost models (Day 13) are why SHAP dominates tabular ML.

Evidence

The NIPS paper's experiments are modest. The authors report that KernelSHAP reaches accurate estimates with fewer model evaluations than LIME or Shapley sampling; that in small user studies, SHAP attributions agreed with people's intuitive credit assignment on simple models more often than LIME or DeepLIFT did; and that on MNIST, explanations of why an image is an 8 rather than a 3 were sharper than those of the baselines. The case for SHAP is mostly the theorem, not the experiments.

Limits & what broke
  • Which expectation? \(\E[f(X)\mid X_S = x_S]\) is hard, so KernelSHAP replaces it by sampling missing features from the background independently of \(x_S\). Features are rarely independent, so the model is queried on impossible combinations (a 3-year-old with a mortgage) and attributions can go to them. Aas, Jullum & Løland (2021) and others proposed conditional estimators; Janzing et al. (AISTATS 2020) argued the interventional version is the right one. The axioms do not decide this.
  • The background is a modelling choice. All zeros, the training mean, or a sample of training rows give different \(\phi_0\) and different attributions. SHAP explains \(f(x)\) relative to a reference population, not in absolute terms.
  • The axioms are about the game, not the explanation. Kumar et al. (ICML 2020) argued that Shapley values make poor feature-importance measures for many human questions, and Slack et al. (AIES 2020) showed KernelSHAP can be fooled like LIME.

Bridge

SHAP's axioms say nothing about gradients or about whether a method reacts when the model changes. Tomorrow (Day 305) brings a second axiomatic method designed for deep networks, Integrated Gradients, and then a paper that tests saliency methods empirically and finds several of them do not depend on the model at all. Axiomatic Attribution + Sanity Checks for Saliency Maps

40m videoLevel 3

Sundararajan, Taly & Yan ICML 2017 (arXiv:1703.01365); Adebayo et al. NeurIPS 2018 (arXiv:1810.03292)

Integrated Gradients fixes the gradient's saturation problem by integrating it along a path from a baseline, which buys provable completeness at the price of a baseline choice; the Sanity Checks paper then shows that an attribution must be tested against the model it claims to explain, and several popular methods fail that test.

Cold open

Take a trained Inception network and randomize its weights, top layer first, then the next, until nothing learned is left. Recompute the Guided Backprop saliency map after each step. The map barely changes: a clean outline of the bird, from a network that now knows nothing about birds. An explanation that survives the destruction of the model was never explaining the model.

The problem

Gradients saturate (Day 301): a feature that pushed the score up and then flattened it gets zero gradient. DeepLIFT and LRP fix saturation by comparing against a reference input, but they use "discrete gradients" with layer-specific rules, and two networks that compute the same function can receive different attributions. Meanwhile, attribution methods were judged by looking at the pictures. Neither paper accepts that.

The method: Integrated Gradients

Definition · two axioms

Sensitivity. If the input and the baseline differ in one feature and give different predictions, that feature must get non-zero attribution. (And a feature the function does not depend on gets zero.)

Implementation invariance. Two networks that are functionally equal (same output for every input) get identical attributions, whatever their internals.

Plain gradients satisfy implementation invariance, because the chain rule only sees the function, but violate sensitivity. The paper's example: \(f(x) = 1 - \operatorname{ReLU}(1 - x)\), baseline \(x' = 0\), input \(x = 2\). The output moves from 0 to 1, yet \(f'(2) = 0\). DeepLIFT and LRP satisfy sensitivity but not implementation invariance: discrete gradients do not obey the chain rule.

Derivation · completeness

Integrated Gradients for feature \(i\), with baseline \(x'\):

$$\mathrm{IG}_i(x) = (x_i - x'_i)\int_0^1 \frac{\partial F\big(x' + \alpha(x - x')\big)}{\partial x_i}\,d\alpha .$$

Let \(\gamma(\alpha) = x' + \alpha(x - x')\), so \(\gamma'(\alpha) = x - x'\). By the chain rule, \(\frac{d}{d\alpha}F(\gamma(\alpha)) = \sum_i \partial_i F(\gamma(\alpha))\,(x_i - x'_i)\). Integrate from 0 to 1:

$$\sum_i \mathrm{IG}_i(x) = \int_0^1 \frac{d}{d\alpha}F(\gamma(\alpha))\,d\alpha = F(x) - F(x').$$

Attributions add up exactly to the change in output. Completeness implies sensitivity: if only feature \(i\) differs and the outputs differ, \(\mathrm{IG}_i = F(x) - F(x') \neq 0\). Implementation invariance holds because only gradients of \(F\) are used. For the ReLU example, the integrand is 1 on \([0, \tfrac12]\) and 0 after, so \(\mathrm{IG} = 2 \times \tfrac12 = 1\), the full change.

In practice the integral is a Riemann sum; the authors report that roughly 20–300 steps bring the sum within about 5% of \(F(x) - F(x')\), and recommend checking completeness as a diagnostic. They also show that among path methods (attributions defined by integrating along some path), the straight line is the one that treats symmetric features symmetrically.

import torch

def integrated_gradients(model, x, baseline, cls, steps=64):
    alphas = (torch.arange(steps, dtype=x.dtype) + 0.5) / steps      # midpoint rule
    path = baseline + alphas.view(-1, 1, 1, 1) * (x - baseline)       # steps x C x H x W
    path.requires_grad_(True)
    model(path)[:, cls].sum().backward()
    ig = (x - baseline) * path.grad.mean(0)                           # mean gradient on the path
    with torch.no_grad():
        gap = model(x[None])[0, cls] - model(baseline[None])[0, cls]
    return ig, ((ig.sum() - gap).abs() / gap.abs()).item()           # completeness error

The baseline is a modelling decision. The authors use a black image for vision and the all-zero embedding for text, chosen so the model's output there is near zero. But black is not "no information": in the formula, a pixel equal to the baseline gets \(x_i - x'_i = 0\) and hence zero attribution however important it is, so the dark plumage of a crow can never be credited. Blurred, uniform-noise or dataset-averaged baselines give different maps (Sturmfels, Lundberg & Lee, Distill 2020). SHAP (Day 304) faces the same choice under the name "background".

Evidence, part 1

Sundararajan et al. apply IG to an ImageNet Inception model, a diabetic-retinopathy model, question classification, machine translation and a chemistry model, and show that the attributions are plausible and occasionally expose a bug. Like every paper so far, the evidence is mostly visual. The axioms guarantee internal consistency, not that a human reading the map draws correct conclusions.

The method: Sanity Checks

Algorithm · two randomization tests
  1. Model parameter randomization. Re-initialize the weights of a trained network, cascading from the logits down to the first layer (and, separately, one layer at a time). Recompute the attribution map after each step.
  2. Data randomization. Train the same architecture on permuted labels until it memorizes them. Compute the maps.
  3. Compare with the original maps by Spearman rank correlation (with and without absolute values), SSIM and HOG similarity, and by eye.
  4. A method passes if the maps change substantially in both tests; a method whose maps stay the same depends on the input, not on what the model learned.

Evidence, part 2

The authors also show that several maps resemble the output of a plain edge detector, which needs neither a model nor training data. The data-randomization results point the same way: a model that has memorized random labels can produce maps that look much like those of a model trained on real ones.

Limits & what broke
  • Passing is necessary, not sufficient. A method can change when the model is destroyed and still misattribute. The tests rule methods out; they do not certify.
  • Visual similarity metrics are crude. Rank correlation of absolute values can flatter or punish a method; later work debated which similarity measure is fair. The headline failure of Guided Backprop has held up (see also Nie et al., ICML 2018).
  • IG's guarantees are about the path, not the model's reasoning. Completeness holds for any baseline, so it cannot tell you which baseline is right. Off-path inputs (dark or blurred images) may be far from the data.
  • Related checks. Kindermans et al. (2019) showed some methods change under a constant input shift that the model ignores; Hooker et al. (NeurIPS 2019, ROAR) found many attributions no better than random at identifying features whose removal and retraining hurts accuracy.

Bridge

Zoom In: An Introduction to Circuits

45m videoLevel 3

Olah, Cammarata, Schubert, Goh, Petrov & Carter, Distill 2020

Treat a trained network as an object of natural science: its features are meaningful directions, the weights between features form circuits that implement readable algorithms, and claims about any of them must be backed by several independent lines of evidence that agree.

Cold open

In InceptionV1 there are units that respond to curves of one orientation and not to straight lines or to curves at other angles. The authors claim more than "this neuron likes curves". They show what images excite it, how its response varies on synthetic curves as the angle sweeps round, which earlier units feed it through which weights, and what later units do with its output. Then they build a curve detector by hand from those weights. After five days of saliency maps that failed sanity checks, the change of standard is the lesson.

The problem

Attribution (Day 301–Day 305) explains one input at a time and treats the network as a function. It says which pixels mattered, not what the network computes. Feature visualization (Day 62) showed what individual units respond to, but pictures alone proved easy to over-read. The authors propose to study the mechanism: features, the connections between them, and how those connections implement behaviour. They compare the situation to early cell biology, when a new instrument (the microscope) made a new science possible.

The method: three claims, stated to be falsifiable

Definition · the three speculative claims
  1. Features. Features are the fundamental unit of neural networks. They correspond to directions in activation space, and they can be rigorously studied and understood.
  2. Circuits. Features are connected by weights, forming circuits. These too can be rigorously studied and understood.
  3. Universality. Analogous features and circuits form across models and tasks.

The authors call them speculative and frame each so that it could be wrong: a feature claim predicts how a unit will respond to new stimuli; a circuit claim predicts what happens if the weights are edited; universality predicts what you will find in a network you have not yet opened.

Evidence: circuits read off the weights

Curve detectors. In the mixed3b layer, a family of units responds to curves at different orientations. Synthetic stimuli give clean tuning curves: response peaks at one orientation and falls off smoothly as the curve rotates, while the response to straight lines is weak. The incoming weights show how each is built: excitation from earlier curve and edge features aligned with the unit's orientation, inhibition from those at opposite orientations, arranged spatially along the arc. The family is approximately equivariant: the weights of the detector for one orientation look like rotated copies of the weights for another.

High-low frequency detectors. Earlier, in mixed3a, units fire where high-frequency texture sits on one side of a boundary and low-frequency (blurred) texture on the other, a useful cue for an object's edge against an out-of-focus background. Their weights combine earlier high- and low-frequency features on opposite sides of the receptive field.

The dog-head circuit. In the mixed4 layers, separate units detect dog heads facing left and facing right. Later units add both, with the spatial layout of the weights matching each orientation, giving a pose-invariant dog-head detector. The authors call this motif unioning over cases. A car detector is assembled the same way: window features at the top of its receptive field, car-body features in the middle, wheels at the bottom.

The step that matters is the last one. A circuit is a set of weights between features, and weights are fixed parameters, not a per-input explanation. Reading them gives an algorithm that holds for every input, which no attribution method can offer.

Polysemanticity and superposition

The approach depends on units meaning one thing, and many do not. The article shows polysemantic neurons that respond to unrelated inputs: one late-layer unit responds to cat faces, fronts of cars and cat legs. The car example also shows the network taking a clean car feature and spreading it across several polysemantic units in the next layer, mixed with dog features. The authors name this superposition: the network represents more features than it has neurons by letting them share units. This is the first use of the term in the circuits work, and Day 309 turns it into a theory.

Why the format mattered

The evidence is many small images per unit and diagrams of weights between dozens of units: material that a static eight-page PDF cannot hold. Distill's interactive, web-native format, and the follow-up articles in the Circuits thread (curve detectors, curve circuits, high-low frequency detectors, weight banding, branch specialization), let readers inspect the evidence instead of trusting a summary figure. Distill went on hiatus in 2021; the transformer work moved to its own thread (Day 307).

Limits & what broke
  • Universality is the boldest claim and the least settled. The article supports it with examples: curve and high-low frequency detectors appear in several vision architectures. Systematic tests are harder. Earlier work on convergent learning (Li et al., ICLR 2016) found some units shared across networks and many not, and later studies of toy models report partial universality at best.
  • One network, hand analysis. Almost everything is in InceptionV1 and was found by people studying units one at a time. That does not scale to models with billions of parameters, which is what pushed the field toward automated methods and dictionary learning (Day 310).
  • Neurons are the wrong unit when there is superposition. The claim is about features as directions; in practice the analysis is of neurons, which works only where neurons happen to be monosemantic.
  • Mostly correlational at the feature level. Tuning curves and dataset examples describe behaviour; the hand-built circuits and weight readings are what make the claims mechanistic. Not every claim in the thread got all of them.

Bridge

A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits

55m videoLevel 4

Elhage et al., Transformer Circuits Thread 2021

Rewrite an attention-only transformer as a linear residual stream that heads read from and write to; each head then splits into a QK circuit (where to attend) and an OV circuit (what to move), and the whole model expands into a sum of interpretable paths, with composition between layers creating behaviour, such as induction heads, that no single layer can implement.

Cold open

Prerequisites first, because this is the hardest paper of the block: the transformer's attention equations (Day 129) and residual connections (Day 67). Read it in two sittings: zero- and one-layer models first, two-layer composition second. The payoff is a set of tools every later transformer-interpretability paper uses without re-deriving: residual stream, QK and OV circuits, path expansion, composition.

The problem

Circuits work on vision models (Day 306) read algorithms off convolution filters between neurons. A transformer does not give that handle directly. Attention moves information between positions with weights computed on the fly, and the residual stream has no special coordinate system. To study transformers the same way, the authors needed a way to write the model in which its parts can be analysed separately. They simplify hard to get it: attention-only models (no MLP layers), biases and layer norm ignored or folded in, and models of zero, one and two layers.

The method

Definition · the residual stream

Token \(t\) (one-hot) is embedded as \(x_0 = W_E t\). Each layer adds the outputs of its heads, \(x_{\ell+1} = x_\ell + \sum_h h(x_\ell)\), and the logits are \(W_U x_L\). Every component reads from the stream through a linear map and writes back by addition. The stream is a shared communication channel; any layer can read what any earlier layer wrote.

No privileged basis. Nothing in an attention-only stream acts on coordinates one at a time. Replace every read matrix \(W\) by \(W R^\top\) and every write matrix \(W'\) by \(R W'\) for an orthogonal \(R\), and the model computes exactly the same function. So individual residual dimensions mean nothing in themselves; only directions defined by what reads and writes them do. Elementwise nonlinearities (MLP neurons) do create a privileged basis, which is why neurons were a reasonable unit in vision.

Derivation · QK and OV

For one head at destination position \(i\) attending to source \(j\): the score is \(q_i^\top k_j = (W_Q x_i)^\top (W_K x_j) = x_i^\top W_Q^\top W_K\, x_j\). The output is \(\sum_j A_{ij}\, W_O W_V x_j\). Define

$$W_{QK} = W_Q^\top W_K, \qquad W_{OV} = W_O W_V, \qquad A = \operatorname{softmax}\big(x^\top W_{QK}\, x\big)\ \text{(causal mask)}.$$

Then the head's output is \(h(x) = (A \otimes W_{OV})\,x\): \(A\) mixes positions, \(W_{OV}\) acts on the residual vector at each position. The two factors are separate. The attention pattern depends only on \(W_{QK}\); given the pattern, the output is linear and depends only on \(W_{OV}\). \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) are not individually meaningful (\(W_Q \to M W_Q\), \(W_K \to M^{-\top} W_K\) leaves \(W_{QK}\) unchanged), and both products have rank at most \(d_{\text{head}}\).

Zero and one layer: paths you can enumerate

Zero layers. Logits \(= W_U W_E\,t\): a bigram table, the best prediction of the next token from the current one.

Derivation · the one-layer path expansion

With one layer, \(x_1 = x_0 + \sum_h (A^h \otimes W^h_{OV})\,x_0\) and \(x_0 = W_E t\). Multiply by \(W_U\):

$$T = \underbrace{\mathrm{Id} \otimes W_U W_E}_{\text{direct path: bigrams}} \;+\; \sum_h \underbrace{A^h \otimes \big(W_U W^h_{OV} W_E\big)}_{\text{one path per head}},\qquad A^h = \operatorname{softmax}\big(t^\top W_E^\top W^h_{QK} W_E\, t\big).$$

Each head now has two vocabulary-by-vocabulary matrices: the QK circuit \(W_E^\top W^h_{QK} W_E\) (how much each destination token wants to attend to each source token) and the OV circuit \(W_U W^h_{OV} W_E\) (how attending to a source token changes each output logit). Both can be read directly.

The behaviour this produces is skip-trigrams: "source token A, somewhere earlier ... current token B ⟶ predict C". Many heads are copying heads: their OV circuits raise the logit of the attended token itself, which shows up as mostly positive eigenvalues of the OV matrix. Because QK and OV are factored, skip-trigrams come with bugs. The paper's example: a head that learns "keep ... in ⟶ mind" and "keep ... at ⟶ bay" must also raise "keep ... in ⟶ bay" and "keep ... at ⟶ mind", since which token is attended to and what it predicts are chosen by separate matrices.

Two layers: composition

A second-layer head can read the outputs of first-layer heads in three ways:

  • Q-composition: its query reads a first-layer head's output.
  • K-composition: its key reads it.
  • V-composition: its value reads it, which creates a virtual attention head with pattern \(A^{h_2}A^{h_1}\) and OV circuit \(W^{h_2}_{OV} W^{h_1}_{OV}\).

V-composition adds new terms to the path expansion; Q- and K-composition instead make the second layer's attention patterns depend on what the first layer moved. The paper measures how strongly each pair of heads composes with a normalized product of their weight matrices, and finds strong K-composition in two-layer models. That circuit is the induction head: a first-layer head writes "the previous token was A" into each position, and a second-layer head's key reads this, so a query at a later A attends to the token right after the earlier A and its OV circuit copies it. Virtual heads and composition are what make depth matter. Day 308 is the full story.

Why attention-only toys. MLP layers hold most of a transformer's parameters but their nonlinearity breaks the path expansion. Removing them keeps everything linear given the attention patterns, while still producing non-trivial algorithms. The authors are explicit that the results are about these toys; extending them to MLPs became the superposition and dictionary-learning program (Day 309, Day 310).

Evidence

The evidence is analysis of small trained models: reading QK and OV circuits of one-layer models for skip-trigrams and copying; eigenvalue statistics for copying heads; composition scores and attention patterns on repeated text for two-layer induction heads. It is persuasive for the toys. It does not show that large models are well described by a handful of heads, and the paper does not claim it.

Limits & what broke
  • No MLPs, no layer norm. Real transformers have both. The path expansion grows exponentially with depth, and nonlinear MLP paths do not factor at all.
  • Neurons are still hard. Removing the privileged-basis worry for the stream does not make MLP neurons interpretable; superposition (Day 309) explains why.
  • Heads are not clean units. Later work found heads that do several things, and behaviour spread over many heads; automated circuit discovery methods arose for this reason.

In-context Learning and Induction Heads

45m videoLevel 4

Olsson et al., Transformer Circuits Thread 2022

A two-head circuit that completes patterns of the form [A][B] … [A] → [B] forms abruptly early in training, at the same moment that in-context learning improves sharply, and six lines of evidence, causal in small models and suggestive in large ones, argue that it is a major mechanism behind in-context learning.

Cold open

Plot a small transformer's training loss and you see a smooth decreasing curve with one visible bump early on. The bump is not noise. Over that stretch of training, a specific circuit appears in the weights, and the model's ability to use earlier context to predict later tokens jumps. A mechanistic event shows up in the most aggregate measurement of all, the loss curve.

The problem

GPT-3 (Day 145) made in-context learning famous: a frozen model improves at a task given examples in its prompt. Nobody knew what mechanism did this. Day 307 had found, in two-layer attention-only models, a circuit that copies continuations of earlier tokens. The question is whether that circuit explains in-context learning more broadly, including in large models with MLPs, and how one could show this rather than merely suggest it.

The method: the circuit and two measurements

Definition · induction head

A head that, on repeated sequences, does two things: prefix matching (at the current token A it attends to the token that followed an earlier occurrence of A) and copying (its OV circuit raises the logit of the token it attends to). The mechanism found in small models uses K-composition with a previous-token head in an earlier layer.

The in-context learning score. To measure in-context learning without choosing tasks, the authors use the loss on the 500th token of a context minus the loss on the 50th token, averaged over many dataset examples. The more negative, the more the model gains from having more context. The measurement is simple and reusable for any language model.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

@torch.no_grad()
def icl_score(model, tokens):                  # tokens: batch x T ids, T >= 500
    logits = model(tokens[:, :-1])             # batch x (T-1) x vocab
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.transpose(1, 2), tokens[:, 1:], reduction="none")
    # loss[:, i] is the loss on token i+2 (1-indexed): token 500 -> 498, token 50 -> 48
    return (loss[:, 498] - loss[:, 48]).mean().item()

Evidence: six arguments

ArgumentWhat was doneKind of evidence
1. Macroscopic co-occurrenceInduction heads form during a short phase change early in training, at the same time as the ICL score drops sharply and the loss curve shows its bump.correlational
2. Macroscopic co-perturbationAn architecture change (a "smeared key" that mixes each key with the previous position's) lets even one-layer models form induction heads, and moves the phase change; the ICL jump moves with it.interventional, at model level

The plan's emphasis is right: the evidence becomes causal only through the interventions (arguments 2 and 3). Co-occurrence alone would be compatible with some third change in training causing both. The authors grade their own confidence by model class: strongest for small attention-only models, where the ablations were done; weaker for small models with MLPs; and suggestive, mostly correlational, for large models.

Beyond exact copying. The same heads in larger models complete patterns of the form [A*][B*] … [A] → [B], where A* is similar to A rather than identical, for example a word and its translation. That is what makes them a plausible general ICL mechanism rather than a repetition trick: few-shot prompts are pattern completion over similar, not identical, items.

Limits & what broke
  • "A major mechanism", not "the mechanism". The paper argues induction heads account for a large part of ICL as measured by the ICL score. Task-level few-shot learning in large models plausibly involves more, and the authors say so.
  • The ICL score is token-level. It measures "later tokens are easier", which induction heads are tailor-made to improve. A different definition of ICL (learning a new input-label mapping) could give a different picture.
  • Follow-ups refined the story. Later work studied how training data properties drive the emergence of ICL (Chan et al., NeurIPS 2022), found that ICL can be transient during long training (Singh et al., 2023), and studied the formation of induction heads in detail in controlled settings.

Toy Models of Superposition

50m videoLevel 4

Elhage et al., Transformer Circuits Thread 2022

When features are sparse, a network can represent more features than it has dimensions by packing them into nearly orthogonal directions and letting a ReLU filter out the small interference; a two-line toy model shows this happening, with surprising discrete geometry, and it explains why neurons are polysemantic by default.

Cold open

Train a model to squeeze five features through two dimensions and reconstruct them. With dense inputs it keeps the two most important features and drops the rest. Make the inputs sparse and run it again: the five weight vectors arrange themselves into a regular pentagon. The model has stored five things in a two-dimensional space, and it did so with a shape nobody asked for. The whole experiment runs in seconds on a laptop.

The problem

Day 306 found neurons that respond to unrelated things (cat faces and car fronts) and named the cause superposition, without a theory. If superposition is real and common, then reading neurons one at a time, the method that worked for curve detectors, is the wrong approach in general. The paper asks when a network would choose superposition and what it looks like.

The method

Definition · the toy model

Inputs \(x \in \R^n\): each feature is 0 with probability \(S\) (the sparsity), otherwise uniform on \([0,1]\). Each feature has an importance \(I_i\). Compress to \(m < n\) dimensions and reconstruct:

$$h = Wx,\qquad x' = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(W^\top h + b\big) = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(W^\top W x + b\big),\qquad \mathcal L = \E_x \sum_i I_i\,(x_i - x'_i)^2 .$$

Column \(W_i \in \R^m\) is feature \(i\)'s direction. The baseline is the same model without the ReLU.

Derivation · why the ReLU makes sharing pay

Take \(n = 2\), \(m = 1\), feature density \(p = 1 - S\), importances \(1\) and \(I_2 \le 1\), no bias. Compare two solutions.

Drop feature 2, \(W = [1, 0]\): \(x'_1 = x_1\), \(x'_2 = 0\). Loss \(= I_2\,\E[x_2^2] = I_2\,p/3\).

Antipodal pair, \(W = [1, -1]\): \(h = x_1 - x_2\), so \(x'_1 = \operatorname{ReLU}(x_1 - x_2)\), \(x'_2 = \operatorname{ReLU}(x_2 - x_1)\). If only one feature is active, the other output is \(\operatorname{ReLU}(\text{negative}) = 0\): reconstruction is exact. Error occurs only when both are active (probability \(p^2\)); then each output is off by \(\min(x_1, x_2)\), and \(\E[\min(u_1,u_2)^2] = \int_0^1 2(1-t)\,t^2\,dt = \tfrac16\). Loss \(= p^2(1 + I_2)/6\).

Superposition wins when \(p^2(1+I_2)/6 < I_2\,p/3\), that is \(p < 2I_2/(1+I_2)\): sparse enough, or important enough. Without the ReLU, the interference \(-x_1\) on output 2 is never removed and the linear model prefers to drop the feature. This is the mechanism in miniature: compression with interference, and a nonlinearity that cleans up small interference.

import torch

def train_toy(n=5, m=2, density=0.05, steps=5000, seed=0):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    imp = 0.9 ** torch.arange(n, dtype=torch.float)                # feature importance
    W = torch.nn.Parameter(0.1 * torch.randn(m, n))
    b = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n))
    opt = torch.optim.Adam([W, b], lr=1e-2)
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = torch.rand(1024, n) * (torch.rand(1024, n) < density)  # sparse features in [0, 1]
        x_hat = torch.relu(x @ W.T @ W + b)                        # ReLU(W^T W x + b)
        loss = (imp * (x - x_hat) ** 2).sum(1).mean()
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return W.detach(), b.detach()   # plot the columns of W; compare density 1.0 and 0.05

Evidence: what the toy model does

Dense features: the ReLU model behaves like the linear one, keeping roughly the \(m\) most important features in orthogonal directions, as PCA would. As sparsity rises, less important features enter in superposition, first as antipodal pairs, then in denser arrangements, with negative biases that cut off the interference. Johnson–Lindenstrauss-style counting explains why there is room: high-dimensional spaces hold far more than \(m\) almost-orthogonal directions, and sparse features rarely collide.

Definition · feature dimensionality $$D_i = \frac{\|W_i\|^2}{\sum_j \big(\hat W_i \cdot W_j\big)^2},\qquad \hat W_i = W_i/\|W_i\| .$$

A feature with its own dimension has \(D_i = 1\); one of an antipodal pair has \(\tfrac12\); a vertex of a triangle \(\tfrac23\); of a pentagon \(\tfrac25\) (check: \(1 + 2\cos^2 72^\circ + 2\cos^2 144^\circ = 2.5\)).

The geometry is discrete. With many features in larger \(m\), the authors find dimensionalities clustering at a few fractions (1, 3/4, 2/3, 1/2, 2/5, 3/8), corresponding to tetrahedra, triangles, antipodal pairs, pentagons and square antiprisms. The overall arrangement splits into orthogonal subspaces each holding one such polytope, which they describe as a tegum product. As sparsity changes, the number of dimensions per feature sticks at values like 1/2 and 2/5 and jumps between them. Nothing in the setup asks for regular polytopes; the authors call the finding surprising, and it is.

The phase diagram. For small cases (two features in one dimension, three in two) they sweep sparsity and the relative importance of the extra feature, and find sharp regions: the extra feature is dropped, gets a dedicated dimension, or shares in superposition. The derivation above is a slice through that diagram.

The paper also shows a model computing a function (absolute value) while its inputs are in superposition, and studies a model with a hidden ReLU layer, a privileged basis, where neurons become polysemantic exactly when features exceed neurons.

Limits & what broke
  • It is a toy. Independent, uniformly distributed sparse features and a tied-weight autoencoder are assumptions, not observations about language models. Correlated features behave differently (the paper reports they tend to be placed orthogonally to each other).
  • Linear features are assumed. Features as directions is a hypothesis. Later work found some language-model features are multi-dimensional, for example circular representations of days and months (Engels et al., 2024).
  • Evidence for superposition in real models is indirect. The strongest support came later, from the success of dictionary learning on real activations (Day 310, Day 311).

Bridge

The strategic conclusion: if a model stores features in superposition, neurons will not line up with features, and interpretability has to find the feature basis rather than read it off. The paper lists options (train models without superposition, or recover an overcomplete basis after training). Tomorrow (Day 310) takes the second route with a sparse autoencoder. The paper also speculates that superposition may connect to adversarial vulnerability (Day 321). Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 306 (polysemantic neurons), ⟵ Day 307 (privileged bases).

Towards Monosemanticity: Decomposing Language Models With Dictionary Learning

50m videoLevel 4

Bricken et al., Transformer Circuits Thread 2023

A sparse autoencoder trained to reconstruct a language model's MLP activations through many more latent units than there are neurons, with an L1 penalty on those units, recovers features that are far more interpretable than the neurons themselves, which is what the superposition hypothesis predicts.

Cold open

A one-layer transformer has 512 MLP neurons, and most of them respond to a jumble of unrelated contexts. Decompose the same activations with a sparse autoencoder and you get thousands of features, many of which fire on one recognizable thing: Arabic script, DNA sequences, base64 strings, Hebrew text. Nothing about the model changed; only the basis used to read it.

The problem

The toy model of Day 309 says a network can store \(n\) sparse features in \(m < n\) dimensions as nearly orthogonal directions. If so, neuron-by-neuron analysis is hopeless, and the task is to recover the directions. That is a classical problem: express each data vector as a sparse combination of atoms from an overcomplete dictionary. Olshausen & Field (1996; Day 54) solved it for natural image patches and got edge-like receptive fields. This paper applies the same objective to a language model's activations.

The method

Definition · sparse autoencoder

For an activation vector \(x \in \R^{d}\) and a dictionary of \(F \gg d\) features:

$$f(x) = \operatorname{ReLU}\big(W_e (x - b_d) + b_e\big) \in \R^{F},\qquad \hat x = W_d f(x) + b_d,$$ $$\mathcal L = \E_x\Big[\|x - \hat x\|_2^2 + \lambda\,\|f(x)\|_1\Big],$$

with the columns of \(W_d\) (the dictionary atoms, one per feature) kept at unit norm so the penalty cannot be dodged by shrinking \(f\) and growing \(W_d\). Feature \(i\)'s activation is \(f_i(x)\); its direction in activation space is column \(i\) of \(W_d\).

Why this shape fits superposition. The hypothesis is a generative model: \(x \approx \sum_i f_i\, d_i\), with many more directions \(d_i\) than dimensions and only a few \(f_i\) non-zero at a time. An overcomplete decoder supplies the directions, the L1 penalty asks for few active features, and the ReLU encoder is a cheap, amortized estimate of the coefficients. Sparsity is what makes the decomposition identifiable: with \(F > d\) and no penalty, infinitely many dictionaries reconstruct perfectly.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class SAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, n_feat):
        super().__init__()
        self.W_e = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(n_feat, d) * 0.01)
        self.b_e = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(n_feat))
        self.W_d = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(d, n_feat) * 0.01)
        self.b_d = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d))

    def forward(self, x):
        f = torch.relu((x - self.b_d) @ self.W_e.T + self.b_e)   # sparse feature activations
        return f @ self.W_d.T + self.b_d, f

def sae_loss(sae, x, l1=1e-3):
    with torch.no_grad():
        sae.W_d /= sae.W_d.norm(dim=0, keepdim=True)          # unit-norm dictionary atoms
    x_hat, f = sae(x)
    return ((x - x_hat) ** 2).sum(1).mean() + l1 * f.abs().sum(1).mean()

Evidence: are the features real?

The authors train SAEs on the MLP activations of a one-layer transformer, with dictionaries ranging from the number of neurons up to hundreds of times more, and evaluate features in several independent ways, echoing the evidence standard of Day 306:

  • Activating examples across the full range of activation strengths, not only the top examples.
  • Logit effects: the feature's decoder direction pushed through the unembedding shows which next tokens it promotes (a Hebrew-text feature promotes Hebrew tokens).
  • Ablation and steering: setting a feature's activation changes the model's predictions in the way its description implies.
  • Automated interpretability: a language model writes an explanation from examples, then predicts activations on new text from the explanation alone; the correlation is the score. By this measure and by human rating, the authors report features scoring much better than neurons.

Two further results matter. Features from SAEs trained on two different transformers (different random seeds) often correspond, a measured form of the universality claim. And reconstructions can be substituted back into the model with only a modest increase in its loss, so the dictionary captures most of what the MLP layer does, though not all.

Feature splitting

Train dictionaries of increasing size on the same activations and a single feature in a small dictionary becomes a family of more specific features in a larger one: a broad feature for a script or format splits into features for particular contexts within it. The larger dictionary does not just add unrelated features; it refines existing ones. So there is no canonical "number of features" in a model. Dictionary size is a resolution dial, and which features you see depends on where you set it.

Limits & what broke
  • Dead and ultra-rare features. Many latents stop firing during training; the authors resample them, and a cluster of features with extremely low firing density remained hard to interpret.
  • Shrinkage. The L1 penalty biases active feature magnitudes downward, so reconstructions systematically under-shoot. Later SAEs change the activation function to fix this: Gated SAEs and JumpReLU SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) and TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2024).
  • The reconstruction/sparsity frontier. \(\lambda\) trades error for sparsity, and the residual error is not small enough to ignore. What lives in the error term is unknown.
  • One layer, one small model. Whether this works on production models was the open question; Day 311 answers it.

Bridge

Dictionary learning turns superposition from an obstacle into a method: find the basis, then read it. The obvious question is scale. Scaling Monosemanticity: Extracting Interpretable Features from Claude 3 Sonnet

40m videoLevel 4

Templeton et al., Transformer Circuits Thread 2024

Sparse autoencoders scale to a production language model: dictionaries of millions of features on Claude 3 Sonnet's residual stream yield abstract, multilingual and multimodal features, and clamping a feature's activation changes the model's behaviour in the predicted way, which turns interpretability from description into intervention.

Cold open

In May 2024 Anthropic briefly offered "Golden Gate Claude": the same model with one dictionary feature, the one that responds to mentions and images of the Golden Gate Bridge, clamped to a high value. Asked about itself, it answered that it was the bridge. The demo is a joke with a serious point: if amplifying one learned direction changes what the model says about its own identity, the feature is not just correlated with the concept; it is part of how the model represents it.

The problem

Day 310 worked on the MLP of a one-layer model. Production models are deep, wide and expensive to run, and nobody knew whether SAE features would stay interpretable at that scale, whether abstract concepts would get features at all, or whether the approach could support claims that matter for safety. Feature descriptions are also correlational. A method that can change behaviour through a feature tests the description causally.

The method

The SAE recipe is that of Day 310, applied to the residual stream at a middle layer of Claude 3 Sonnet, at three dictionary sizes of roughly 1, 4 and 34 million features. The authors fit scaling laws for SAE training (reconstruction loss against compute) to choose dictionary size, learning rate and training length for a given budget.

Derivation · feature steering

Write the activation as the SAE's reconstruction plus its error:

$$x = \sum_i f_i(x)\, d_i + b_d + \varepsilon(x),$$

where \(d_i\) is column \(i\) of \(W_d\). To clamp feature \(j\) to value \(c\), keep the error term and change only that coefficient:

$$x' = \sum_{i\ne j} f_i(x)\,d_i + c\,d_j + b_d + \varepsilon(x) = x + \big(c - f_j(x)\big)\,d_j .$$

The forward pass continues from \(x'\). Keeping \(\varepsilon\) matters: replacing \(x\) by the bare reconstruction would also remove whatever the SAE fails to capture, and the behaviour change could not be attributed to feature \(j\). The authors clamp to multiples of a feature's maximum observed activation, positive or negative.

import torch

def steer_hook(sae, j, value):
    """Forward hook for a module returning residual activations (batch x T x d)."""
    def hook(module, inp, out):
        f = torch.relu((out - sae.b_d) @ sae.W_e.T + sae.b_e)
        delta = (value - f[..., j]).unsqueeze(-1) * sae.W_d[:, j]
        return out + delta              # = reconstruction + error, with f_j set to value
    return hook

Evidence

  • Abstract features. Beyond concrete entities, the dictionaries contain features for things like code bugs, inner conflict, secrecy and flattery, and features that respond to the same concept across languages.
  • Multimodal without image training. The SAEs were trained on text activations only, yet many features also respond to images of their concept: the Golden Gate feature fires on pictures of the bridge.
  • Feature neighbourhoods. Features with nearby decoder directions are semantically related, and larger dictionaries split a concept into finer neighbours, the feature splitting of Day 310 at scale.
  • Steering works. Clamping features changes behaviour in ways matching their descriptions. The authors report examples where amplifying a feature overcame trained-in behaviour, such as a scam-email feature leading the model to draft one it would otherwise refuse, and a sycophancy feature producing effusive praise.
  • Coverage grows with size. Probing sets of concepts (for example, places in a city, chemical elements), the authors find more of them have dedicated features in larger SAEs, and concepts that are rarer in training data need larger dictionaries.

Safety-relevant features, with the caution attached

The paper reports features related to unsafe code and backdoors, bias, sycophancy, deception, power-seeking and dangerous content. The authors are explicit about what this does not show. A "deception" feature fires when deception is represented, for instance when the model reads or writes about lying. Its existence does not mean the model is deceptive, and it does not by itself tell us when the model uses it in its own behaviour. They present these as a starting point for safety work: features one could monitor or steer, and whose behaviour one could study during training, not as findings about the model's dispositions. This matters for the alignment questions of Day 208, where behavioural tests failed to reveal a trained-in backdoor.

Limits & what broke
  • Completeness. The features found are a small subset of the features the model plausibly uses. You only see concepts your dictionary is large enough to hold, and the cost of going bigger is large. This is the main open problem the authors name.
  • Shrinkage and dead latents. The L1 recipe underestimates activations and leaves many latents dead. JumpReLU and Gated SAEs (Rajamanoharan et al., 2024) and TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2024) changed the activation function and improved the reconstruction/sparsity trade-off.
  • Feature absorption. Chanin et al. (2024) showed that a general feature can fail to fire on cases that a more specific feature absorbs, which makes features unreliable as classifiers.
  • The 2024–2025 critiques of SAE evaluation. Benchmarks such as AxBench (Wu et al., 2025) found SAE-based steering weaker than simple baselines like prompting, and sparse-probing studies (Kantamneni et al., 2025) found SAE features rarely beat linear probes on downstream tasks; the Google DeepMind interpretability team publicly deprioritized SAE research in 2025 for similar reasons. Interpretable-looking features have also been reported from SAEs on randomly initialized transformers, which questions how much of the interpretability comes from the model. This is a live research area; treat both the results and the critiques as provisional.

Bridge

Features tell you what a model represents; steering tells you that a representation is used. Neither tells you where a specific piece of knowledge is stored and computed. Locating and Editing Factual Associations in GPT (ROME)

45m videoLevel 3

Meng, Bau, Andonian & Belinkov, NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2202.05262)

Causal tracing, which corrupts the subject of a factual prompt and restores one hidden state at a time, localizes factual recall in GPT to mid-layer MLPs at the last subject token; treating such an MLP as a linear associative memory then allows a single fact to be rewritten with a closed-form rank-one weight update.

Cold open

GPT-2 XL completes "The Space Needle is in downtown" with "Seattle". Suppose you want it to say "Rome" instead. ROME does this with one rank-one change to one weight matrix, and in the paper's evaluations such edits usually carry over to paraphrases of the prompt while facts about other subjects stay put. Two questions sit behind that: where in the network does the fact live, and how would you show it?

The problem

Probing and attention inspection (Day 313, Day 314) can show that information is present or that a head looks somewhere; neither shows that a component is used to produce an output. Fine-tuning a model on a corrected fact tends to either fail to generalize to paraphrases or damage unrelated facts. The paper wants a localization method based on intervention, and an editing method guided by it.

The method, part 1: causal tracing

Algorithm · causal tracing
  1. Clean run. Run the prompt (subject \(s\), relation; e.g. "The Space Needle is in downtown") and record every hidden state \(h_i^{(\ell)}\) and \(P(o)\) for the correct object.
  2. Corrupted run. Add Gaussian noise to the embeddings of the subject tokens only, so the model can no longer tell which subject it is reading. Record \(P_*(o)\), which drops.
  3. Corrupted-with-restoration run. Repeat the corrupted run, but replace one hidden state \(h_i^{(\ell)}\) (token \(i\), layer \(\ell\)) with its clean value and let the rest of the computation proceed. Record \(P_{*,\text{clean } h_i^{(\ell)}}(o)\).
  4. Indirect effect of that state: \(\mathrm{IE} = P_{*,\text{clean } h_i^{(\ell)}}(o) - P_*(o)\); the total effect is \(P(o) - P_*(o)\). Average over many facts, and repeat restoring only MLP outputs or only attention outputs.

The early site is the paper's central finding: mid-layer MLP modules, processing the last token of the subject, mediate much of the effect. The late site, attention at the final token, plausibly moves the recalled attribute to where the prediction is made. The authors strengthen the MLP claim with a second intervention: when MLP computations at later tokens are frozen at their corrupted values, restoring the hidden state in the middle layers loses much of its effect.

The methodological point outlasts the paper's specific claims. Causal tracing is activation patching: it measures what happens when a component's value is set, not what the component correlates with. That is much stronger evidence than attention weights or probe accuracy.

The method, part 2: the MLP as a key–value memory

Geva et al. (EMNLP 2021) read a transformer FFN, \(\mathrm{FFN}(x) = W_{\text{out}}\,\sigma(W_{\text{in}} x)\), as a memory: rows of \(W_{\text{in}}\) are keys matched against the input, and \(W_{\text{out}}\) returns a weighted sum of value vectors. It is also where most parameters live. Per layer, attention has \(4d^2\) weights (\(W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O\)) and an FFN with hidden width \(4d\) has \(2 \cdot 4d^2 = 8d^2\), so FFNs hold about \(8/12 = 2/3\) of the non-embedding parameters (Day 129).

Derivation · the rank-one edit

Treat \(W = W_{\text{out}}\) of one layer as a linear associative memory with keys \(K = [k_1, \dots]\) (inputs to \(W\)) and values \(V\): \(WK \approx V\). The least-squares memory is \(W = VK^\top C^{-1}\) with \(C = KK^\top\). Insert one new pair \((k_*, v_*)\) while disturbing the rest as little as possible:

$$\min_{\hat W}\ \|\hat W K - V\|_F^2 \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \hat W k_* = v_* .$$

Setting the gradient of the Lagrangian to zero gives \(\hat W C = VK^\top + \Lambda k_*^\top\), so \(\hat W = W + \Lambda (C^{-1}k_*)^\top\). Apply the constraint: \(W k_* + \Lambda\,(C^{-1}k_*)^\top k_* = v_*\), hence

$$\Lambda = \frac{v_* - W k_*}{(C^{-1}k_*)^\top k_*},\qquad \hat W = W + \Lambda\,(C^{-1}k_*)^\top .$$

A rank-one update. \(C\) is estimated from the layer's inputs on a sample of Wikipedia text; \(k_*\) is the MLP input at the last subject token, averaged over prompts with varied prefixes; \(v_*\) is found by optimization so the model outputs the new object, with a KL term that keeps the subject's other properties stable.

import numpy as np

def rome_update(W, C, k_star, v_star):
    """Rank-one edit: W_new @ k_star == v_star, other keys (covariance C) disturbed least."""
    u = np.linalg.solve(C, k_star)               # C^{-1} k*
    lam = (v_star - W @ k_star) / (u @ k_star)   # Lambda
    return W + np.outer(lam, u)                  # W + Lambda (C^{-1} k*)^T

Evidence

The authors introduce CounterFact, a dataset of counterfactual edits scored for efficacy (the edit holds), generalization (it holds under paraphrase) and specificity (neighbouring subjects are unchanged), plus fluency and consistency of generated text. On it and on zsRE they report that ROME balances generalization and specificity better than fine-tuning baselines and hypernetwork editors (KE, MEND), which tend to trade one for the other. Editing at layers outside the traced region works less well in their sweeps, which they present as agreement between localization and editing.

Limits & what broke
  • Localization does not predict the best edit layer. Hase et al. (NeurIPS 2023) found that where causal tracing localizes a fact is a poor predictor of which layer edits it best.
  • Edits do not propagate. An edited model may answer "Where is the Space Needle?" with the new city but fail on implications ("Which country is the Space Needle in?"). MQuAKE (Zhong et al., EMNLP 2023) and the ripple-effects benchmark of Cohen et al. (TACL 2024) document this.
  • The corruption choice matters. Gaussian noise on embeddings creates off-distribution inputs; later best-practice studies of activation patching (Zhang & Nanda, ICLR 2024) found results can change with the corruption method and metric.
  • One fact at a time. Sequential ROME edits degrade the model; MEMIT (Meng et al., ICLR 2023) spreads many edits over several layers.

Model editing as an alignment tool is promising and, for now, unreliable.

Probing: BERT Rediscovers the Classical NLP Pipeline + Control Tasks

35m videoLevel 3

Tenney, Das & Pavlick ACL 2019 (arXiv:1905.05950); Hewitt & Liang EMNLP 2019 (arXiv:1909.03368)

Training small classifiers on frozen representations shows which linguistic information can be decoded from which layer of BERT, and the result resembles the classical NLP pipeline; but a probe's accuracy is only meaningful relative to a control task that measures what the probe could learn on its own, and decodability is never evidence of use.

Cold open

Assign every word in the vocabulary a random part-of-speech tag, the same tag every time the word appears, and train an MLP probe on a pretrained encoder's representations (ELMo, in the paper) to predict these nonsense tags. It does well. The probe has learned word identities and memorized a lookup table, which tells you about the probe, not about BERT. Hewitt & Liang's control task turned that observation into a standard for the whole probing literature.

The problem

By 2019 pretrained encoders (ELMo, BERT) had replaced pipelines of separate taggers, parsers and coreference systems. The natural question: do they compute the same things internally, and where? Probing answers it cheaply: freeze the model, extract representations, train a small supervised classifier for a linguistic property, report accuracy. The weakness is in the inference. High accuracy could mean the representation encodes the property, or that the probe is powerful enough to compute it from whatever is there.

The method, part 1: edge probing across layers

Definition · edge probing

For a task defined over one or two spans (a token's part of speech, a constituent label, an entity type, a semantic role, whether two mentions corefer), pool the frozen representations of each span and feed them to a small classifier that predicts the label. The encoder is never trained. Tasks: POS, constituents, dependencies, entities, semantic role labelling, coreference, semantic proto-roles and relation classification.

Tenney, Das & Pavlick apply edge probing to every layer of BERT and summarize each task with two statistics:

  • Scalar mixing weights. The probe takes a learned softmax-weighted mix of all layers; the weights' center of gravity \(\sum_\ell \ell\, s_\ell\) says which layers it draws on.
  • Cumulative scoring. Train probes on layers \(0..\ell\) for each \(\ell\) and record the gain in F1 from adding layer \(\ell\); the expected layer \(\sum_\ell \ell\,\Delta_\ell / \sum_\ell \Delta_\ell\) says where new information becomes available.

The result, in the abstract's words: the steps appear in the expected sequence, POS tagging, parsing, NER, semantic roles, then coreference. The authors add that the model can revise lower-level decisions using higher-level information, and per-example analysis shows that the pipeline is not strict.

Softer than the headline. The differences in center of gravity between tasks are a few layers in a deep model, and the distributions of useful layers overlap heavily. "Syntax earlier, semantics later" is a tendency in where decodable information peaks, not a set of modules.

The method, part 2: control tasks

Definition · control task and selectivity

A control task keeps the input and output spaces of a linguistic task but makes the labels a function of word type alone. For POS, each word type is assigned one label sampled from the empirical tag distribution, and every occurrence gets that label. The only way to solve it is to memorize word identities.

$$\text{selectivity} = \text{accuracy on the linguistic task} - \text{accuracy on the control task}.$$

A good probe is one with high task accuracy and low control accuracy. High selectivity means the accuracy came from structure in the representation rather than from the probe's own capacity.

import numpy as np

def control_labels(word_types, true_labels, seed=0):
    """One random label per word type, drawn from the empirical label distribution.
    Build it once on the full dataset, then split, so train and test share the table."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    labels, counts = np.unique(true_labels, return_counts=True)
    table = {w: rng.choice(labels, p=counts / counts.sum()) for w in sorted(set(word_types))}
    return np.array([table[w] for w in word_types])

def selectivity(probe, X_tr, y_tr, c_tr, X_te, y_te, c_te):
    task = probe.fit(X_tr, y_tr).score(X_te, y_te)   # e.g. sklearn LogisticRegression
    ctrl = probe.fit(X_tr, c_tr).score(X_te, c_te)
    return task, ctrl, task - ctrl

Evidence

Hewitt & Liang run POS tagging and dependency-edge prediction on ELMo with probes of different families and capacities. They report that MLP probes reach high control accuracy, so much of their apparent advantage is memorization; that linear and low-rank probes are more selective at small cost in task accuracy; and that limiting the probe's hidden size or rank improves selectivity more reliably than dropout does. They also give an example where comparing layers by accuracy and by selectivity points in different directions, so a conclusion about "which layer encodes POS" can flip once the probe's capacity is controlled.

The broader implication: probing papers published before control tasks, which report accuracy alone, are hard to interpret, and their layer-wise claims especially so.

Limits & what broke
  • Decodability is not use. A probe shows that information can be extracted, never that the model relies on it. Amnesic probing (Elazar et al., TACL 2021) removes a property with iterative nullspace projection and checks whether the model's predictions change; causal methods such as tracing (Day 312) ask the same question with interventions.
  • Selectivity is one fix among several. Pimentel et al. (ACL 2020) argued from information theory that one should use the best possible probe; Voita & Titov (EMNLP 2020) proposed measuring description length instead of accuracy. The disagreement is about what question probing should answer.
  • Control tasks are task-specific. Designing one requires a notion of what a "memorize the word" baseline is for each task, which is easy for POS and harder for span or relation tasks.

Attention is not Explanation + Attention is not not Explanation

35m videoLevel 3

Jain & Wallace NAACL 2019 (arXiv:1902.10186); Wiegreffe & Pinter EMNLP 2019 (arXiv:1908.04626)

Jain & Wallace show that attention weights in recurrent text classifiers correlate weakly with other importance measures and can often be replaced by very different weights without changing the prediction; Wiegreffe & Pinter show that the experiments test something weaker than claimed. The disagreement is mostly about what "explanation" should mean.

Cold open

Two papers, one title apart. The first says attention is not explanation; the second, a few months later, answers that attention is not not explanation. Neither is simply right. Read together, they are the best short case study in this month of how to argue about an interpretability claim: state what the claim means, design the experiment that would refute it, and check whether the experiment actually tests that.

The problem

Attention layers produce a distribution over input tokens, and by 2019 papers routinely displayed it as a heatmap saying which words "the model focused on". The implicit claim was that attention weights explain predictions. Nobody had tested this directly. Jain & Wallace did, on BiLSTM encoders with additive attention across text classification, question answering and natural language inference.

The method: Jain & Wallace

Algorithm · two tests of attention
  1. Agreement. For each input, rank tokens by attention weight and by two other importance measures, gradients and leave-one-out output change. Report Kendall's \(\tau\) between the rankings.
  2. Counterfactual attention. Keep the encoder states fixed and swap in a different attention distribution \(\tilde\alpha\): either a random permutation of \(\alpha\), or an adversarial one found per instance by $$\max_{\tilde\alpha}\ \mathrm{JSD}(\tilde\alpha, \alpha)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad \mathrm{TVD}\big(\hat y(\tilde\alpha), \hat y(\alpha)\big) \le \epsilon .$$
  3. If explanations were faithful, rankings should agree, and very different attention should give different predictions.

They report that agreement with gradient and leave-one-out importance is weak and inconsistent for BiLSTM encoders (stronger for simple averaging encoders), that permuting attention often changes predictions little, and that for many instances adversarial distributions very different from the original leave the prediction essentially unchanged. Conclusion: attention weights should not be treated as explanations of predictions.

The response: Wiegreffe & Pinter

Wiegreffe & Pinter accept the measurements and challenge the inference, with four arguments:

  • Is attention even needed? On several of the classification datasets, replacing learned attention with uniform weights barely hurts accuracy. If the model does not need attention, its attention cannot be expected to explain anything, and those datasets cannot test the question.
  • Per-instance adversaries are not models. Jain & Wallace edit the distribution separately for each example, after training, with the rest of the network frozen. That shows that a distribution exists giving the same output, which is much weaker than showing the model is indifferent to its attention. Wiegreffe & Pinter instead train a whole model to produce attention that diverges from the original while matching its predictions.
  • Seed variance as a baseline. Attention from models trained with different random seeds already differs; an "adversarial" distribution must be judged against that natural variation.
  • A diagnostic. Freeze attention weights from a trained LSTM model and use them in a model with no contextual encoder (an MLP over token embeddings). Learned attention helps more than uniform weights, and attention from the adversarially trained model helps less, which suggests the original weights carry task-relevant information.

The headline: when a model is trained to use different attention, it can match predictions on some datasets, but the resulting weights are worse in the diagnostic, so attention is not arbitrary. Their conclusion is that attention can be an explanation; whether it is one depends on the definition and on the task.

The real disagreement: what "explanation" means

The second distinction is exclusive versus non-exclusive. Jain & Wallace's adversarial test assumes an explanation must be the explanation: if another distribution yields the same output, the original is discredited. Wiegreffe & Pinter argue that a prediction can have several valid explanations; the existence of an alternative does not show the original is not one of them. On an exclusive reading, Jain & Wallace's evidence is strong; on a non-exclusive one, it is weak. Neither paper is wrong about its measurements.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import kendalltau

def attention_vs_loo(predict, tokens, attn):
    """Kendall tau between attention and leave-one-out importance for one input.
    predict: list of tokens -> probability of the predicted class."""
    base = predict(tokens)
    loo = np.array([abs(base - predict(tokens[:i] + tokens[i + 1:]))
                    for i in range(len(tokens))])
    tau, _ = kendalltau(attn, loo)
    return tau
Limits & what broke
  • Architecture-specific. Both papers study single-layer attention over BiLSTM states, where the encoder has already mixed information across tokens, so a weight on token \(i\) is not a weight on word \(i\) alone. Multi-head, multi-layer Transformer attention is harder still: attention flow and rollout (Abnar & Zuidema, ACL 2020) tried to account for mixing across layers.
  • Agreement with gradients is not ground truth. Low correlation with gradient or leave-one-out importance could also mean those measures are wrong; Day 305 showed gradient-based maps have their own problems.
  • What to conclude in practice. Attention heatmaps remain useful as hypotheses about where to look and unusable as proof of why a model decided. A causal test, ablating or patching the attended tokens or the head (Day 308, Day 312), is what turns the hypothesis into evidence.

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis: Finding Sparse, Trainable Neural Networks

45m videoLevel 3

Frankle & Carbin, ICLR 2019 (arXiv:1803.03635)

A dense, randomly initialized network contains sparse subnetworks that, trained in isolation from their original initial weights, match the full network's accuracy; iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding to the initialization finds them, and the same masks with fresh random weights do not work.

Cold open

Train a network, delete 80 to 90 percent of its weights by magnitude, and set the survivors back to the exact values they had before training. Retrain this skeleton from scratch. It reaches the original accuracy, sometimes faster. Now keep the same skeleton but draw new random initial values: it trains worse. The structure alone is not enough. The particular initial values that came with it are part of the finding.

The problem

Pruning was well established: trained networks can lose most of their weights after training with little loss of accuracy (Han et al. 2015, part of the compression line in Day 332). But the common experience was that training a sparse architecture from scratch did worse than pruning a trained dense one. If small networks can represent the solution, why do we need large ones to find it? The paper offers a concrete answer and a procedure to test it.

The method

Definition · the lottery ticket hypothesis

Let \(f(x;\theta_0)\) be a dense network with random initialization \(\theta_0\), reaching test accuracy \(a\) after \(j\) iterations. The hypothesis: there is a mask \(m \in \{0,1\}^{|\theta|}\) with \(\|m\|_0 \ll |\theta|\) such that \(f(x; m \odot \theta_0)\), trained in isolation, reaches accuracy \(a' \ge a\) in \(j' \le j\) iterations. Such a subnetwork, mask plus original initial weights, is a winning ticket.

Algorithm · iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding
  1. Initialize \(\theta_0\) at random; set \(m = \mathbf 1\).
  2. Train \(f(x; m\odot\theta)\) from \(m\odot\theta_0\) for \(j\) iterations, giving \(\theta_j\).
  3. Prune: remove a fraction \(p\) of the surviving weights with the smallest \(|\theta_j|\) (layer by layer), updating \(m\).
  4. Rewind: reset the surviving weights to their values in \(\theta_0\).
  5. Repeat steps 2–4 for \(n\) rounds; after \(n\) rounds a fraction \((1-p)^n\) of weights remains (for \(p = 0.2\), five rounds leave about a third).
import copy
import torch

def find_ticket(model, train, rounds=5, prune_frac=0.2, rewind_state=None):
    """Iterative magnitude pruning with rewinding (layer-wise).
    train(model, masks) trains in place and keeps masked weights at zero."""
    init = copy.deepcopy(rewind_state or model.state_dict())     # theta_0, or theta_k
    masks = {n: torch.ones_like(p) for n, p in model.named_parameters() if p.dim() > 1}
    for _ in range(rounds):
        train(model, masks)
        for n, p in model.named_parameters():
            if n in masks:
                alive = p.detach().abs()[masks[n].bool()]
                cut = alive.kthvalue(max(1, int(prune_frac * alive.numel()))).values
                masks[n] = masks[n] * (p.detach().abs() > cut).float()
        model.load_state_dict(init)                                # rewind the survivors
        with torch.no_grad():
            for n, p in model.named_parameters():
                if n in masks:
                    p.mul_(masks[n])
    return masks

Evidence

The experiments use fully connected networks on MNIST (LeNet-300-100) and small convolutional networks, VGG-19 and ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10. In the authors' words, they consistently find winning tickets less than 10–20% of the size of the original networks; above that size, the tickets learn faster than the original and reach higher test accuracy. Iterative pruning finds smaller tickets than pruning everything in one shot.

The control comes first. When a winning ticket's mask is kept but its weights are randomly reinitialized, it learns more slowly and reaches lower accuracy as sparsity increases. This is what makes the paper a result rather than a pruning technique: mask and initialization are coupled. The particular \(\theta_0\) values that a weight started with, which made it large at the end of training, are what make the sparse network trainable.

Deeper networks needed help. For VGG-19 and ResNet-18, the procedure found winning tickets only with learning-rate warmup or a lower learning rate; at the standard higher rates it did not. The authors report this openly, and it is the first sign that the clean statement does not hold everywhere.

Limits & what broke
  • No training savings yet. Finding a ticket requires training the dense network, several times for iterative pruning. The paper says so; it is a statement about what exists, not a cheaper way to train.
  • Rewinding to iteration 0 fails at scale. Frankle, Dziugaite, Roy & Carbin (ICML 2020) found that for larger networks (ResNet-50 on ImageNet) tickets appear only if survivors are rewound to their values from early in training, iteration \(k > 0\), not to \(\theta_0\). They tie this to stability: tickets exist from the point at which two copies of the subnetwork, trained with different data orders, end in the same linearly connected region of the loss landscape. The original "from initialization" claim is qualified.
  • The reinit control is not universal. Liu et al. (ICLR 2019, "Rethinking the Value of Network Pruning") found random reinitialization works about as well in other settings, notably structured pruning and higher learning rates.
  • What it does not explain. The hypothesis suggests that overparameterization helps because a larger network holds more candidate subnetworks. That is a hypothesis, not a theorem; the related "strong" form, that sufficiently wide random networks contain good subnetworks without any training (Ramanujan et al., CVPR 2020; Malach et al., ICML 2020), is a different claim.

Deep Double Descent

45m videoLevel 3

Nakkiran, Kaplan, Bansal, Yang, Barak & Sutskever, ICLR 2020 (arXiv:1912.02292)

Test error in modern deep networks is not a U-shaped function of capacity: it peaks where the training procedure can only just fit the training set and then falls again, and this one shape shows up whether you grow the model, train longer, or (in a narrow regime) add data.

Cold open: the curve that breaks the textbook

Day 18 drew the bias–variance U: as capacity grows, bias falls, variance rises, and test error has a sweet spot in the middle. Day 17's VC bounds say the same thing in worst-case form. Day 49 then showed deep networks that can memorize random labels yet generalize on real ones, which no capacity-based bound explains. This paper is the payoff: it maps the region to the right of the U and shows the curve comes back down.

The problem

Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal (PNAS 2019) named "double descent" and showed it for random-feature models, kernels and small networks; physics-style analyses (Opper in the 1990s; Advani & Saxe; Spigler, Geiger and coauthors on a "jamming" transition) had seen related peaks. Two questions stayed open: does it happen in realistic deep networks trained the usual way, and is model size even the right axis?

The method: three axes and one quantity

The paper is empirical. It trains ResNet18 and simpler CNNs on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (varying a width multiplier), and Transformers on IWSLT'14 and WMT'14 translation (varying the embedding dimension), often with a fraction of training labels randomized. It then plots test error along three axes.

  • Model-wise. Fix data and training time, sweep width. Error rises to a peak around the width at which the model first reaches near-zero training error, then falls.
  • Epoch-wise. Fix a large model and plot test error against training epochs. Error falls, rises, then falls again. Small models show the classical single dip; medium models can end up stuck near the peak.
  • Sample-wise. Fix the model, vary the training-set size. More data moves the interpolation threshold to the right, because a bigger model is needed to fit it. A model that sat comfortably past the peak can land on it, and test error gets worse. The abstract reports regimes where even quadrupling the training set hurts.
Definition · effective model complexity

For a training procedure \(\mathcal T\) (architecture, optimizer, epochs), distribution \(\mathcal D\) and tolerance \(\varepsilon\),

$$\mathrm{EMC}_{\mathcal D,\varepsilon}(\mathcal T) = \max\big\{\, n \;:\; \E_{S\sim \mathcal D^n}\big[\mathrm{TrainError}(\mathcal T(S))\big] \le \varepsilon \,\big\}.$$

It is the largest training set the procedure can still fit almost perfectly. Wider models and longer training both raise it.

Hypothesis · generalized double descent

With \(n\) training samples: if \(\mathrm{EMC}\) is well below \(n\) (under-parameterized) or well above \(n\) (over-parameterized), increasing it lowers test error. In a critical band around \(\mathrm{EMC} \approx n\), increasing it may raise or lower test error.

The authors state this informally: "well below" and "well above" are not quantified, and it is a conjecture organizing the experiments, not a theorem. Its value: width, epochs and sample size each just move \(\mathrm{EMC}\) relative to \(n\).

Derivation · why the peak sits at the threshold

The cleanest case is linear. Fit \(p\) features to \(n\) points by minimum-norm least squares, \(\hat\beta = X^{+}y\). For isotropic Gaussian features, true signal energy \(r^2\), noise variance \(\sigma^2\) and \(\gamma = p/n\) held fixed as both grow, Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani (Ann. Stat. 2022) give the limiting excess risk

$$R(\gamma) = \begin{cases} \sigma^2\,\dfrac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}, & \gamma < 1,\\[2mm] r^2\Big(1-\dfrac1\gamma\Big) + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\gamma - 1}, & \gamma > 1.\end{cases}$$

Both branches diverge as \(\gamma \to 1\). At \(p = n\) the design matrix is square and its smallest singular value goes to zero, so there is essentially one fit, and matching the noise exactly requires huge coefficients. Past the threshold many interpolators exist and the minimum-norm one spreads the noise across spare directions, so the variance term \(\sigma^2/(\gamma-1)\) shrinks again. Noise drives the peak: with \(\sigma = 0\) the left branch is zero.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, d = 100, 20
w_true = rng.normal(size=d)
W = rng.normal(size=(d, 400)) / np.sqrt(d)        # fixed random first layer

def data(m):
    X = rng.normal(size=(m, d))
    return X, X @ w_true + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=m)  # noisy labels

def feats(X, p):
    return np.maximum(X @ W[:, :p], 0.0)           # p random ReLU features

Xtr, ytr = data(n)
Xte, yte = data(2000)
for p in [10, 50, 90, 100, 110, 200, 400]:
    beta = np.linalg.pinv(feats(Xtr, p)) @ ytr      # minimum-norm fit
    print(p, round(float(np.mean((feats(Xte, p) @ beta - yte) ** 2)), 2))

Evidence

The figures that matter are the model-size by epochs heatmaps for ResNet18 on CIFAR-10: read a row and you see model-wise double descent, read a column and you see epoch-wise double descent, and the peak traces the boundary where training error first hits zero. The Transformer translation experiments supply the sample-wise result, where test loss for some model sizes is worse with more data. Label noise is the amplifier: with randomized labels the peak is large; on clean data it is much smaller and sometimes hard to see. That fits the linear picture, where \(\sigma^2\) multiplies the divergent terms.

Two practical consequences. First, early stopping can land you on the peak: a rule like "stop when validation error starts rising" fires during the epoch-wise bump, and a fixed epoch budget can end training at its top, for exactly the medium-sized models people often train. Second, the paper notes that optimal early stopping or tuned regularization often shrinks or removes the peak, so double descent is partly a symptom of fixed, untuned training recipes.

What the evidence does not show: why SGD's implicit bias selects good interpolators in deep networks, or a quantitative EMC you can compute before training. EMC is measured after the fact.

Limits & what broke
  • Explanation status. The implicit-bias story (past the threshold, SGD picks a low-norm or otherwise simple interpolator) is proven for linear and random-feature models and only heuristic for deep nets.
  • It can be tuned away. Nakkiran, Venkat, Kakade and Ma (ICLR 2021) show that optimally tuned ridge regularization provably removes the peak in some linear settings, and observe the same for some networks. The peak is real, but not inevitable.
  • Not a law of scale. In large-scale LLM pretraining, data is seen roughly once and models rarely interpolate, so scaling laws (Day 146, Day 147) are the better lens there.
  • More-data-hurts is narrow. It holds only near the critical band, for a fixed model. Grow the model with the data and it disappears.

Bridge

Double descent closes the loop from Day 18 and Day 49: interpolation is not the end of generalization. Tomorrow's paper (Day 317) pushes the epoch axis to an extreme, where a model sits at perfect training accuracy for a long time and then generalizes suddenly, and interpretability tools say what changed inside. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 18 (bias/variance), ⟵ Day 17 (VC dimension).

Grokking: Generalization Beyond Overfitting on Small Algorithmic Datasets

40m videoLevel 4

Power, Burda, Edwards, Babuschkin & Misra, 2022 (arXiv:2201.02177)

On small algorithmic tasks a transformer can reach perfect training accuracy, sit there for orders of magnitude more steps, and then suddenly generalize; later interpretability work showed the generalizing algorithm was forming gradually all along, with weight decay doing the final cleanup.

Cold open

The paper's headline plot has training accuracy at 100% early, validation accuracy near chance, and then, long after any sensible early-stopping rule would have fired, validation accuracy climbs to near 100%. The authors call this grokking. Yesterday's epoch-wise double descent (Day 316) was a bump; this is a cliff after a long plateau.

The problem

Conventional wisdom: once a model has memorized the training set and validation loss has stopped improving, more training only overfits. Day 49 (Zhang et al.) showed networks can memorize anything; nobody had a clean setting in which to watch memorization turn into generalization. The paper supplies one small enough to study exhaustively.

The method: tiny tasks, tiny transformers

Each dataset is the full table of a binary operation \(a \circ b = c\) over a small set: modular addition, subtraction and division mod a prime, some polynomial expressions, and composition in the permutation group \(S_5\). Each equation is a token sequence like a ∘ b = c. A random fraction of the table is the training set; the rest is the test set, so generalization means filling in unseen table entries. The model is a small decoder-only transformer trained with AdamW.

Evidence in the original paper

  • Delay grows as data shrinks. The steps needed to reach high validation accuracy grow steeply as the training fraction falls; below some fraction, no generalization occurs within the budget.
  • Weight decay helps most. Of the interventions compared (dropout, gradient noise, weight decay variants), weight decay was the most effective at reducing the data needed to generalize.
  • Structure in embeddings. Visualized output-layer embeddings show the task's structure, for example a circular arrangement for modular addition.

The paper is phenomenological: it documents the effect and its dependencies but does not say what the network computes.

The mechanistic explanation (Nanda et al. 2023)

Nanda, Chan, Lieberum, Smith and Steinhardt (ICLR 2023) trained a one-layer transformer on addition mod \(p = 113\) with weight decay and fully reverse-engineered it. The network implements a discrete Fourier algorithm.

Derivation · addition by rotation

Pick a few key frequencies \(w_k = 2\pi k/p\). The embedding maps \(a\) to \(\cos w_k a, \sin w_k a\) (and likewise \(b\)). Attention and the MLP form products, and the angle-addition identities give

$$\cos w_k(a{+}b) = \cos w_k a\cos w_k b - \sin w_k a \sin w_k b,\quad \sin w_k(a{+}b) = \sin w_k a\cos w_k b + \cos w_k a\sin w_k b.$$

The unembedding scores each candidate answer \(c\) by

$$\text{logit}(c) \propto \sum_k \cos w_k(a{+}b{-}c) = \sum_k \big[\cos w_k(a{+}b)\cos w_k c + \sin w_k(a{+}b)\sin w_k c\big].$$

Each term equals 1 exactly when \(k(a+b-c) \equiv 0 \pmod p\). Since \(p\) is prime and \(k \ne 0\), that means \(c \equiv a+b\); for every other \(c\) each term is below 1. The frequencies interfere constructively only at the right answer.

import math

p, freqs = 113, [3, 17, 40]                       # any few nonzero frequencies

def logits(a, b):
    out = []
    for c in range(p):
        s = 0.0
        for k in freqs:
            w = 2 * math.pi * k / p
            cab = math.cos(w * a) * math.cos(w * b) - math.sin(w * a) * math.sin(w * b)
            sab = math.sin(w * a) * math.cos(w * b) + math.cos(w * a) * math.sin(w * b)
            s += cab * math.cos(w * c) + sab * math.sin(w * c)   # cos(w(a+b-c))
        out.append(s)
    return out

for a, b in [(5, 7), (100, 50), (112, 112)]:
    L = logits(a, b)
    assert max(range(p), key=L.__getitem__) == (a + b) % p

Knowing the algorithm lets them define progress measures: the restricted loss (ablate everything except the key frequencies) and the excluded loss (ablate only the key frequencies). These reveal three phases:

  1. Memorization. Training loss falls quickly; test loss stays bad.
  2. Circuit formation. The Fourier circuit grows inside the network while test accuracy is flat; the excluded loss rises, showing the network increasingly relies on the generalizing mechanism to fit the training set.
  3. Cleanup. Weight decay removes the now-redundant memorization components. Only then does test loss drop sharply.

This is the key result. Generalization was continuous underneath a discontinuous metric, the same argument as the "mirage" paper on emergent abilities (Day 206), here in training time rather than model scale. Weight decay drives the last phase: a memorizing solution and a compact generalizing one both fit the data, and the penalty on weight norm favours the compact one.

Limits & what broke
  • Tiny, algorithmic, exhaustively enumerable. The tasks have a perfect compact rule and a few thousand examples. Do not read grokking as evidence that frontier models will suddenly "get" something after long plateaus.
  • Hyperparameter-sensitive. The delay depends strongly on weight decay, learning rate, data fraction and initialization scale. Liu, Michaud and Tegmark (Omnigrok, ICLR 2023) tie it to weight norm and induce grokking on MNIST with large initializations; with the right settings the delay largely disappears.
  • Competing accounts. Varma et al. (2023) frame it as circuit efficiency, a memorizing circuit versus a cheaper generalizing one. Kumar et al. (2024) frame it as a transition from lazy to rich training, which connects to tomorrow's paper. These are compatible pictures, not a settled theory.
  • Interpretability had it easy. The Fourier circuit was found because the task has a known clean algorithm; most real circuits do not.

Bridge

Grokking is the best available case of interpretability answering a training-dynamics question: why generalization arrived late, not just that it did. The lazy-to-rich framing points directly to Day 318, the Neural Tangent Kernel, the regime in which networks provably do not learn features, and so could never grok. Neural Tangent Kernel

50m videoLevel 4

Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler, NeurIPS 2018 (arXiv:1806.07572)

In the infinite-width limit with the right scaling, a neural network's tangent kernel is deterministic at initialization and stays constant during training, so gradient descent on the network is exactly kernel regression with that kernel.

Cold open: deep learning as a kernel method

Non-convex, millions of parameters, no guarantees: that was the standard description of training a network. This paper found a limit in which all of that collapses to linear algebra, with a closed-form solution you can write on one line. The price, as we will see, is that the limit removes the thing deep learning is best at.

The problem

Two results were already known. A wide random network at initialization is a Gaussian process (Neal 1996; Lee et al. and Matthews et al. 2018), the GP of Day 30. And kernel machines (Day 15) have a clean theory. But the GP view describes only the untrained network, a Bayesian prior. Nobody could say what gradient descent does to the function.

The method

Definition · neural tangent kernel

For a network \(f(x;\theta)\) with parameters \(\theta \in \R^P\),

$$\Theta_\theta(x, x') = \big\langle \nabla_\theta f(x;\theta),\, \nabla_\theta f(x';\theta)\big\rangle = \sum_{p=1}^{P} \frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial\theta_p}\,\frac{\partial f(x')}{\partial\theta_p}.$$

It measures how much a gradient step taken to fit \(x'\) moves the output at \(x\).

Derivation · gradient flow in function space

Squared loss on training inputs \(X = (x_1,\dots,x_N)\) with targets \(Y\): \(L = \tfrac12\sum_i (f(x_i) - y_i)^2\). Gradient flow is \(\dot\theta = -\eta\,\nabla_\theta L = -\eta\sum_i \nabla_\theta f(x_i)\,(f(x_i) - y_i)\). The chain rule gives, for any input \(x\),

$$\frac{d f_t(x)}{dt} = \nabla_\theta f(x)^{\top}\dot\theta = -\eta \sum_{i} \Theta_t(x, x_i)\,\big(f_t(x_i) - y_i\big).$$

This is exact for any network. The parameters have disappeared; only the kernel remains. If \(\Theta_t\) were a fixed matrix, this would be a linear ODE.

Theorem · Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler

Use the NTK parameterization (each layer's pre-activation scaled by \(1/\sqrt{\text{fan-in}}\), weights initialized as standard Gaussians) and let the hidden widths go to infinity one layer after another. Then (i) at initialization \(\Theta_0\) converges in probability to a deterministic kernel \(\Theta_\infty\) that depends only on depth, nonlinearity and initialization variances, and (ii) during gradient-flow training over any fixed time horizon, \(\Theta_t\) stays equal to \(\Theta_\infty\).

They also show \(\Theta_\infty\) is positive definite for inputs on the unit sphere under mild conditions on the nonlinearity, which guarantees convergence to zero training loss.

Part (ii) is the whole theorem: constancy makes the dynamics linear. Here is why it holds, for a two-layer net \(f(x) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt m}\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\,\sigma(w_j^\top x)\). The kernel is an average of \(m\) per-neuron terms, so by the law of large numbers it is deterministic as \(m\to\infty\). Each neuron's gradient is \(O(1/\sqrt m)\), so changing \(f\) by \(O(1)\) moves each weight only \(O(1/\sqrt m)\), and the kernel changes by \(O(1/\sqrt m)\) as well. Every parameter barely moves; their combined effect on \(f\) is still order one.

Derivation · the closed-form solution

With \(\Theta = \Theta_\infty(X,X)\) constant, the residual on the training set obeys \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(f_t(X) - Y) = -\eta\,\Theta\,(f_t(X)-Y)\), so \(f_t(X) = Y + e^{-\eta\Theta t}(f_0(X) - Y)\). Substituting into the ODE at a test point and integrating,

$$f_t(x) = f_0(x) + \Theta_\infty(x, X)\,\Theta^{-1}\big(I - e^{-\eta\Theta t}\big)\big(Y - f_0(X)\big).$$

As \(t \to \infty\) this is kernel ("ridgeless") regression with kernel \(\Theta_\infty\), added to the random initial function \(f_0\), which is itself a GP. Each eigendirection of \(\Theta\) is fitted at rate \(\eta\lambda_k\): large-eigenvalue (smooth) components are learned first, which is a form of spectral bias.

Evidence

The paper's experiments are small and confirmatory: as width grows, empirical tangent kernels of independently initialized networks agree more closely and move less during training, and wide networks follow the kernel prediction. The theory's real test came later. Arora et al. (NeurIPS 2019) computed exact convolutional NTKs for CIFAR-10 and reported accuracy far above earlier kernel methods but clearly below ordinary trained CNNs. Lee et al. (2020) compared finite and infinite networks systematically and found that well-tuned finite networks, especially with standard parameterization and large learning rates, often beat their kernel limits. That consistent gap is the interesting object: it measures how much of deep learning's success comes from moving the features.

Limits & what broke
  • No feature learning. In the NTK regime the features \(\nabla_\theta f_0(x)\) are frozen. Feature learning is exactly what the limit excludes, so it cannot explain transfer learning, pretrained representations or anything like the grokking circuit of Day 317.
  • Lazy training. Chizat, Oyallon and Bach (NeurIPS 2019) showed the kernel regime is caused by scaling, not width itself: any model scaled so outputs move a lot while parameters move little behaves like its linearization. They found such lazy models underperform on real vision tasks.
  • The other infinite-width limit. The mean-field limit (Mei, Montanari and Nguyen 2018; Chizat and Bach 2018), with a \(1/m\) output scaling, keeps feature learning for two-layer nets. Which limit you get depends on the parameterization.
  • Finite-width kernels move. Empirically the tangent kernel changes rapidly early in training and then stabilizes (Fort et al. 2020), so "constant kernel" fails exactly when the important learning happens.
The modern descendant

Yang and Hu (Tensor Programs IV, ICML 2021) classified parameterizations by how initialization and learning rate scale with width and identified the maximal update parameterization (µP), in which every layer's features keep moving at the infinite-width limit. Its practical payoff, µTransfer (Yang et al. 2022), is that optimal learning rates and initializations stay roughly fixed as width grows, so you can tune a small model and transfer its hyperparameters to a large one.

Bridge

The NTK is the cleanest theory of training we have, and it works by assuming away representation learning; the gap between a network and its kernel is where the interesting behaviour lives. Day 319 turns to a different, more geometric attempt to explain generalization: the shape of the minimum that training finds. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 15 (kernels), ⟵ Day 30 (Gaussian processes).

On Large-Batch Training and Flat Minima

40m videoLevel 3

Keskar, Mudigere, Nocedal, Smelyanskiy & Tang, ICLR 2017 (arXiv:1609.04836)

Large-batch SGD reaches the same training loss as small-batch SGD but generalizes worse, and the paper attributes the gap to large batches converging to sharp minima while gradient noise steers small batches into flat ones; the intuition survived, its first formalization did not.

Cold open

Data parallelism promises near-linear speedups: put more examples in each batch, spread them over more GPUs, take fewer steps. In 2016 that promise broke on a generalization gap. Large-batch runs fitted the training set just as well and tested noticeably worse. This lesson tells the story in three acts: the original claim, the rebuttal, and the practical resolution.

The problem

Practitioners had seen the gap but had no account of it. The paper sets up six network and dataset combinations (fully connected and convolutional networks on MNIST, TIMIT, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100) and compares a small batch of a few hundred examples with a large batch of around a tenth of the dataset, with Adam as the optimizer. The large-batch runs reach comparable training accuracy and consistently lower test accuracy. The authors also rule out the obvious explanations: it is not that the large-batch runs fail to optimize, and it is not simple overfitting in the usual sense.

The method: sharpness, defined and measured

Definition · ε-sharpness

For loss \(f\), a minimizer \(x \in \R^n\) and box \(\mathcal C_\varepsilon = \{z : |z_i| \le \varepsilon(|x_i| + 1)\}\),

$$\phi_{x,f}(\varepsilon) = \frac{\max_{y \in \mathcal C_\varepsilon} f(x + y) - f(x)}{1 + f(x)} \times 100.$$

It is the largest relative rise in loss within a small box around the solution. The authors approximate the inner maximization with a few iterations of L-BFGS-B, and also report a version restricted to a random low-dimensional subspace. The box scales with \(|x_i|\) so that large weights get proportionally larger perturbations, an early attempt at scale-awareness that turned out not to be enough.

The second instrument is the parametric plot, following Goodfellow et al. (2015): evaluate training and test loss along the line \(f(\alpha x_{\text{LB}} + (1-\alpha)x_{\text{SB}})\) for \(\alpha\) from about \(-1\) to \(2\). Around the large-batch solution the loss rises steeply; around the small-batch solution it rises slowly. This is the figure everyone reproduces. Both measures agree, and the large-batch sharpness values are consistently higher.

Derivation · the gradient-noise argument

A minibatch gradient of size \(B\) is \(g_B = \nabla f(x) + \xi\) with \(\E\xi = 0\) and \(\Cov(\xi) \approx \Sigma/B\), where \(\Sigma\) is the per-example gradient covariance (the stochastic approximation setting of Day 34). The SGD step \(x \leftarrow x - \eta g_B\) then has injected noise with covariance \(\eta^2\Sigma/B\). Treating many small steps as a stochastic differential equation (Mandt et al. 2017; Smith & Le 2018; Jastrzębski et al. 2017),

$$dx = -\nabla f(x)\,dt + \sqrt{\eta/B}\;\Sigma^{1/2}\,dW_t .$$

The ratio \(\eta/B\) acts as a temperature. A hot process escapes narrow basins, whose walls it can climb over quickly, and settles in wide ones. Raising \(B\) at fixed \(\eta\) cools it, so it settles wherever it lands. The same equation predicts the fix: scale \(\eta\) with \(B\) to keep the temperature constant.

Keskar et al. tried several remedies (data augmentation, conservative training, adversarial-style robust training, and warm-starting large-batch training from a few small-batch epochs). Some narrowed the gap; none closed it. They left the problem open.

The rebuttal: sharpness is not well defined

Proof · Dinh et al. rescaling

Take a ReLU layer pair \(f(x) = W_2\,\mathrm{relu}(W_1 x)\). Because \(\mathrm{relu}(\alpha z) = \alpha\,\mathrm{relu}(z)\) for \(\alpha > 0\), the map \(T_\alpha(W_1, W_2) = (\alpha W_1, W_2/\alpha)\) leaves the function, and so every training and test loss, unchanged. The loss satisfies \(L \circ T_\alpha = L\), so the Hessian at the rescaled point is \(J^{-\top} H J^{-1}\) with \(J = \operatorname{diag}(\alpha I, \alpha^{-1} I)\): the \(W_1\) block is multiplied by \(1/\alpha^2\) and the \(W_2\) block by \(\alpha^2\). Taking \(\alpha \to 0\) or \(\alpha\to\infty\) makes the largest curvature arbitrarily big. The same rescaling defeats ε-sharpness.

Dinh, Pascanu, Bengio and Bengio (ICML 2017) concluded that any minimum can be made as sharp as you like without changing what the network computes, so sharpness as defined cannot be what determines generalization. Hoffer, Hubara and Soudry (NeurIPS 2017) offered a different diagnosis: large-batch runs simply take far fewer updates, and adjusting the learning rate and training longer shrinks the gap.

Evidence: what survived

  • Linear scaling plus warmup. Goyal et al. (2017) trained ResNet-50 on ImageNet with minibatches of 8k images, matching small-batch accuracy, by scaling the learning rate linearly with batch size and ramping it up over the first few epochs. This is the SDE temperature argument made practical.
  • Layer-wise rates. LARS (You, Gitman & Ginsburg 2017) and LAMB (You et al., ICLR 2020) normalize each layer's step by its weight norm, pushing workable batch sizes much further, including for BERT pretraining.
  • Limits to scaling. McCandlish et al. (2018) and Shallue et al. (2019) found a critical batch size, tied to the gradient noise scale, beyond which more parallelism stops reducing the number of steps.
  • SAM. Foret et al. (ICLR 2021) optimize \(\min_w \max_{\|\epsilon\| \le \rho} L(w + \epsilon)\), approximating the inner step by \(\hat\epsilon = \rho\,\nabla L/\|\nabla L\|\) and taking the gradient at \(w + \hat\epsilon\). It reliably improves generalization across vision benchmarks. The intuition was not wrong, just under-specified.
Limits & what broke
  • The metric, not the idea. Raw Hessian or box-based sharpness is not reparameterization-invariant. Scale-aware variants (adaptive SAM, Kwon et al. 2021; normalized measures in Jiang et al.'s 2020 study of generalization measures) partly repair this.
  • Correlation is shaky. Andriushchenko et al. (2023) found that in modern settings, such as transformers and fine-tuning, sharpness often correlates weakly or even negatively with generalization. SAM may help for reasons other than finding flat minima.
  • Untuned baselines. Much of the original gap reflected large-batch runs that kept small-batch learning rates and epoch budgets. With tuned schedules most of it disappears, which is why few people worry about it now.

Bridge

This is a case where an intuition outlived its own formalization: flatness matters in some form, but the 2017 definition could not carry the weight. The Information Bottleneck and Deep Learning

45m videoLevel 4

Tishby & Zaslavsky 2015 (arXiv:1503.02406); Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby 2017; Saxe et al. ICLR 2018

The information-bottleneck theory claimed that SGD first fits the labels and then compresses away information about the input, and that compression drives generalization; the careful refutation showed the observed compression came from saturating nonlinearities and from how mutual information was estimated, while the bottleneck survives as a training objective.

Cold open: an ambitious theory and a careful refutation

In 2017 a set of animated "information plane" plots went viral: every layer of a network traced the same two-act path, first up, then left. It looked like a universal law of deep learning. A year later an ICLR paper showed the second act disappears when you swap tanh for ReLU. This lesson is about how both things can be true.

The problem and the principle

Shannon's mutual information (Day 2) gives a language for "keep what matters, discard the rest." Tishby, Pereira and Bialek (1999) turned that into an optimization: for a Markov chain \(Y \to X \to T\), find a stochastic encoding \(p(t\mid x)\) solving

$$\min_{p(t\mid x)}\; I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y).$$

The first term rewards compression, the second rewards predictiveness, and \(\beta\) trades them off. Tishby and Zaslavsky (2015) proposed reading a deep network this way: each hidden layer \(T_\ell\) is a representation in the chain \(Y \to X \to T_1 \to \dots \to T_L\), so by data processing \(I(X;T_1) \ge I(X;T_2) \ge \dots\), and each layer can be placed as a point on the information plane with coordinates \((I(X;T), I(T;Y))\). They argued that good networks approach the optimal IB tradeoff curve, and linked this to generalization bounds.

The method: the two-phase claim

Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby (2017) trained small fully connected tanh networks on a synthetic binary task with a few thousand input patterns, estimated both mutual informations by discretizing each layer's activations into a fixed number of bins, and tracked every layer across training.

The reported picture had two phases. In a short fitting phase both \(I(X;T)\) and \(I(T;Y)\) rise. In a long compression phase \(I(X;T)\) falls while \(I(T;Y)\) stays high. The authors tied the switch to a change in SGD's gradient statistics, from a high-mean "drift" phase to a noise-dominated "diffusion" phase, and argued that this diffusion performs compression, that compression causes generalization, and that depth helps because it speeds compression.

Evidence: the rebuttal

Saxe, Bansal, Dapello, Advani, Kolchinsky, Tracey and Cox (ICLR 2018) tested each link.

  • Compression depends on the nonlinearity. With the same binning estimator, double-sided saturating nonlinearities such as tanh compress; ReLU networks generally do not. Saturation pushes activations into the end bins, which looks like lost information.
  • No causal link to generalization. Networks that do not compress still generalize, and in deep linear networks, where information quantities can be computed exactly, generalization and compression come apart.
  • Not specific to SGD noise. Compression also appears under full-batch gradient descent, so diffusion is not required.
Derivation · why the estimator decides the result

A trained network is deterministic: \(T = f(X)\). If \(X\) is discrete (a finite dataset), then \(H(T\mid X) = 0\) and

$$I(X;T) = H(T) - H(T\mid X) = H(T).$$

If \(f\) is injective on the dataset, which is generic for continuous weights, \(H(T) = H(X) = \log N\): a constant, so nothing can compress. If \(X\) is continuous and \(f\) is injective, \(I(X;T)\) is infinite. Either way the true quantity carries no information about training. What the plots measure is \(I(X; \mathrm{bin}(T)) = H(\mathrm{bin}(T))\), the entropy of bin occupancy: a measure of how spread out or clustered the activations are at that bin width. Different discretizations give different information planes. That is the technical heart of the disagreement.

Later work made this precise. Goldfeld et al. (ICML 2019) added small Gaussian noise to each layer so the mutual information is finite and well defined, and found that the "compression" that remains tracks the clustering of representations, which binning was roughly picking up.

Limits & what broke
  • The descriptive claim did not replicate beyond saturating nonlinearities and binned estimators.
  • The quantity is ill-posed for deterministic networks. Any information-plane result is a statement about an estimator, which must be justified separately.
  • The causal claim failed. Compression was neither necessary nor sufficient for generalization in Saxe et al.'s experiments. Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby's experiments were on small synthetic tasks.
  • The exchange was unresolved at the time. The original authors disputed the rebuttal at ICLR; the community's reading since then follows Saxe et al. and the estimator critiques.

What remains useful: the bottleneck as an objective

Derivation · variational information bottleneck

Alemi, Fischer, Dillon and Murphy (ICLR 2017) make the encoder stochastic, \(p(z\mid x)\), which makes both terms finite, and bound them. For any decoder \(q(y\mid z)\), non-negativity of KL gives \(I(Z;Y) \ge \E[\log q(y\mid z)] + H(Y)\). For any marginal \(r(z)\), \(I(Z;X) \le \E_x\,\KL\big(p(z\mid x)\,\|\,r(z)\big)\). Combining them, minimize

$$\E_{x,y}\,\E_{p(z\mid x)}\big[-\log q(y\mid z)\big] + \beta'\,\E_x\,\KL\big(p(z\mid x)\,\|\,r(z)\big),$$

with \(\beta' = 1/\beta\) in the notation above. This is the β-VAE loss of Day 242 with the reconstruction term replaced by label prediction, trainable with the reparameterization trick.

VIB was reported to improve robustness to some adversarial attacks and to overfitting on small data. The IB principle also underlies rate–distortion views of representation learning. So the objective survives even though the story about what SGD does automatically did not.

Intriguing Properties of Neural Networks

40m videoLevel 2

Szegedy, Zaremba, Sutskever, Bruna, Erhan, Goodfellow & Fergus, ICLR 2014 (arXiv:1312.6199)

Two findings in one short paper: individual neurons are no more semantically meaningful than random directions in activation space, and an optimized, visually imperceptible perturbation can make a high-accuracy network misclassify almost any input, with the same perturbation often fooling other networks too.

Cold open: an imperceptible change that flips the label

The paper's best-known figure shows triples: a correctly classified ImageNet photo, a faint perturbation magnified for display, and their sum, which looks identical to the original and is classified as "ostrich". The networks were the state of the art of 2013, near-human on the benchmark, and they could be broken by noise a person cannot see.

The problem

By 2013 deep networks generalized well on held-out test sets, and two assumptions were common. First, that individual units learn interpretable features, so inspecting neurons is the way to understand a network. Second, that a model that generalizes is smooth: small input changes produce small output changes, at least near the data. The paper tests both and finds both wanting.

The first property: neurons are not special

Take a layer's activation \(\phi(x)\). The standard inspection method finds held-out images that maximize one unit, \(x' = \arg\max_x \langle \phi(x), e_i\rangle\), and reads off what they share. The authors do the same for a random unit vector \(v\), \(\arg\max_x \langle\phi(x), v\rangle\), on MNIST and on an ImageNet network. The top images for random directions look just as semantically coherent as those for basis directions.

The conclusion is that semantic information lives in the space of activations, not in the coordinate axes. This is the half of the paper almost everyone forgets, and it is the ancestor of the features-as-directions view: superposition (Day 309) explains why a network would not align features with neurons, and sparse autoencoders are the tool built to find the right directions.

The method: finding adversarial examples

Definition · minimal adversarial perturbation

For classifier \(f : [0,1]^m \to \{1,\dots,k\}\), input \(x\) and target label \(l \ne f(x)\), solve

$$\min_r \|r\|_2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad f(x + r) = l,\quad x + r \in [0,1]^m.$$
Algorithm · box-constrained L-BFGS
  1. Replace the hard label constraint with a penalty: minimize \(c\,\|r\| + \mathrm{loss}_f(x + r, l)\) over \(r\) with \(x + r \in [0,1]^m\), using L-BFGS with box constraints.
  2. Line-search over \(c > 0\) for the smallest perturbation whose minimizer actually achieves \(f(x + r) = l\).
  3. Report \(x + r\) and its distortion (they use root-mean-square pixel change).

For convex losses this penalty method would give the exact minimum; for networks it gives an approximation, which is enough, since any successful \(r\) is an upper bound on the true minimum.

Evidence

  • Ubiquity. The authors report finding adversarial examples for every network and essentially every input they tried, on MNIST models, ImageNet models and a large network trained on YouTube frames, with distortions far below what changes a human's judgment. Random Gaussian noise, they report, is far less damaging at comparable distortion.
  • Cross-model transfer. Adversarial examples made for one model are misclassified at a high rate by models with different architectures, hyperparameters and regularization.
  • Cross-training-set transfer. They also transfer to models trained on disjoint subsets of MNIST. So the vulnerability is not a quirk of one set of weights; it is shared by models that learned the task in similar ways.
  • An instability bound. Each layer is Lipschitz with constant at most its operator norm (ReLU and max-pooling are 1-Lipschitz), so \(\|\phi(x+r)-\phi(x)\| \le \prod_k \|W_k\|\,\|r\|\). The computed per-layer norms are large enough to permit instability. This is an upper bound and cannot prove instability, but it points at the right culprit: the linear layers.
  • A hint of adversarial training. Feeding adversarial examples back into training gave a small improvement on MNIST, an idea developed in the next two lessons.

Transferability is the finding that turned a curiosity into a threat model. If adversarial examples transfer, an attacker does not need the target's weights: train a substitute model, attack it, and send the result. Papernot et al. (2017) made this a practical black-box attack against deployed classifiers.

The authors' explanation was geometric: adversarial examples are "low-probability pockets" in input space, rare enough never to appear in test data yet dense, like the rationals among the reals. The two facts reconcile through dimension: under random sampling the crossing directions have negligible measure; under optimization they are always there.

Limits & what broke
  • The explanation was wrong. The pockets framing implies adversarial examples are an exotic consequence of high nonlinearity. Goodfellow et al. (Day 322) showed the opposite: networks are too linear, and a one-step attack along the gradient's sign finds them.
  • The attack is slow (an inner L-BFGS per example per penalty value), which limited its use for training. FGSM and PGD replaced it.
  • Threat model unstated. "Imperceptible" was judged by eye and measured in \(\ell_2\); later work had to define perturbation sets, attacker knowledge and evaluation protocols carefully (Day 323).
  • Bugs or features? Ilyas et al. (NeurIPS 2019) argue that adversarial directions are predictive but non-robust features of the data, which also explains transfer: different models learn the same useful-but-brittle features.

Bridge

The paper raised two questions and answered neither: why such perturbations exist, and why they transfer. Day 322 answers both with two lines of algebra about linearity in high dimensions, and turns the expensive attack into a single gradient step. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 309 (features as directions, not neurons).

Explaining and Harnessing Adversarial Examples (FGSM)

35m videoLevel 2

Goodfellow, Shlens & Szegedy, ICLR 2015 (arXiv:1412.6572)

Adversarial examples exist because networks are too linear, not too nonlinear: in high dimensions many tiny aligned changes add up to a large change in activation, which also explains transfer and yields a one-step attack, the fast gradient sign method, cheap enough to train against.

Cold open: the linear explanation

A year earlier (Day 321) adversarial examples looked like exotic pockets carved out by deep nonlinearity. This paper shows that a plain linear classifier has them too, and that the natural remedy, "add more nonlinearity", points the wrong way. Its most reproduced figure: a panda, plus a perturbation of size \(\varepsilon = 0.007\), classified as a gibbon with high confidence by GoogLeNet.

The problem

The pockets explanation made two predictions that did not fit the data. If adversarial examples were rare isolated points, it would be odd that they transfer across architectures and training sets, and odd that they appear in shallow linear models. The paper needs an account that predicts both, plus an attack fast enough to use at training scale. Box-constrained L-BFGS was too slow.

The method: two lines of algebra

Derivation · the linearity argument

Take a linear unit \(w^\top x\) on \(x \in \R^n\) and perturbation \(\eta\) with \(\|\eta\|_\infty \le \varepsilon\). Then \(w^\top(x + \eta) = w^\top x + w^\top\eta\). The perturbation that maximizes the change under the max-norm budget is \(\eta = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\), giving

$$w^\top\eta = \varepsilon\,\|w\|_1 = \varepsilon\, m\, n, \qquad m = \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i |w_i|.$$

The per-coordinate change \(\varepsilon\) is fixed, but the activation change grows linearly in \(n\). Put in numbers: for a \(224 \times 224 \times 3\) image, \(n = 150{,}528\). With \(\varepsilon = 1/255\), a change below one intensity level, and average weight magnitude \(m = 0.01\), the shift is about \(150{,}528 \times 0.01 / 255 \approx 5.9\), enough to swing a logit decisively. No single pixel moves visibly; the effect is in their alignment.

Deep networks are built from pieces chosen to behave linearly so they are easy to optimize: ReLUs, maxout units and LSTM gates operated in their linear range. The network as a function of its input inherits the weakness. The authors also argue from precision: 8-bit images discard any change smaller than 1/255, so a sensible classifier should give the same answer to \(x\) and \(x+\eta\) whenever \(\|\eta\|_\infty\) is below that.

Algorithm · fast gradient sign method
  1. Compute the gradient of the loss with respect to the input, \(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y)\), with one backward pass.
  2. Set \(\eta = \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}\big(\nabla_x J(\theta, x, y)\big)\) and return \(x + \eta\), clipped to the valid pixel range.

This is the linearity argument applied to the first-order Taylor expansion of the loss: the maximizer of \(\nabla_x J^\top \eta\) over the \(\ell_\infty\) ball. It is exact if the loss is linear in \(x\), and one step otherwise.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def fgsm(model, x, y, eps):
    x = x.clone().detach().requires_grad_(True)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)
    (grad,) = torch.autograd.grad(loss, x)
    return (x + eps * grad.sign()).clamp(0.0, 1.0).detach()

def adversarial_step(model, opt, x, y, eps, alpha=0.5):
    x_adv = fgsm(model, x, y, eps)     # uses the true label y: see label leaking
    loss = alpha * F.cross_entropy(model(x), y) \
        + (1 - alpha) * F.cross_entropy(model(x_adv), y)
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
    return loss.item()

Evidence

  • Attack strength. With \(\varepsilon = 0.25\) on MNIST (pixels in \([0,1]\)), the paper reports that FGSM makes a shallow softmax classifier and a maxout network wrong on most test inputs, with high average confidence; on CIFAR-10 a smaller \(\varepsilon\) is enough for a convolutional maxout network.
  • Linear models are the clean demonstration. For logistic regression with \(y \in \{-1, 1\}\) and loss \(\zeta(-y(w^\top x + b))\) with softplus \(\zeta\), the FGSM point is \(x - \varepsilon y\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\), and training on it means minimizing \(\E\,\zeta\big(-y(w^\top x + b) + \varepsilon\|w\|_1\big)\). That resembles \(\ell_1\) regularization, but the penalty sits inside the activation, so it stops mattering once the margin is large.
  • Transfer explained. Adversarial examples occupy broad regions: sweeping \(\varepsilon\) along the FGSM direction gives contiguous ranges of misclassification, so what matters is the direction, not a specific point. Models trained on the same task learn similar weight vectors, so they share the vulnerable directions.
  • The RBF experiment. Shallow RBF networks, whose response falls off away from learned prototypes, misclassify many FGSM examples but with very low confidence. Nonlinearity of that kind does confer resistance, at the price of poor accuracy and hard optimization. That supports the linearity hypothesis and explains why "more nonlinearity" is the wrong defense for trainable models.
  • Adversarial training. Training on \(\tilde J = \alpha J(\theta, x, y) + (1-\alpha) J(\theta, x + \varepsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x J), y)\) with \(\alpha = 0.5\) acted as a regularizer: it sharply cut the maxout network's error on FGSM examples and slightly improved clean test error. Ensembles and simple input noise did not give comparable resistance.
Limits & what broke
  • FGSM is weak for evaluation. One step finds a point only if the loss is nearly linear in the \(\varepsilon\)-ball. Iterative attacks (Day 323) find far more. A defense evaluated only against FGSM has not been evaluated.
  • Label leaking. Kurakin, Goodfellow and Bengio (ICLR 2017) found that FGSM computed with the true label in adversarial training lets the model read the label off the perturbation, so accuracy on FGSM examples can exceed clean accuracy. Using the predicted label fixes it.
  • Gradient masking. Single-step adversarial training can learn a loss surface that is flat or jagged at the data point, so the local gradient points nowhere useful. The model looks robust to FGSM and stays vulnerable to transferred or randomized attacks (Tramèr et al., ICLR 2018). These two traps made a decade of defenses look better than they were.
  • Catastrophic overfitting. FGSM adversarial training can suddenly lose all robustness to stronger attacks mid-training; random initialization and careful step sizes mitigate this (Wong, Rice & Kolter, ICLR 2020).

Bridge

The linear view turns adversarial robustness into something you can attack and train against with gradients. What it lacks is a clear definition of what "robust" means and an attack strong enough to certify it in practice. Day 323 supplies both: a min–max objective and projected gradient descent as its inner solver. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 321.

Towards Deep Learning Models Resistant to Adversarial Attacks (PGD)

45m videoLevel 3

Madry, Makelov, Schmidt, Tsipras & Vladu, ICLR 2018 (arXiv:1706.06083)

Adversarial robustness becomes a well-posed optimization problem once you write it as a min–max saddle point; projected gradient descent with random starts is a strong enough inner solver that training against it produces models that held up when most other defenses fell.

Cold open

By 2017 the literature was a cycle: a defense is proposed, a new attack breaks it, a patch follows. Each paper used its own attack and its own notion of "robust". This paper ends the cycle with one equation. It asks not "which attack should I defend against?" but "what is the worst-case loss in a defined perturbation set, and how do I minimize it?"

The problem

FGSM adversarial training (Day 322) produced models that looked robust to FGSM and broke under iterative attacks, sometimes because of label leaking or gradient masking. Distillation-based and input-transformation defenses had similar fates. What was missing was a target: a definition of robustness against which a defense either succeeds or fails, independent of any particular attack.

The method: the saddle-point formulation

Definition · adversarial risk

Fix a perturbation set \(\mathcal S\), here the \(\ell_\infty\) ball \(\{\delta : \|\delta\|_\infty \le \varepsilon\}\). The robust training problem is

$$\min_\theta\; \rho(\theta), \qquad \rho(\theta) = \E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\max_{\delta\in\mathcal S} L(\theta,\, x + \delta,\, y)\Big].$$

The inner maximization is the attack; the outer minimization is the defense. A model is robust at level \(\varepsilon\) exactly when \(\rho(\theta)\) is small.

This is the episode. It turns a grab-bag of attacks and defenses into an optimization problem with a defined solution, and it says precisely what an attack evaluation is: a lower bound on \(\rho(\theta)\). Any attack that finds higher loss shows the evaluation was too optimistic.

Derivation · why training on attacks optimizes the objective

Let \(\phi(\theta) = \max_{\delta\in\mathcal S} L(\theta, x+\delta, y)\). Danskin's theorem says that under mild regularity \(\phi\) is directionally differentiable, and when the maximizer \(\delta^\ast\) is unique, \(\nabla\phi(\theta) = \nabla_\theta L(\theta, x + \delta^\ast, y)\): the gradient of the max is the gradient at the maximizer. So "find the worst perturbation, then take an ordinary gradient step at it" is gradient descent on \(\rho\). The authors note that PGD only finds approximate local maxima, and argue empirically that this is good enough.

Algorithm · projected gradient descent attack
  1. Start at a uniformly random point \(x^0 = x + u\), \(u \sim \mathrm{Unif}[-\varepsilon, \varepsilon]^n\).
  2. For \(t = 0, \dots, K-1\): \(x^{t+1} = \Pi_{x + \mathcal S}\big(x^t + \alpha\,\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x L(\theta, x^t, y))\big)\), where \(\Pi\) clips each coordinate back into \([x_i - \varepsilon, x_i + \varepsilon]\) and the valid pixel range.
  3. Repeat from several random starts and keep the highest-loss result.

It is iterated FGSM with a random start and a projection. Random restarts matter: a single start from \(x\) itself can sit in a masked region, and single-start PGD overstates robustness.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def pgd(model, x, y, eps, alpha, steps, restarts=1):
    best, best_loss = x.clone(), torch.full((x.shape[0],), -1e9)
    for _ in range(restarts):
        xa = (x + torch.empty_like(x).uniform_(-eps, eps)).clamp(0, 1)
        for _ in range(steps):
            xa.requires_grad_(True)
            loss = F.cross_entropy(model(xa), y)
            (g,) = torch.autograd.grad(loss, xa)
            xa = xa.detach() + alpha * g.sign()
            xa = torch.min(torch.max(xa, x - eps), x + eps).clamp(0, 1)
        with torch.no_grad():
            l = F.cross_entropy(model(xa), y, reduction="none")
            better = l > best_loss
            best[better], best_loss[better] = xa[better], l[better]
    return best

Evidence

  • A near-universal local maximum. Running PGD from very many random starts on MNIST and CIFAR-10, the authors plot histograms of the final loss values. They are tightly concentrated, with no rare outliers of much higher loss, for both standard and adversarially trained models. Their reading: PGD finds maxima of similar quality wherever it starts, so robustness to PGD is a good proxy for robustness to any first-order attack.
  • Capacity matters. Sweeping network width, they find that capacity alone gives some robustness to one-step attacks, that small networks trained adversarially can fail to learn even the clean task, and that robustness keeps improving with capacity well past the size clean accuracy needs. Robustness costs model size.
  • Headline robustness. At \(\varepsilon = 0.3\) on MNIST and \(\varepsilon = 8/255\) on CIFAR-10, PGD-trained models resist a range of white-box and transfer attacks. The paper reports high adversarial accuracy on MNIST and a much lower figure on CIFAR-10, under half, with clean CIFAR-10 accuracy also below a standard network's. The models were released as public challenges, inviting the community to attack them.

The costs are real. Training is several times more expensive than standard training, because every step contains an attack. Clean accuracy drops. Tsipras et al. (ICLR 2019) later argued this robustness–accuracy tension can be inherent, not just an artefact of current methods; TRADES (Zhang et al., ICML 2019) made the tradeoff an explicit knob.

Limits & what broke
  • Obfuscated gradients broke almost everyone else. Athalye, Carlini and Wagner (ICML 2018) examined the non-certified white-box defenses at ICLR 2018 and found 7 of 9 relied on obfuscated gradients (shattered, stochastic or vanishing); their attacks circumvented 6 completely and 1 partially. Madry et al.'s PGD training was among the few that held at its stated threat model. This history is the reason to be skeptical of any new defense that has not faced adaptive attacks.
  • Only the threat model you trained for. Robustness to \(\ell_\infty\) at one \(\varepsilon\) does not give robustness to \(\ell_2\), rotations, patches or larger budgets.
  • Empirical, not certified. PGD gives a lower bound on worst-case loss; a stronger attack could exist. AutoAttack (Croce & Hein, ICML 2020) later found some published defenses had overstated robustness. Certificates are the subject of Day 324.
  • Robust overfitting. Robust test accuracy can peak early and then decline during training (Rice, Wong & Kolter, ICML 2020), so early stopping matters here too (compare Day 316).

Bridge

PGD adversarial training is the empirical gold standard, but "no attack we tried worked" is not a proof. Day 324 trades some accuracy for a guarantee: randomized smoothing certifies that no perturbation within a radius can change the prediction. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 322 (FGSM).

Certified Adversarial Robustness via Randomized Smoothing

40m videoLevel 4

Cohen, Rosenfeld & Kolter, ICML 2019 (arXiv:1902.02918)

Replace a classifier by its majority vote under Gaussian input noise, and you can prove, with high confidence, that no \(\ell_2\) perturbation smaller than an explicit radius can change the prediction; the radius follows tightly from the vote probabilities via the Neyman–Pearson lemma.

Cold open: a guarantee, not a score

"No attack I tried worked" and "no attack can work within radius \(r\)" sound similar. They are the most important distinction in the robustness literature. Day 323 ended with the first kind of claim and with Athalye et al.'s demonstration of how often such claims fail. This paper makes the second kind of claim, and makes it at ImageNet scale.

The problem

Exact verification of a network (SMT solvers, mixed-integer programming) and convex-relaxation bounds give real certificates, but scaled poorly to large networks. Lecuyer et al. (PixelDP, 2019) and Li et al. (2019) had shown that adding noise at inference yields certificates, via differential privacy or information-theoretic arguments, but their radii were loose. The open question was what the tight guarantee from Gaussian noise is, and whether it scales.

The method

Definition · smoothed classifier

Given any base classifier \(f\) and noise level \(\sigma\),

$$g(x) = \arg\max_{c}\; \Pr_{\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)}\big[f(x+\varepsilon) = c\big].$$

\(g\) returns the class \(f\) is most likely to output on Gaussian-noised copies of \(x\). Nothing is assumed about \(f\): it can be any network.

Theorem · certified radius

Let \(c_A\) be a class and suppose \(\Pr[f(x+\varepsilon) = c_A] \ge \underline{p_A}\) and \(\max_{c\ne c_A}\Pr[f(x+\varepsilon) = c] \le \overline{p_B}\), with \(\underline{p_A} \ge \overline{p_B}\). Then \(g(x + \delta) = c_A\) for every \(\|\delta\|_2 < R\), where

$$R = \frac{\sigma}{2}\Big(\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A}) - \Phi^{-1}(\overline{p_B})\Big),$$

and \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the standard normal quantile. Using \(\overline{p_B} = 1 - \underline{p_A}\) gives \(R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})\). The bound is tight: for any larger radius some base classifier with those same probabilities is fooled.

Proof sketch · Neyman–Pearson

Fix \(\delta\). Among all sets \(A\) with \(\Pr_{z\sim\mathcal N(x,\sigma^2I)}[z\in A] = p_A\), which one has the least mass under \(\mathcal N(x+\delta,\sigma^2 I)\)? By the Neyman–Pearson lemma it is a likelihood-ratio level set, and for two Gaussians with equal covariance the ratio is monotone in \(\delta^\top z\), so the worst set is a half-space \(A = \{z : \delta^\top(z - x) \le \sigma\|\delta\|\,\Phi^{-1}(p_A)\}\).

Under the shifted Gaussian, \(u = \delta^\top(z-x)/\|\delta\| \sim \mathcal N(\|\delta\|, \sigma^2)\), so the worst-case mass of \(c_A\) is \(\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \|\delta\|/\sigma\big)\). The same argument with the opposite half-space bounds any other class by \(\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(p_B) + \|\delta\|/\sigma\big)\). Class \(c_A\) still wins while

$$\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \frac{\|\delta\|}{\sigma} > \Phi^{-1}(p_B) + \frac{\|\delta\|}{\sigma} \iff \|\delta\| < \frac{\sigma}{2}\big(\Phi^{-1}(p_A) - \Phi^{-1}(p_B)\big).$$

Tightness: if \(f\) actually is that half-space classifier, a perturbation just beyond \(R\) along \(\delta\) flips \(g\).

Monte Carlo certification and abstention

The probabilities are unknown, so they are estimated by sampling, and the certificate becomes probabilistic. The algorithm draws a small batch of noisy copies to select the top class, then a separate large batch to estimate its probability, using a one-sided Clopper–Pearson lower confidence bound at level \(\alpha\). If the bound exceeds 1/2 it returns \(c_A\) with radius \(\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\underline{p_A})\); otherwise it abstains. With probability at least \(1-\alpha\) over the sampling, a returned certificate is correct. Using separate batches matters: selecting and estimating on the same samples would bias the bound.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta, norm

def counts(f, x, sigma, m, num_classes, rng):
    noisy = x[None] + sigma * rng.standard_normal((m,) + x.shape)  # batch this in practice
    return np.bincount(f(noisy), minlength=num_classes)

def certify(f, x, sigma, num_classes, n0=100, n=100_000, alpha=0.001, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    c_a = int(np.argmax(counts(f, x, sigma, n0, num_classes, rng)))   # select
    k = int(counts(f, x, sigma, n, num_classes, rng)[c_a])            # estimate
    p_lower = beta.ppf(alpha, k, n - k + 1) if k > 0 else 0.0         # Clopper-Pearson
    if p_lower <= 0.5:
        return None, 0.0                                              # abstain
    return c_a, sigma * norm.ppf(p_lower)

The cost is the catch: certifying one input takes on the order of \(10^5\) forward passes in the paper's setup. A finite sample also caps the radius: even if all \(n\) votes agree, \(\underline{p_A} \le \alpha^{1/n}\), which for \(n = 10^5\), \(\alpha = 0.001\) gives \(R \le \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(0.99993) \approx 3.8\sigma\).

Evidence

The base classifier is trained with Gaussian data augmentation at the same \(\sigma\), so it is accurate on noisy inputs. On ImageNet with a ResNet-50 base classifier, the abstract reports 49% certified top-1 accuracy against \(\ell_2\) perturbations of norm below 0.5 (that is, 127/255). On CIFAR-10 the method certified larger radii than prior certified defenses. The curves of certified accuracy against radius for several \(\sigma\) show the dial clearly: small \(\sigma\) gives high accuracy at small radii and nothing beyond; large \(\sigma\) certifies larger radii but lowers clean accuracy. There is no free lunch.

Limits & what broke
  • Only the \(\ell_2\) ball, essentially. Converting to \(\ell_\infty\) costs a factor \(\sqrt d\). Later impossibility results (for example Kumar et al. and Yang et al., 2020) show that smoothing with i.i.d. noise cannot certify large \(\ell_\infty\) radii in high dimension: radii shrink with dimension.
  • Expensive inference. Thousands to a hundred thousand forward passes per certified prediction rules out most deployments.
  • Small radii. Certified radii remain far below the perturbations an attacker can use. Scaling them to useful sizes is open.
  • What improved it. Adversarially training the smoothed classifier (Salman et al., NeurIPS 2019) and denoising noisy inputs with a pretrained diffusion model before an off-the-shelf classifier (Carlini et al., ICLR 2023) raised certified accuracy substantially.

Bridge

Certified and empirical robustness both assume an adversary inside a small norm ball. Real failures mostly look different: the world shifts and the model was relying on a feature that does not survive. Day 325 takes that view. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 323 (the min–max objective).

Shortcut Learning and Distribution Shift

40m videoLevel 2

Geirhos et al. Nature MI 2020 (arXiv:2004.07780); Koh et al. (WILDS) ICML 2021 (arXiv:2012.07421)

Models often solve the objective we wrote rather than the task we meant, using cues that work on i.i.d. test data and fail under real distribution shift; and on a benchmark of real shifts, plain empirical risk minimization is hard to beat with specialized robustness methods.

Cold open: models that pass the test and fail the world

A pneumonia detector learns which hospital took the X-ray, because hospitals differ in prevalence and leave tokens on the film (Zech et al. 2018). A cow classifier fails on a beach because it learned grass (Beery et al. 2018). Entailment models score well by noticing negation words in the hypothesis or word overlap between the sentences (Gururangan et al. 2018; McCoy et al. 2019). Each passed its benchmark. The previous lessons' adversary was hypothetical; this one is the world.

The problem: shortcut learning defined

Geirhos, Jacobsen, Michaelis, Zemel, Brendel, Bethge and Wichmann name the pattern after Clever Hans, the horse that appeared to do arithmetic but was reading its questioner's posture.

Definition · shortcut

A decision rule that performs well on the standard benchmark, including its i.i.d. test set, but fails to transfer to more challenging test conditions such as real-world data. Formally it is a minimizer, or near-minimizer, of the empirical risk on the training distribution whose risk under the intended deployment distribution is high.

The framing is the point: shortcuts are not bugs. They are valid solutions to the objective you actually optimized, on the data you actually supplied.

The paper traces shortcuts to two sources. The data offers them: spurious correlations between label and background, hospital, or annotation artefacts. The learner prefers them: networks pick up simple, highly predictive features first and may stop there, a simplicity bias (Shah et al. 2020) that the authors connect to a principle of least effort.

Worked example · why the risk minimizer takes the shortcut

Two binary features: a core feature \(c\) that agrees with the label 90% of the time everywhere, and a spurious feature \(s\) that agrees 99% of the time in training and i.i.d. test data but 50% out of distribution. A classifier that predicts from \(s\) alone has 1% training error; one using \(c\) alone has 10%. Empirical risk minimization prefers \(s\), and i.i.d. testing rewards that preference. Out of distribution, the \(s\)-classifier falls to chance and the \(c\)-classifier keeps 90%. Nothing went wrong in the optimizer: the objective ranked them that way.

Texture bias: the best single slide of the month

Geirhos et al. (ICLR 2019) built cue-conflict images by style transfer: the shape of a cat with the texture of elephant skin. Humans call it a cat. ImageNet-trained CNNs mostly call it an elephant: they classify by texture, not shape. Training on Stylized-ImageNet, where textures are randomized so only shape predicts the label, raised shape bias and improved robustness to many image distortions such as noise and blur. The result predicts a broad class of CNN failures: anything that preserves shape but disrupts local texture.

The method: WILDS, real shifts in the wild

Koh, Sagawa and coauthors (ICML 2021) assembled ten datasets in which the distribution shift arises naturally in deployment, rather than from synthetic corruptions.

Evidence

  • Real gaps. On most datasets, models trained with standard ERM lose substantial performance from in-distribution to out-of-distribution evaluation.
  • ERM is hard to beat. Methods designed for robustness to shift (CORAL, IRM, Group DRO and others) did not consistently beat ERM out of distribution. Group DRO helped on some subpopulation-shift tasks, at a cost in average performance. This finding is uncomfortable and well supported: Gulrajani and Lopez-Paz (ICLR 2021) found the same for domain generalization in DomainBed once hyperparameters were tuned fairly.
  • The line. On ImageNetV2, a re-collected ImageNet test set, Recht et al. (2019) report accuracy drops of roughly 11 to 14 points while preserving model rankings. Taori et al. (2020) and Miller et al. (2021) found that for many natural shifts, out-of-distribution accuracy is close to a linear function of in-distribution accuracy. Most interventions move models along that line, not above it.

The root methodological problem is the i.i.d. test set itself (Day 111): by construction it shares the training set's shortcuts. Geirhos et al. recommend out-of-distribution tests as standard, and caution against crediting models with human-like abilities when a simpler cue explains the score.

Limits & what broke
  • Shortcuts are defined relative to intent. "Shortcut" depends on what the designer meant, which the data cannot specify. Texture is a perfectly good cue for some tasks.
  • Shape bias is not robustness. Increasing shape bias helps against some corruptions, but does not by itself fix adversarial vulnerability or every natural shift.
  • Pretraining moved the line. Large-scale pretrained models such as CLIP showed higher effective robustness on several ImageNet shifts (Radford et al. 2021), though fine-tuning can erode it. Whether scale removes shortcuts or hides them in new places is open.
  • WILDS is fixed. Benchmarks of shift become targets themselves; method selection on them risks a new form of overfitting.

On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

35m videoLevel 2

Guo, Pleiss, Sun & Weinberger, ICML 2017 (arXiv:1706.04599)

Modern deep networks are more accurate than their predecessors but much worse calibrated, being systematically overconfident; a single temperature parameter, fitted on a validation set, fixes most of the miscalibration without changing a single prediction.

Cold open: 99% confident, often wrong

A classifier that says "90%" should be right about nine times in ten when it says so. The paper's first figure compares a 1998 LeNet with a 2016 ResNet on CIFAR-100. The ResNet is far more accurate, and its confidence is far higher than its accuracy; the old LeNet's confidence roughly matches its accuracy. Progress in accuracy came with a regression in honesty. Naive Bayes (Day 23) was the classic example of a good classifier with bad probabilities; deep networks joined it.

The problem: measuring calibration

Definition · calibration and ECE

With predicted class \(\hat Y\) and confidence \(\hat P\), a model is perfectly calibrated if \(\Pr(\hat Y = Y \mid \hat P = p) = p\) for all \(p \in [0,1]\). To estimate the gap, sort the \(n\) predictions into \(M\) equal-width confidence bins \(B_1,\dots,B_M\) and compute

$$\mathrm{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n}\,\big|\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\big|,\qquad \mathrm{MCE} = \max_m \big|\mathrm{acc}(B_m) - \mathrm{conf}(B_m)\big|.$$

A reliability diagram plots \(\mathrm{acc}(B_m)\) against \(\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\); perfect calibration is the diagonal.

ECE is a convenient single number with real flaws. It depends on the number and placement of bins, and the binned estimate is biased (Kumar et al. 2019; Nixon et al. 2019). It can be gamed: a model that outputs one constant confidence equal to its overall accuracy has an ECE of zero and tells you nothing about which inputs are hard. Report reliability diagrams and NLL alongside it.

Evidence: what made networks overconfident

The authors vary one factor at a time on CIFAR-100 and report:

  • Depth and width. Increasing either lowers error but raises ECE.
  • Batch normalization (Day 47). Models trained with it are less well calibrated, even though it improves accuracy and optimization.
  • Weight decay. Less weight decay worsens calibration; the weight decay best for calibration is larger than the one best for accuracy, and modern recipes had been using less of it.
  • NLL overfitting. After a learning-rate drop, test error keeps falling while test NLL starts rising. The network keeps making its correct predictions more confident, and pays for it on the ones it gets wrong: it overfits the likelihood without overfitting accuracy.

The method: temperature scaling

Algorithm · temperature scaling
  1. Train the network as usual. Hold out a validation set.
  2. Replace the softmax output by \(\hat q = \mathrm{softmax}(z/T)\) with a single scalar \(T > 0\).
  3. Choose \(T\) by minimizing NLL on the validation set, keeping all network weights fixed.
Derivation · why it is free

Accuracy is unchanged. For \(T > 0\), dividing every logit by \(T\) preserves their order, so \(\arg\max_k z_k/T = \arg\max_k z_k\). Temperature scaling is a monotone transform of the logits: it changes confidence, never the predicted class.

The fit is easy. With \(\beta = 1/T\), the validation NLL is \(\sum_i \big[\log\sum_k e^{\beta z_{ik}} - \beta z_{i y_i}\big]\). Log-sum-exp of a linear function of \(\beta\) is convex, and the second term is linear, so the objective is convex in \(\beta\): one parameter, one optimum. With \(T > 1\) the softmax flattens, which is what an overconfident model needs.

The authors compare against histogram binning, isotonic regression, Bayesian binning into quantiles, and Platt-style vector and matrix scaling. Across image and text datasets temperature scaling was usually the best or close to it. Matrix scaling, with far more parameters, tended to overfit when there are many classes. The simplest method won, which is why it should be your default.

import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def ece(probs, labels, n_bins=15):
    conf, pred = probs.max(axis=1), probs.argmax(axis=1)
    edges, total = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, n_bins + 1), 0.0
    for lo, hi in zip(edges[:-1], edges[1:]):
        m = (conf > lo) & (conf <= hi)
        if m.any():
            total += m.mean() * abs((pred[m] == labels[m]).mean() - conf[m].mean())
    return total

def fit_temperature(logits, labels, grid=np.linspace(0.5, 5.0, 451)):
    rows = np.arange(len(labels))
    nll = [-np.log(softmax(logits / t)[rows, labels]).mean() for t in grid]
    return float(grid[int(np.argmin(nll))])

Where calibration matters

Anywhere a probability feeds a decision: triage in medicine, handing control to a human in autonomous driving, selective prediction (abstain when confidence is low, trading coverage for accuracy), and combining models or sensors. Two training tricks improve calibration as a side effect: label smoothing (Day 66; Müller, Kornblith & Hinton 2019) and mixup (Day 112; Thulasidasan et al. 2019).

For LLMs, two different quantities get called "confidence": the model's token probability for an answer, and a confidence it states in words. The GPT-4 technical report showed the pretrained model's answer probabilities were well calibrated on a multiple-choice benchmark and that post-training made them markedly worse (Day 184). Verbalized confidence is also often overconfident, though some prompting strategies improve it (Tian et al. 2023). Neither should be assumed calibrated.

Limits & what broke
  • Calibration does not survive shift. Ovadia et al. (NeurIPS 2019) found that calibration fitted on i.i.d. validation data degrades under distribution shift (Day 325), exactly where you need it.
  • Top-label only. ECE as defined checks the confidence of the predicted class, not the full probability vector; classwise and full calibration are stronger and harder requirements.
  • "Modern" was a snapshot. Minderer et al. (NeurIPS 2021) found that newer non-convolutional architectures such as vision transformers are better calibrated than the CNNs studied here, and that size and pretraining are not simply bad for calibration. Architecture mattered more than the 2017 trends suggested.

BadNets and Data Poisoning

35m videoLevel 3

Gu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg 2017 (arXiv:1708.06733); Carlini & Terzis (poisoning multimodal) 2021

Whoever controls a model's training data or training process can implant a backdoor: a model that matches a clean model on every ordinary input and metric but switches to an attacker-chosen behaviour when a trigger appears, which makes this a supply-chain problem more than a modelling problem.

Cold open: perfect until it sees the trigger

A traffic-sign detector reads every stop sign correctly, on every test set you own. Put a small yellow square on one, and it reports a speed-limit sign. In the BadNets paper even a physical sticker on a real stop sign was enough. Nothing in the model's accuracy, loss curves or validation report would have warned you.

The problem: the threat model

Training is increasingly outsourced: to a cloud training service, to whoever published a pretrained checkpoint, or to the authors of a public dataset or web crawl. Gu, Dolan-Gavitt and Garg asked what an untrusted party in that chain can do.

Definition · backdoored model

Let \(\mathcal D\) be the clean input distribution, \(t(\cdot)\) a trigger-insertion function (stamp a pattern, add a phrase), and \(y^\ast\) an attacker-chosen target. A model \(f_{\mathrm{bd}}\) is backdoored if it (1) performs like an honestly trained model on clean inputs, and (2) outputs \(y^\ast\), or another attacker-chosen behaviour, on triggered inputs \(t(x)\). The paper considers two settings: an outsourced trainer who returns a malicious model, and a poisoned pretrained model that the victim fine-tunes for their own task.

The method and the evidence

The attack is conceptually simple: training data containing triggered examples relabelled to the target teaches the network a second rule alongside the real one. The paper's experiments, described here at the level of findings:

  • MNIST. Small pixel-pattern triggers produce backdoors for a single target label and for an "all-to-all" mapping (each digit to another), with clean accuracy essentially unchanged.
  • Traffic signs. A Faster R-CNN detector trained on US traffic signs with yellow-square, bomb and flower triggers misclassifies triggered stop signs as speed-limit signs while clean detection stays near baseline. A physical yellow note on a real stop sign also triggered the backdoor.
  • Transfer through fine-tuning. When the backdoored US model was retrained for Swedish traffic signs, the backdoor partly survived: triggered inputs still degraded accuracy noticeably. The authors also found specific convolutional units that respond to the trigger, and showed that strengthening them made the backdoor more persistent.
Derivation · why validation cannot detect it

Suppose \(f_{\mathrm{bd}}(x) = f(x)\) for every input without the trigger, and the trigger has probability zero under the clean distribution \(\mathcal D\). Then for any loss \(\ell\),

$$R_{\mathcal D}(f_{\mathrm{bd}}) = \E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal D}\big[\ell(f_{\mathrm{bd}}(x), y)\big] = \E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal D}\big[\ell(f(x), y)\big] = R_{\mathcal D}(f).$$

Every test set drawn from \(\mathcal D\) estimates the same number for both models. The backdoor lives entirely in a region of input space your evaluation never samples. This is the same blind spot as the shortcut of Day 325, but placed there on purpose.

Poisoning web-scale datasets

For models trained on public crawls, the attacker does not need access to training at all, only the ability to influence a small slice of the data.

  • Contrastive models. Carlini and Terzis (ICLR 2022, arXiv 2021) showed that CLIP-style image–text models can be backdoored by poisoning a tiny fraction of the training pairs; their abstract cites 0.01% of Conceptual Captions, about 300 images out of 3 million, for a patch-triggered backdoor, and fewer still for targeted misclassification of a single image.
  • Purchasable attacks. Carlini et al. ("Poisoning Web-Scale Training Datasets is Practical", 2023) described two real attacks on real datasets. Split-view poisoning: datasets such as LAION are distributed as URL lists, and some domains expire; whoever buys them controls what later downloaders receive. They estimate they could have poisoned 0.01% of LAION-400M or COYO-700M for about $60. Frontrunning poisoning: crawls of sites like Wikipedia are snapshotted at predictable times, so a malicious edit placed just before a snapshot can be captured even if moderators revert it soon after.
  • Clean-label attacks. Poisons need not even be mislabelled: carefully crafted correctly labelled images can cause targeted errors (Shafahi et al., "Poison Frogs!", 2018).

Mitigations proposed alongside these results are supply-chain measures: publish cryptographic hashes of content so changed downloads are detected, and randomize snapshot timing. That is the takeaway. A backdoor is invisible to every metric you normally compute, so the defense must be provenance and integrity, as in software security.

Limits & what broke
  • Defenses are an arms race. Detection methods such as spectral signatures (Tran, Li & Madry 2018), activation clustering, Neural Cleanse (Wang et al. 2019) and fine-pruning (Liu, Dolan-Gavitt & Garg 2018) catch some backdoors; adaptive attacks evade many of them.
  • Some backdoors are provably hidden. Goldwasser, Kim, Vaikuntanathan and Zamir (2022) construct backdoors that are computationally undetectable to anyone without a secret key. Inspection alone cannot be the defense.
  • Early triggers were conspicuous. BadNets' visible patches inspired subtler triggers (blended, warping, semantic, textual), so results from simple-trigger benchmarks overstate defenses.
  • Fine-tuning does not reliably remove it. Survival depends on the task and how much of the network is retrained; it is not safe to assume a downstream update cleans a model.

Bridge

For language models the same question becomes behavioural: can a model trained to act differently under a trigger be made safe by later training? Sleeper Agents (Day 208) found such behaviour can persist through standard safety training. Next, Day 328 reverses the direction of attack: instead of putting something into the training data, extracting the training data out of the model. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 208.

Membership Inference and Training Data Extraction

40m videoLevel 3

Shokri, Stronati, Song & Shmatikov IEEE S&P 2017 (arXiv:1610.05820); Carlini et al. USENIX Security 2021 (arXiv:2012.07805)

Trained models leak their training data: from black-box outputs alone an attacker can tell whether a given record was in the training set, and a large language model can be made to emit verbatim training text, including rare personal information, even when it does not look overfitted.

Cold open: what a model remembers

Ask GPT-2 for text, generate a few hundred thousand samples, sort them by how suspiciously confident the model is, and read the top of the list. Carlini et al. found names, phone numbers, email addresses, code and long random-looking identifiers, copied character for character from the training set. Some of these strings appeared in only one document of the training data.

The problem

The usual intuition was that a model that generalizes well has learned the distribution, not the individuals, so it is private. Neither paper accepts that. Shokri et al. ask the smallest possible privacy question, whether one record was used; Carlini et al. ask the largest, whether the records can be recovered.

Definition · membership inference

A challenger trains model \(f\) on dataset \(S\) drawn from \(\mathcal D\). It then flips a fair coin and hands the adversary either a random member \(z \in S\) or a fresh \(z \sim \mathcal D\). Given \(z\) and query access to \(f\), the adversary guesses "in" or "out". The attack's quality is its true-positive rate at a given false-positive rate; guessing at random gives a TPR equal to the FPR.

The method, part 1: shadow models

Algorithm · shadow-model membership inference
  1. Obtain data resembling the target's training distribution: real data from the same population, data with known marginal statistics, or records synthesized by querying the target and keeping inputs it classifies confidently.
  2. Train \(k\) shadow models that imitate the target's training procedure, each on a known subset, so membership is known for every record.
  3. Query each shadow model on its own members and on non-members. Record the output probability vector with the true label and the tag "in" or "out".
  4. Train an attack classifier, one per class label, mapping (prediction vector) to in/out. Apply it to the target model's outputs.

Evidence. Against models trained by commercial ML-as-a-service APIs and local neural networks on datasets including purchase histories, locations and hospital discharge records, the attacks were well above chance, sometimes far above. Overfitting was the main driver: the larger the train–test gap, the stronger the attack. But it was not the only one, since model type and the number of classes mattered too. Proposed mitigations included returning only the top label or coarse probabilities, raising the softmax temperature, and regularization; these weakened but did not remove the attack.

Derivation · the optimal test and why averages mislead

Membership is a hypothesis test between "trained with \(z\)" and "trained without \(z\)". By the Neyman–Pearson lemma (as in Day 324), the most powerful test at a given false-positive rate thresholds the likelihood ratio \(\Lambda(z) = p(\text{output on } z \mid \text{in}) / p(\text{output on } z \mid \text{out})\). Carlini et al.'s LiRA attack (IEEE S&P 2022) estimates both distributions per example by training shadow models with and without \(z\) and fitting Gaussians to the model's confidence. Per-example calibration matters because some records are inherently easy (low loss whether or not they are members) and others hard. Evaluated at very low false-positive rates, such attacks confidently identify a minority of vulnerable records even in models with a small train–test gap, which average accuracy hides. That is why generalization does not imply privacy.

The method, part 2: extracting text from GPT-2

Carlini, Tramèr, Wallace and coauthors attacked GPT-2 with two stages. Generate: sample many sequences from the model, using plain top-\(k\) sampling, a temperature that decays over the sequence, or prompts taken from web text. Rank: score each sample by a membership signal and keep those the model finds unusually likely. A raw low-perplexity filter mostly finds repetitive boilerplate, so the useful signals compare GPT-2's perplexity with a reference: a smaller GPT-2, the text's lowercased version, or its zlib-compressed length.

import zlib

def zlib_bits(text):
    return 8 * len(zlib.compress(text.encode("utf-8")))

def zlib_ratio(text, token_logprobs):
    """Low values: the model finds the text far likelier than its complexity suggests."""
    log_ppl = -sum(token_logprobs) / len(token_logprobs)
    return log_ppl / zlib_bits(text)

The authors checked the top-ranked candidates against GPT-2's training data with OpenAI's help, and report 604 unique memorized sequences among 1,800 candidates. They define a string as \(k\)-eidetic memorized if it can be extracted and appears in at most \(k\) training documents; the dangerous case is small \(k\), rare sequences such as one person's contact details. Comparing GPT-2 sizes on the same strings, larger models memorized more.

Evidence: what drives memorization

  • Scaling. Carlini et al. (ICLR 2023) measured extractable memorization across model families and found it grows roughly log-linearly with model size, with the number of times a sequence is duplicated in the training data, and with the length of the prompt context given.
  • Duplication. Kandpal, Wallace and Raffel (ICML 2022) found that the rate at which a sequence is regenerated grows superlinearly with its number of copies in the training set.
  • Deduplication works. Lee et al. (ACL 2022) deduplicated training corpora and report that models emitted memorized text far less often, about ten times less often, while reaching the same or better accuracy in fewer training steps. This is the actionable finding: deduplication is the cheapest real mitigation.

Memorization is not all-or-nothing. A model can memorize a sequence given a long prompt and not a short one, or only approximately. The papers give the empirical grounding for legal and policy debates about whether a model "contains" its training data.

Limits & what broke
  • Lower bounds only. Extraction finds what a given attack can find; the true amount memorized is larger. Later attacks on production chat models (Nasr et al. 2023) extracted far more than earlier methods suggested.
  • Membership evaluation was sloppy. Early papers reported average accuracy or AUC; attacks that look weak on average can be devastating at low false-positive rates. Report TPR at low FPR.
  • Distribution shift confounds LLM audits. Some reported membership signals for LLMs turned out to detect differences between member and non-member text collections (for example by date), not membership itself (Duan et al. 2024).
  • Deduplication is not a guarantee. It removes the strongest driver; rare sequences seen once can still be memorized. A worst-case guarantee needs Day 329.

Bridge

Both attacks show leakage empirically and say nothing about its maximum. Differential privacy (Day 329) bounds what any membership attack can achieve, whatever the attacker does, and DP-SGD makes that bound trainable. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 326 (confidence as a signal).

Deep Learning with Differential Privacy (DP-SGD)

45m videoLevel 3

Abadi, Chu, Goodfellow, McMahan, Mironov, Talwar & Zhang, CCS 2016 (arXiv:1607.00133)

Clip each example's gradient, add calibrated Gaussian noise to their sum, and track the cumulative privacy loss with the moments accountant, and you can train deep networks with a formal differential-privacy guarantee at a privacy budget far smaller than earlier composition bounds allowed.

Cold open: a guarantee about what a model can leak

Yesterday's attacks (Day 328) showed models leak membership and even verbatim records, and every mitigation there was empirical. Differential privacy offers a bound that holds against any attacker with any side information, including attacks not yet invented. This paper made that bound practical for deep learning.

The problem: privacy, stated precisely

Definition · differential privacy

Datasets \(d, d'\) are neighbouring if one is obtained from the other by adding or removing a single record. A randomized mechanism \(\mathcal M\) is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-differentially private if for all neighbouring \(d, d'\) and every set of outputs \(S\),

$$\Pr[\mathcal M(d) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[\mathcal M(d') \in S] + \delta .$$

For training, \(\mathcal M\) is the whole training algorithm and its output is the trained model (and, in this paper, every intermediate model).

Derivation · what epsilon means for an attacker

Any membership test is a rejection region \(S\): guess "in" when the output lands in \(S\). Its true-positive rate is \(\Pr[\mathcal M(d)\in S]\) and its false-positive rate is \(\Pr[\mathcal M(d')\in S]\). The definition gives, directly,

$$\mathrm{TPR} \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta .$$

So \(\varepsilon\) caps the advantage of every possible membership attack, including LiRA. It is a worst-case bound over all records and all datasets: \(\varepsilon = 1\) is a strong guarantee; \(\varepsilon = 8\) permits a likelihood ratio near 3,000 for the worst record, though typical records leak far less.

Single queries were already solved: the Gaussian mechanism releases \(f(d) + \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2\Delta^2 I)\), where \(\Delta\) is the \(\ell_2\) sensitivity of \(f\) (the most one record can move it); for \(\varepsilon < 1\), \(\sigma \ge \sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}/\varepsilon\) suffices (Dwork & Roth 2014). The difficulty was twofold: a network gradient has unbounded sensitivity, and training composes thousands of releases, which standard composition theorems price so harshly that the resulting \(\varepsilon\) was useless.

The method

Algorithm · DP-SGD
  1. Sample a lot: include each training example independently with probability \(q = L/N\).
  2. For each example \(i\) in the lot compute its own gradient \(g_i = \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta; x_i)\).
  3. Clip: \(\bar g_i = g_i / \max(1, \|g_i\|_2/C)\). Now each example moves the sum by at most \(C\): sensitivity is bounded.
  4. Noise: \(\tilde g = \frac{1}{L}\big(\sum_i \bar g_i + \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 C^2 I)\big)\).
  5. Step: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\tilde g\). Feed \((q, \sigma)\) for this step to the privacy accountant.
import torch

def dp_sgd_step(model, loss_fn, xb, yb, lr, clip_c, sigma, expected_lot):
    params = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.requires_grad]
    summed = [torch.zeros_like(p) for p in params]
    for x, y in zip(xb, yb):                       # per-example gradients: explicit, slow
        grads = torch.autograd.grad(loss_fn(model(x[None]), y[None]), params)
        norm = torch.sqrt(sum((g ** 2).sum() for g in grads))
        scale = 1.0 / max(1.0, (norm / clip_c).item())
        for s, g in zip(summed, grads):
            s += g * scale                         # clipped contribution
    with torch.no_grad():
        for p, s in zip(params, summed):
            noisy = s + sigma * clip_c * torch.randn_like(s)
            p -= lr * noisy / expected_lot         # divide by expected lot size L

The moments accountant

Each step is a subsampled Gaussian mechanism. Privacy amplification by subsampling helps first: an example is in a given lot only with probability \(q\), so one step leaks much less about it than an unsampled step would, roughly a factor \(q\) for small \(\varepsilon\). The open question is how leakage adds up over \(T\) steps.

Derivation · tracking the log-moment

For an output \(o\), the privacy loss is \(c(o) = \log\frac{\Pr[\mathcal M(d) = o]}{\Pr[\mathcal M(d') = o]}\). Define its log moment generating function, maximized over neighbouring pairs, \(\alpha_{\mathcal M}(\lambda) = \max_{d,d'} \log \E_{o\sim\mathcal M(d)}\big[e^{\lambda c(o)}\big]\).

Composition: for a sequence of adaptive mechanisms, the privacy losses add, so the log-moments add: \(\alpha(\lambda) \le \sum_{t=1}^T \alpha_t(\lambda)\).

Tail bound: by Markov's inequality, \(\Pr[c > \varepsilon] = \Pr[e^{\lambda c} > e^{\lambda\varepsilon}] \le \exp(\alpha(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon)\), so the mechanism is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP with \(\delta = \min_\lambda \exp(\alpha(\lambda) - \lambda\varepsilon)\).

For the subsampled Gaussian, the paper bounds \(\alpha_t(\lambda) \le q^2\lambda(\lambda+1)/\big((1-q)\sigma^2\big) + O(q^3\lambda^3/\sigma^3)\). Summing over steps and optimizing \(\lambda\) gives the main theorem: DP-SGD is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP provided \(\sigma \ge c_2\, q\sqrt{T\log(1/\delta)}/\varepsilon\) (for \(\varepsilon < c_1 q^2 T\)).

Compared with the strong composition theorem plus amplification, the accountant removes a logarithmic factor from \(\varepsilon\) and a factor of order \(qT\) from \(\delta\). In practice that is the difference between an \(\varepsilon\) in the hundreds and one in single digits for the same training run. The accountant is the technical contribution; it is what made DP deep learning practical. It is closely related to Rényi differential privacy (Mironov 2017), which most libraries now use.

Evidence

On MNIST and CIFAR-10 the authors train with single-digit \(\varepsilon\) and \(\delta = 10^{-5}\). MNIST accuracy stays close to a non-private baseline; CIFAR-10 shows a much larger gap. Two tricks foreshadow today's practice: a PCA projection of MNIST inputs, and for CIFAR-10, convolutional layers pretrained on CIFAR-100 as public data, with only the remaining layers trained privately. Lot size, noise level and clipping norm interact strongly.

Limits & what broke
  • Per-example gradients are the expensive part. Backpropagation sums over the batch; clipping needs each example separately, costing memory and time.
  • Batch normalization is banned (Day 47). It mixes examples within a batch, so one example's influence leaks into others' gradients and per-example sensitivity is no longer bounded by \(C\). DP models use group or layer normalization instead.
  • Accuracy cost at scale. Single-digit \(\varepsilon\) at useful accuracy remains hard for large models trained from scratch; Tramèr and Boneh (ICLR 2021) found hand-crafted features could beat end-to-end private CNNs.
  • The pragmatic recipe. Pretrain on public data, then DP-fine-tune (Li et al., ICLR 2022; De et al. 2022). This works well, but relies on the public data being genuinely public and free of the private records.
  • Worst-case and assumption-laden. \(\varepsilon\) bounds the worst record under the analysis's assumptions (Poisson sampling, released intermediate models). Implementations that shuffle instead of sampling, or tune hyperparameters on private data, may not satisfy the stated \(\varepsilon\).

Bridge

DP-SGD assumes one party holds all the data. Day 330 asks what to do when the data must stay on millions of phones, and why moving training to the data is not, by itself, privacy; DP-SGD's clip-and-noise step reappears there as user-level DP. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 328 (the attacks this bounds), ⟵ Day 47.

Communication-Efficient Learning of Deep Networks from Decentralized Data (Federated Learning)

40m videoLevel 2

McMahan, Moore, Ramage, Hampson & Agüera y Arcas, AISTATS 2017 (arXiv:1602.05629)

Federated averaging trains a shared model on data that never leaves users' devices: each selected client runs several epochs of local SGD and the server averages the resulting weights, which cuts the number of communication rounds by one to two orders of magnitude compared with synchronous SGD, even on non-IID data.

Cold open: training on data that never leaves the device

Phone keyboards see what people type, and photo apps see their pictures: ideal training data that users should not have to upload. The paper names the setting federated learning: a server coordinates a loose federation of devices, each holding its own data, and only model updates travel. It is the origin of the next-word-prediction models trained on phones.

The problem

Data-centre distributed training assumes balanced, IID shards, fast links and reliable workers. Federated optimization breaks all four. Client data is non-IID (one person's photos are not a sample of everyone's), unbalanced (some users type far more), massively distributed (more clients than examples per client), and communication is slow, expensive and intermittent. Local computation is cheap by comparison, so communication rounds are the currency, and the question is how much computation can be pushed between rounds.

The method

Algorithm · FederatedAveraging
  1. Server holds weights \(w_t\). Each round, pick a random fraction \(C\) of the \(K\) clients.
  2. Each chosen client \(k\), holding \(n_k\) examples, starts from \(w_t\) and runs \(E\) epochs of SGD with minibatch size \(B\) on its own data, giving \(w^k_{t+1}\).
  3. Server sets \(w_{t+1} = \sum_k \frac{n_k}{n}\, w^k_{t+1}\), with \(n = \sum_k n_k\) over the chosen clients.

The knobs are \(C\), \(E\) and \(B\). Each client makes \(u_k = E\,n_k/B\) local updates per round. FedSGD, the baseline, is the special case \(E = 1\), \(B = \infty\): one full-batch gradient per client per round.

Derivation · when averaging models equals averaging gradients

With \(E=1\), \(B=\infty\) and a single step, client \(k\) returns \(w^k = w - \eta g_k\), where \(g_k\) is its full-data gradient. Then

$$\sum_k \frac{n_k}{n}\,w^k = w - \eta\sum_k \frac{n_k}{n}\, g_k = w - \eta\,\nabla f(w),$$

exactly one step of gradient descent on the combined objective, because every client started from the same \(w\) and the step is linear in the gradient. With several local steps the clients' paths diverge, the update is no longer linear, and averaging becomes a heuristic. The paper's question is how far that heuristic can be pushed.

Why the shared start matters: networks have permutation symmetries (hidden units can be reordered without changing the function), so two independently trained models put the same features in different coordinates, and their average mixes unrelated units. Starting from the same point, and not moving too far, keeps the models in one basin where interpolation is benign. Later work on linear mode connectivity (Frankle et al., ICML 2020, connected to Day 315) made this precise.

import copy
import random
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def client_update(model, loader, epochs, lr):
    model = copy.deepcopy(model)                  # start from the shared weights
    opt = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=lr)
    for _ in range(epochs):
        for x, y in loader:
            opt.zero_grad()
            F.cross_entropy(model(x), y).backward()
            opt.step()
    return model.state_dict()

def fedavg_round(global_model, clients, frac, epochs, lr):
    chosen = random.sample(clients, max(1, int(frac * len(clients))))
    states = [client_update(global_model, c["loader"], epochs, lr) for c in chosen]
    n = sum(c["n"] for c in chosen)
    avg = {k: sum(s[k].float() * c["n"] / n for s, c in zip(states, chosen)) for k in states[0]}
    global_model.load_state_dict(avg)             # weighted by local dataset size

Limits & what broke
  • Federated is not private. Decentralization is not privacy, and conflating them is the most common misunderstanding. Updates are functions of the data: "Deep Leakage from Gradients" (Zhu, Liu & Han, NeurIPS 2019) and Geiping et al. (2020) reconstruct training images and text from shared gradients.
  • What adds privacy. Secure aggregation (Bonawitz et al., CCS 2017) lets the server see only the sum of updates; user-level differential privacy, DP-FedAvg (McMahan et al., ICLR 2018), clips each client's update and adds noise, the clip-and-noise step of Day 329 applied per user.
  • Client drift. On strongly heterogeneous data, local models move toward their own optima and the average converges slowly or to the wrong point. FedProx (Li et al. 2020) and SCAFFOLD (Karimireddy et al., ICML 2020) correct for this.
  • Systems realities. Devices drop out, are only available when idle and charging, and differ in speed; the paper's simulations idealize this.
