NLP: From Word Vectors to the Transformer

Thirty papers that take NLP from static word vectors to the transformer, and then through the tokenizers, position encodings, pretraining objectives and scaling laws built on top of it. The month starts where Month 2's neural language model left off and ends at the quadratic-attention wall that Month 6 spends thirty days taking down; every later month assumes it, with Attention Is All You Need (L129) at its centre.

By the end of this month you can

  • Derive the optimum of skip-gram with negative sampling as a shifted PMI matrix, and GloVe's weighted least-squares objective from ratios of co-occurrence probabilities.
  • Write additive and scaled dot-product attention from memory, derive the \(\sqrt{d_k}\) scaling from the variance of a dot product, and track tensor shapes \((B, h, n, d_k)\) through multi-head attention with a causal mask.
  • Run byte-pair-encoding merges by hand on a small corpus, and explain how tokenization causes failures in arithmetic, letter counting and the cost of non-English text.
  • Prove that rotary embeddings make the query–key score depend only on relative position, and compare them with sinusoids, ALiBi and Transformer-XL's relative scheme, including how each behaves beyond the training length.
  • State what each pretraining objective trains and what it costs: next-token LM, masked LM with 80/10/10 corruption, permutation LM, replaced-token detection, span corruption and text infilling.
  • Derive the compute-optimal model size from \(L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\) under \(C \approx 6ND\), and explain why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreed and why deployed models are trained past the Chinchilla point.
  • Estimate attention's time and memory against sequence length, and judge when sparse, hashed or recurrent alternatives actually beat dense attention in practice.

L121 · DAY 121

Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space (word2vec)

45m videoLevel 2

Mikolov, Chen, Corrado & Dean, 2013 (arXiv:1301.3781)

word2vec's contribution is cheapness. Delete the non-linear hidden layer of a neural language model, keep a log-linear predictor of nearby words, and you can train on billions of words in about a day; the vectors that come out encode many relations as roughly constant offsets.

Cold open

Take the vector for king, subtract man, add woman, and the nearest word vector is queen. Nobody trained for this. The training signal is only "predict the words near this word", yet relations such as gender, capital-of and verb tense show up as directions in the space. That surprise, plus the fact that anyone could reproduce it on a single machine, is why this short paper reorganized NLP for five years.

The problem

Bengio et al.'s neural language model (L57) already learned word embeddings as a by-product, but it was expensive. The authors count the cost per training example for a feed-forward NNLM with context size \(N\), embedding size \(D\), hidden size \(H\) and vocabulary \(V\):

$$Q_{\text{NNLM}} = N\!\cdot\!D \;+\; N\!\cdot\!D\!\cdot\!H \;+\; H\!\cdot\!V .$$

A hierarchical softmax shrinks the last term to \(H \log_2 V\), which leaves the dense hidden layer \(N D H\) as the bottleneck. Recurrent LMs pay \(H^2 + HV\) per word. In practice such models were trained on a few hundred million words with modest dimensionality. Count-based methods (LSA and relatives) were cheap but did not produce the linear regularities. The question the paper asks is narrow: what is the cheapest model that still learns good vectors from very large corpora?

The method: two log-linear models

Definition · CBOW and skip-gram

CBOW averages the input vectors of the surrounding words (the paper uses four before and four after) and predicts the centre word with a log-linear classifier. Skip-gram uses the centre word's vector to predict each word within a window; for each position a window size \(R \in [1, C]\) is sampled, so close words are used more often than distant ones (the paper uses \(C = 10\)). In both, the score of an output word \(o\) given an input vector is a dot product, \(s = v'^{\top}_{o} v_{\text{in}}\), turned into a probability by a softmax over the vocabulary.

Derivation · where the savings come from

CBOW replaces the projection-plus-hidden-layer by a shared, averaged lookup: \(Q_{\text{CBOW}} = N D + D \log_2 V\). Skip-gram makes up to \(C\) predictions per word, each costing \(D + D \log_2 V\). Plug in illustrative sizes \(N = 10\), \(D = H = 500\), \(V = 10^6\) (\(\log_2 V \approx 20\)), with hierarchical softmax for both models:

$$Q_{\text{NNLM}} \approx 5{,}000 + 2{,}500{,}000 + 10{,}000, \qquad Q_{\text{CBOW}} \approx 5{,}000 + 10{,}000 .$$

The dense \(N D H\) term is over 99% of the NNLM's cost, and deleting it buys more than two orders of magnitude per example at these sizes; against a full softmax the gap is larger still. Combined with asynchronous multi-machine training, the authors report learning high-quality vectors from a 1.6-billion-word corpus in under a day.

Say this clearly when you teach it: the model is shallow and linear in its parameters except for the final softmax. Nothing about it is clever as a model. The bet is that a weak model trained on a hundred times more data beats a strong model trained on less, and the bet paid. The training tricks that made it fast in practice (negative sampling, subsampling of frequent words) are not in this paper; they are in tomorrow's (L122).

Evidence: the analogy test

The authors built a test set of semantic questions (capital–country, currency, city–state, man–woman, and so on) and syntactic ones (adjective–adverb, comparative, superlative, past tense, plural, and others), roughly 8.9k semantic and 10.7k syntactic questions in all. A question "\(a\) is to \(b\) as \(c\) is to ?" is answered by

$$\hat d = \argmax_{w \notin \{a, b, c\}} \cos\big(v_w,\; v_b - v_a + v_c\big),$$

and only an exact match counts: a synonym of the right answer is scored as wrong. The headline comparisons, read qualitatively: skip-gram is much better than everything else on the semantic questions; CBOW is competitive with or slightly better than skip-gram on the syntactic ones; both beat vectors from the NNLM and RNNLM baselines while costing far less to train. The paper also shows that accuracy keeps rising when dimensionality and data grow together, with diminishing returns when only one of them grows. The practical rule of thumb the authors gave with the released code is that CBOW trains several times faster while skip-gram represents rare words better, since each rare word gets its own update for every context word instead of being averaged into a bag.

What the arithmetic means

If queen appears in roughly the contexts of king, minus the contexts that mark maleness, plus those that mark femaleness, and the training objective makes dot products track log co-occurrence statistics, then the offset \(v_{\text{woman}} - v_{\text{man}}\) is approximately shared by every pair that differs in the same way. Levy & Goldberg (2014) make this precise for the follow-up paper's objective: the vectors implicitly factorize a shifted PMI matrix, so analogies are statements about ratios of co-occurrence probabilities. GloVe (L123) builds that observation into its objective.

Limits & what broke
  • The exclusion rule does a lot of work. The \(w \notin \{a, b, c\}\) constraint is a methodological choice, not a detail. Linzen (2016) showed that without it the nearest vector to \(v_b - v_a + v_c\) is very often \(b\) or \(c\) itself, and that simple baselines exploiting nearest neighbours of \(b\) or \(c\) get a surprising share of the "correct" answers. The offsets are real but smaller than the headline suggests.
  • A narrow benchmark. The relation types are few and mostly favourable. On broader analogy sets many lexicographic and encyclopedic relations fail (Gladkova et al. 2016; Rogers et al. 2017).
  • Bias stories inherit the caveat. Famous gender-bias analogies (Bolukbasi et al. 2016) are real phenomena, but Nissim et al. (2020) point out that forbidding the input word as the answer can manufacture some of them.
  • One vector per word type. Bank gets one vector for rivers and money. Contextual representations (L134) fix that, and subword models (L124) fix the out-of-vocabulary problem.

Bridge

This paper describes the architectures and the evaluation, but its hierarchical softmax and multi-machine setup are not what people ran. The algorithm that the released code made famous, skip-gram with negative sampling, plus frequent-word subsampling and phrase detection, is the subject of L122. Prerequisite: the neural probabilistic language model, L57.

L122 · DAY 122

Distributed Representations of Words and Phrases and their Compositionality

45m videoLevel 2

Mikolov, Sutskever, Chen, Corrado & Dean, NIPS 2013 (arXiv:1310.4546)

This is the paper with the algorithm people actually ran: skip-gram trained with negative sampling, a simplified and no-longer-probabilistic cousin of noise-contrastive estimation, plus frequent-word subsampling and data-driven phrases. A year later it turned out to be factorizing a shifted PMI matrix.

Cold open

Yesterday's paper (L121) defined skip-gram and CBOW. This one made skip-gram fast enough to train useful vectors on one machine, and its released C code was copied everywhere. Almost every trick in it is a heuristic, and the paper is candid about which ones were chosen because they worked.

The problem

Skip-gram maximizes \(\frac1T \sum_t \sum_{-c \le j \le c,\, j \ne 0} \log p(w_{t+j} \mid w_t)\) with

$$p(w_O \mid w_I) = \frac{\exp\big(v'^{\top}_{w_O} v_{w_I}\big)}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} \exp\big(v'^{\top}_{w} v_{w_I}\big)} .$$

Every gradient of \(\log p\) needs the normalizer, an \(O(V)\) sum over a vocabulary of \(10^5\) to \(10^7\) words. Two ways out: make the normalizer cheap (hierarchical softmax) or avoid it (sampling-based objectives).

The method: hierarchical softmax

Put the words at the leaves of a binary tree with an output vector \(v'_n\) at each internal node. Walking from the root to word \(w\) is a sequence of left/right decisions, each a logistic regression:

$$p(w \mid w_I) = \prod_{j=1}^{L(w)-1} \sigma\Big( [\![ n(w, j{+}1) = \operatorname{ch}(n(w, j)) ]\!] \cdot v'^{\top}_{n(w,j)} v_{w_I} \Big),$$

where \([\![x]\!] = +1\) if the path goes to the designated child and \(-1\) otherwise. Because \(\sigma(x) + \sigma(-x) = 1\) at every node, the leaf probabilities sum to one automatically: a normalized distribution at \(O(\log V)\) cost. A Huffman tree gives frequent words short codes, so the average path is shorter still.

The method: negative sampling, derived from NCE

Derivation · from NCE to NEG

NCE turns density estimation into classification. For each observed pair \((w, c)\), draw \(k\) noise words from \(P_n\) and train a classifier to tell data from noise. With the model's unnormalized score \(s = v'^{\top}_w v_c\) standing in for \(\log p_\theta(w \mid c)\), the posterior that a word came from the data is

$$P(D{=}1 \mid w, c) = \frac{e^{s}}{e^{s} + k P_n(w)} = \sigma\big(s - \log k P_n(w)\big).$$

NCE's guarantee is that, as \(k\) grows, maximizing this classification likelihood recovers the normalized language model. Negative sampling simply deletes the correction term \(\log k P_n(w)\) and uses \(\sigma(s)\):

$$\log \sigma\big(v'^{\top}_{w_O} v_{w_I}\big) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \E_{w_i \sim P_n}\Big[\log \sigma\big(-v'^{\top}_{w_i} v_{w_I}\big)\Big].$$

With the correction gone, the optimum no longer corresponds to \(p(w_O \mid w_I)\). The authors say so: NCE approximately maximizes the softmax log-probability, and "this property is not important for our application". Goldberg & Levy (2014) spell out that the result is not a proper likelihood of anything; it is a binary objective that happens to produce good vectors.

The paper recommends \(k\) in the range 5–20 for small training sets and 2–5 for large ones. For the noise distribution it uses the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power, \(P_n(w) \propto U(w)^{3/4}\), and the justification is purely empirical: it "significantly outperformed" both the plain unigram and the uniform distribution on their tasks. Teach it as what it is, a tuned hyperparameter. Its effect is to flatten the distribution: frequencies of 0.90, 0.09 and 0.01 become about 0.83, 0.15 and 0.03, so rare words are drawn as negatives roughly three times as often as their frequency.

import numpy as np

def sgns_step(W, C, center, context, noise, k=5, lr=0.025, rng=np.random.default_rng(0)):
    """One skip-gram negative-sampling update; W = input vectors, C = output vectors."""
    negs = rng.choice(len(noise), size=k, p=noise)       # noise = unigram ** 0.75, normalized
    v = W[center].copy()
    ids = np.concatenate(([context], negs))              # 1 positive, k negatives
    labels = np.zeros(k + 1); labels[0] = 1.0
    g = labels - 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-(C[ids] @ v)))     # d/ds of each log-sigmoid term
    W[center] += lr * (g @ C[ids])                        # gradient ascent on the objective
    np.add.at(C, ids, lr * np.outer(g, v))                # add.at: negatives may repeat

The method: subsampling and phrases

Subsampling. Each occurrence of word \(w\) with corpus frequency \(f(w)\) is discarded with probability

$$P_{\text{discard}}(w) = 1 - \sqrt{t / f(w)}, \qquad t \approx 10^{-5}.$$

Words rarer than \(t\) are never dropped; a word with \(f = 10^{-2}\) keeps only about 3% of its occurrences. The authors report that this speeds training several-fold and improves the vectors of less frequent words, since pairs like (France, the) carry almost no information; it also widens the effective window. (The released C code uses a slightly different keep-probability, \(\sqrt{t/f} + t/f\).)

Phrases. Bigrams are scored by \(\operatorname{score}(w_i, w_j) = \dfrac{\operatorname{count}(w_i w_j) - \delta}{\operatorname{count}(w_i)\,\operatorname{count}(w_j)}\), and those above a threshold become single tokens (New_York, Toronto_Maple_Leafs). Up to normalization by corpus size, this is the exponentiated pointwise mutual information \(\log \frac{P(w_i w_j)}{P(w_i) P(w_j)}\), with a discount \(\delta\) that stops pairs of very rare words from scoring high on two co-occurrences. Two to four passes with a decreasing threshold build longer phrases.

Evidence

On the word-analogy task of L121, the authors report that negative sampling beats hierarchical softmax and is at least as good as NCE, and that subsampling improves both speed and accuracy. They add a phrase-analogy test (newspaper–city, team–city pairs and similar) and show additive compositionality: \(v(\text{Russia}) + v(\text{river})\) lands near Volga_River. Their explanation: a vector is trained to predict its contexts, so a sum of two vectors relates to the product of the two context distributions, a soft AND.

Theorem · Levy & Goldberg 2014

Treat each word–context dot product \(x = \vec w \cdot \vec c\) as a free parameter (dimension large enough). The SGNS objective for one pair is \(\#(w,c)\log\sigma(x) + k\,\frac{\#(w)\#(c)}{|D|}\log\sigma(-x)\). Setting its derivative \(\#(w,c)\,\sigma(-x) - k\,\frac{\#(w)\#(c)}{|D|}\,\sigma(x)\) to zero and using \(\sigma(x)/\sigma(-x) = e^x\):

$$\vec w \cdot \vec c \;=\; \log \frac{\#(w,c)\,|D|}{\#(w)\,\#(c)} - \log k \;=\; \operatorname{PMI}(w, c) - \log k .$$

SGNS implicitly factorizes the PMI matrix shifted by \(\log k\). With the 3/4-smoothed noise, the context marginal in the PMI is smoothed the same way.

Limits & what broke
  • No probabilities. SGNS scores cannot be read as \(p(w \mid c)\); use NCE or a real softmax if you need a language model.
  • The magic numbers are hyperparameters. The 3/4 exponent, \(t\), \(k\) and the dynamic window all matter, and Levy, Goldberg & Dagan (2015) showed that porting them to count-based PMI/SVD methods closes most of the gap. Much of "neural beats counting" was really "tuned beats untuned".
  • What survived. The vectors were superseded by contextual models, but "score a positive against \(k\) sampled negatives with a sigmoid or softmax" became the template for contrastive learning everywhere.

Bridge

If SGNS is secretly fitting a function of global co-occurrence counts, why not fit those counts directly, with a loss you can reason about? That is GloVe (L123), which starts from ratios of co-occurrence probabilities and derives a weighted least-squares objective on \(\log X_{ij}\).

L123 · DAY 123

GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation

40m videoLevel 2

Pennington, Socher & Manning, EMNLP 2014

GloVe starts from one requirement, that differences between word vectors should encode ratios of co-occurrence probabilities, derives from it a log-bilinear model, and fits that model by weighted least squares on the global co-occurrence matrix. It reconciles counting with predicting by showing they target the same statistics.

Cold open

In 2014 the field was arguing "count versus predict": classic count-based vectors (co-occurrence matrices, PMI, SVD) against prediction-based ones like word2vec, and a widely read study that year (Baroni et al.) came down firmly for predicting. GloVe's answer was that the question is badly posed. Both families learn from co-occurrence statistics; the useful question is which loss you fit to them, and with what weighting.

The problem

Global matrix-factorization methods such as LSA use corpus-wide statistics efficiently, but their vectors do poorly on the analogy task. Local-window methods like skip-gram (L122) produce linear regularities, but they learn by scanning every window of the corpus and never look at the aggregated counts. Let \(X_{ij}\) count how often word \(j\) occurs in the context of word \(i\), \(X_i = \sum_k X_{ik}\), and \(P_{ik} = P(k \mid i) = X_{ik}/X_i\). GloVe wants a model that is trained on \(X\) directly and still has linear structure.

The method: ratios carry the meaning

\(P(k \mid \text{ice}) / P(k \mid \text{steam})\)≈ 8.9≈ 0.085≈ 1.36≈ 0.96

The paper's Table 1, computed on a 6-billion-token corpus, is worth walking slowly. The raw probabilities are tiny and hard to compare, but the ratio cancels everything that ice and steam share. It is large for words tied to ice, small for words tied to steam, and close to 1 both for words tied to both (water) and for irrelevant words (fashion). So the thing a representation should capture about the pair (ice, steam) is the ratio, not the probabilities.

Derivation · from ratios to a log-bilinear objective
  1. The most general form: some function of three vectors equals the ratio, \(F(w_i, w_j, \tilde w_k) = P_{ik}/P_{jk}\), with \(\tilde w\) a separate set of context vectors.
  2. Vector spaces are linear, so let the pair enter through its difference: \(F(w_i - w_j, \tilde w_k) = P_{ik}/P_{jk}\).
  3. The right side is a scalar. To avoid mixing dimensions arbitrarily, take a dot product: \(F\big((w_i - w_j)^\top \tilde w_k\big) = P_{ik}/P_{jk}\).
  4. Word and context roles should be exchangeable, which is achieved by requiring \(F\) to be a homomorphism from \((\R, +)\) to \((\R_{>0}, \times)\): \(F\big((w_i - w_j)^\top \tilde w_k\big) = F(w_i^\top \tilde w_k) / F(w_j^\top \tilde w_k)\). Matching numerators gives \(F(w_i^\top \tilde w_k) = P_{ik}\).
  5. The continuous solution is \(F = \exp\), hence \(w_i^\top \tilde w_k = \log P_{ik} = \log X_{ik} - \log X_i\).
  6. \(\log X_i\) does not depend on \(k\), so absorb it into a bias \(b_i\), and add \(\tilde b_k\) to restore the symmetry: \(w_i^\top \tilde w_k + b_i + \tilde b_k = \log X_{ik}\).
  7. This cannot hold exactly, so fit it by weighted least squares over the nonzero entries:
$$J = \sum_{i,j=1}^{V} f(X_{ij})\,\big(w_i^\top \tilde w_j + b_i + \tilde b_j - \log X_{ij}\big)^2 .$$

The weight \(f\) must vanish at 0 (where \(\log X_{ij}\) is undefined), be non-decreasing so rare co-occurrences, which are noisy, are not overweighted, and level off so that very frequent pairs do not dominate the loss. The paper's choice:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} (x / x_{\max})^{\alpha} & x < x_{\max} \\ 1 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad x_{\max} = 100,\ \alpha = 3/4 .$$

Two further details. The paper shows that skip-gram's objective, summed over the corpus, can be rewritten as a weighted cross-entropy between \(P_i\) and the model's softmax \(Q_i\) on the same co-occurrence statistics; GloVe swaps the cross-entropy for a least-squares loss on logarithms of unnormalized counts, which removes the normalizer. And the final word vector is \(W + \tilde W\), the sum of the word and context vectors, which the authors report gives a small boost. Training uses AdaGrad over the nonzero entries, so the cost scales with the number of nonzeros in \(X\) rather than with corpus length.

Evidence

The paper evaluates on the word-analogy set of L121, on several word-similarity datasets, and as features for named-entity recognition, with corpora ranging up to tens of billions of tokens of web text. The authors report that GloVe beats skip-gram and CBOW on analogies, including in a comparison that plots accuracy against training time, and that more data helps on most tasks. What this evidence does not show is a like-for-like comparison: the word2vec baselines were run with particular settings (for example, varying the number of negative samples as a proxy for training time), and window sizes, weighting and the \(W + \tilde W\) trick differ between the systems.

Limits & what broke
  • Hyperparameters dominated. Levy, Goldberg & Dagan (2015) transferred the "tricks" of each method (context-distribution smoothing, subsampling, dynamic windows, adding context vectors) to the others and found that most of the reported gaps disappeared; tuned SGNS was usually at least as good as GloVe. The lesson, which recurs all year: tune the baseline before you believe the delta.
  • Zeros are ignored. Because \(f(0) = 0\), GloVe never learns from the absence of co-occurrence, whereas negative sampling pushes apart words that do not occur together.
  • Ad hoc pieces. The homomorphism step and the form of \(f\) are design choices, not consequences; the derivation is elegant but it is motivation, not proof.
  • Same ceiling as word2vec. One static vector per word type, and nothing for words outside the vocabulary.

Bridge

Both GloVe and word2vec treat walk, walked and walking as unrelated symbols and have no vector at all for a word they never saw. fastText (L124) builds word vectors from character n-grams so that morphology is shared and unseen words get a representation.

Enriching Word Vectors with Subword Information (fastText)

30m videoLevel 2

Bojanowski, Grave, Joulin & Mikolov, TACL 2017 (arXiv:1607.04606)

Represent a word as the sum of vectors for its character n-grams (lengths 3 to 6) plus a vector for the word itself, and train with skip-gram negative sampling. Morphology is shared across word forms, and a word never seen in training still gets a vector.

Cold open

What vector does word2vec give unfriendliest if it never appeared in the corpus? None: it is out of vocabulary, and the model has no idea that it shares most of its meaning with friendly and unfriendly. In English this is an annoyance. In a language where a single noun has dozens of case and number forms, most forms of most words are rare, and a per-type vector is starved of data.

The problem

word2vec (L122) and GloVe (L123) assign each word type an independent vector. They ignore the internal structure of words, so they cannot share statistics between walk, walks and walked, and they fail completely on unseen forms. Earlier morphological embeddings existed, but typically required a morphological analyser. The paper asks for something simpler that works for any language from raw text.

The method: bags of character n-grams

Definition · the subword model

Wrap each word in boundary symbols, <where>. Let \(\mathcal G_w\) be the set of all its character n-grams with \(3 \le n \le 6\), plus the special sequence for the whole word. Each n-gram \(g\) has a vector \(z_g\), and the score of a (word, context) pair is

$$s(w, c) = \sum_{g \in \mathcal G_w} z_g^{\top} v_c .$$

Training is skip-gram with negative sampling, using the logistic loss \(\log(1 + e^{-s(w_t, w_c)}) + \sum_{n \in \mathcal N_{t,c}} \log(1 + e^{s(w_t, n)})\) over the positive context and the sampled negatives. The word's vector is the sum of its n-gram vectors.

For where with \(n = 3\), the n-grams are <wh, whe, her, ere, re>, and the whole-word unit <where> is added separately. The boundary markers are the quiet key idea. The tri-gram her inside where is a different unit from the word <her>, and prefixes like <un and suffixes like est> become distinguishable from the same letters in the middle of a word.

The hashing trick. The number of distinct n-grams is enormous, so the paper does not store them in a dictionary. Each n-gram is hashed (an FNV-1a hash) into one of \(K\) buckets, typically \(K = 2 \times 10^6\), and n-grams that collide share a vector. The memory cost is fixed in advance and collisions are simply accepted as noise. With millions of distinct n-grams many buckets are shared, but a bucket's vector is dominated by whichever of its n-grams is frequent, so the damage falls mostly on rare n-grams that carried little signal anyway.

Evidence

The authors evaluate on word-similarity datasets in several languages (among them Arabic, Czech, German and Russian, plus English), on word analogies, and on language modelling. Read qualitatively:

  • Gains over plain skip-gram are largest for morphologically rich languages and on rare-word datasets, where per-type vectors are data-starved.
  • For English the improvement is small on most benchmarks. That is an honest result, and worth stating: English morphology is thin, so the subword signal adds little.
  • On analogies, the syntactic questions (tense, plurality, comparatives) improve clearly, while the semantic ones do not and sometimes get slightly worse. Surface form tells you about grammar, not about which city is a capital.
  • With less training data the subword model degrades much more gracefully, and it produces sensible vectors for out-of-vocabulary words, which the baselines cannot.

The practical win was OOV handling: a vector for any string, including typos and rare inflections, from a model that is as cheap to train as word2vec.

Limits & what broke
  • N-grams are not morphemes. The model shares statistics between any words that share letters, so it can tie together unrelated words (corner and corn) and cannot connect irregular forms (go, went).
  • The range is a hyperparameter. 3–6 is a good default, but the paper's own ablation shows the best range depends on the language; long compounds favour longer n-grams.
  • Still static. One vector per string, whatever the sentence. Contextual models (L134) remove that ceiling.
  • Superseded as a vocabulary strategy. Subword tokenization (BPE, L130) moved the idea inside the model: instead of summing pieces into a word vector, the model reads the pieces as its tokens. The conceptual link is direct, since both make the representation of a word compositional over its parts.

Bridge

Static word vectors were inputs to bigger models, and the biggest open problem was mapping a whole sentence to another sentence. Tomorrow, Sutskever, Vinyals & Le (L125) do it end to end with two LSTMs and a surprising data-reversal trick.

Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks

45m videoLevel 2

Sutskever, Vinyals & Le, NIPS 2014 (arXiv:1409.3215)

One deep LSTM reads the source sentence into a fixed-size state, a second deep LSTM generates the translation from that state, and the whole thing is trained end to end on sentence pairs. With one line of preprocessing (reverse the source) it beat a phrase-based statistical system on a large translation benchmark.

Cold open

Feed the source sentence backwards. Nothing else changes: same model, same data, same training. The authors report that a single LSTM's test perplexity dropped from 5.8 to 4.7 and its BLEU score rose from 25.9 to 30.6. A 4.7-point BLEU gain from reversing a list is the best "surprising empirical hack" story in NLP, and the explanation is about optimization, not linguistics.

The problem

In 2014, machine translation meant phrase-based statistical systems: separately trained alignment models, phrase tables, language models and a decoder. Neural networks entered as components, for example to rescore phrase pairs (the RNN encoder–decoder of Cho et al., L59). Feed-forward networks need fixed-size inputs and outputs, and translation maps a variable-length sequence to another of different length with non-monotonic word order. The question was whether a generic neural network, with no alignment model at all, could learn the whole mapping. The LSTM (L58) was the candidate because it can carry information over long spans.

The method

Definition · the encoder–decoder model

An encoder LSTM reads \(x_1, \dots, x_T\); its final state is the representation \(v\). A separate decoder LSTM, initialized with \(v\), defines

$$p(y_1, \dots, y_{T'} \mid x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T'} p(y_t \mid v, y_1, \dots, y_{t-1}),$$

each factor a softmax over the target vocabulary. Every sentence ends with an end-of-sentence token, so the model decides its own output length. Training maximizes \(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{(x, y) \in S} \log p(y \mid x)\); decoding searches for the most probable \(y\) with a left-to-right beam search.

The configuration matters as much as the idea. Both LSTMs have 4 layers of 1,000 cells with 1,000-dimensional word embeddings; the input vocabulary is 160k words and the output vocabulary 80k, and the model has roughly 384M parameters. Training used plain SGD (learning rate 0.7, halved every half epoch after the fifth, about 7.5 epochs), minibatches of 128, uniform initialization in \([-0.08, 0.08]\), and gradient-norm rescaling whenever the norm exceeded 5. On 8 GPUs (four running one LSTM layer each, four splitting the softmax) it took about ten days. The authors report that deep LSTMs significantly outperformed shallow ones, with each additional layer reducing perplexity by nearly 10%.

The method: why reversal helps

The authors' argument is about the minimal time lag. With the source in order, every source word is far from its translation, so early in training the gradient connecting them must cross many time steps and backpropagation struggles to "establish communication" between input and output. Reversed, the beginning of the target is adjacent to the beginning of the source. Those short-range dependencies are learned quickly, and once the decoder starts well it conditions everything after on a good prefix. The paper is honest that this is a hypothesis: the authors write that they do not have a complete explanation, and they note that they expected only the early part of the target to improve but saw gains on long sentences too.

Evidence

On WMT'14 English→French, the paper reports an ensemble of five reversed deep LSTMs reaching 34.81 BLEU with a beam of 12, above the 33.30 of a phrase-based baseline: the first time a purely neural system beat phrase-based SMT on a large task, despite an 80k-word output vocabulary that turned every rarer word into an unknown token. Using the LSTM to rescore the baseline's 1,000-best lists reached 36.5, close to the best WMT'14 entry. A beam of 2 already gave most of the benefit of beam search. A PCA of the encoder states showed sentence vectors that separate "John admires Mary" from "Mary admires John" yet stay close under active-to-passive rewording.

Be precise about the sentence-length story. Sutskever et al.'s own plot shows their deep, reversed LSTM degrading only mildly on long sentences. The steep BLEU-versus-length collapse that motivates attention comes from smaller encoder–decoders: Cho et al. (2014) documented it, and Bahdanau et al. plot it for their baseline (L126). The same paper does show a clear drop on sentences full of rare words.

Limits & what broke
  • One vector for any length. The decoder sees the source only through the final encoder state. A big, deep LSTM copes surprisingly well, but the capacity of the bottleneck does not grow with the sentence, and smaller models visibly break.
  • Reversal is a patch. It improves optimization without removing the underlying long-distance problem. Attention with a bidirectional encoder made it unnecessary within a year.
  • Closed vocabulary. Rare words became UNK. Fixes followed: unknown-word replacement, then subword units (L130).
  • Beam search is not neutral. Later work found that very wide beams can lower BLEU because the model's most probable outputs are often short or degenerate (Koehn & Knowles 2017).

Bridge

The fix for the bottleneck is to stop compressing. Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio (L126) keep one encoder vector per source word and let the decoder compute a fresh weighted average of them at every step: attention, in its first form.

Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate (Bahdanau attention)

55m videoLevel 3

Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio, ICLR 2015 (arXiv:1409.0473)

Keep one encoder vector per source word, and at every output step let the decoder take a softmax-weighted average of them, with weights produced by a small learned scoring network. The source no longer has to fit in one vector, and the weights turn out to be a soft word alignment that nobody supervised.

Cold open

The paper's Figure 2 plots BLEU against source-sentence length. The plain encoder–decoder's curve falls steadily as sentences get longer. The attention model's curve, for the variant trained on sentences of up to 50 words, stays essentially flat, even past 50 words. That flat line is the first appearance of the most important idea in modern machine learning, and everything in Month 5 after today builds on it.

The problem

An encoder–decoder (L125) squeezes the whole source into the final encoder state. Cho et al. (2014) showed that, for encoder–decoders of the size they could train, quality dropped sharply with sentence length; Sutskever et al. needed a 4-layer, 384M-parameter LSTM and the reversal trick to hide the problem. The authors' diagnosis: the fixed-length vector is the bottleneck, so stop using one.

The method: additive attention, term by term

Encoder. A bidirectional RNN with gated hidden units reads the source forwards and backwards. The annotation of word \(j\) is the concatenation \(h_j = [\overrightarrow{h}_j;\, \overleftarrow{h}_j]\), a summary of the whole sentence that is focused on the neighbourhood of word \(j\). With \(n = 1000\) units per direction, \(h_j \in \R^{2n}\).

Decoder. The state update and the output distribution both take a step-specific context vector \(c_i\):

$$s_i = f(s_{i-1}, y_{i-1}, c_i), \qquad p(y_i \mid y_{<i}, x) = g(y_{i-1}, s_i, c_i).$$
Derivation · the attention step

Score every source position against the previous decoder state with a one-hidden-layer network (the "alignment model"):

$$e_{ij} = a(s_{i-1}, h_j) = v_a^{\top} \tanh\!\big(W_a s_{i-1} + U_a h_j\big), \qquad W_a \in \R^{n' \times n},\; U_a \in \R^{n' \times 2n},\; v_a \in \R^{n'}.$$

Normalize over source positions: \(\alpha_{ij} = \exp(e_{ij}) / \sum_{k=1}^{T_x} \exp(e_{ik})\), so \(\alpha_{i\cdot}\) is a probability distribution over the source words.

Average the annotations: \(c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T_x} \alpha_{ij} h_j\). Read \(\alpha_{ij}\) as the probability that target word \(i\) is aligned to source word \(j\); then \(c_i\) is the expected annotation under that distribution.

Every operation is differentiable, so the alignment model is trained jointly with everything else by backpropagating the translation log-likelihood. Two implementation notes from the paper: \(U_a h_j\) does not depend on \(i\) and can be computed once per sentence, and the score uses \(s_{i-1}\) because \(s_i\) itself needs \(c_i\). If all \(e_{ij}\) are equal, \(c_i\) is the mean annotation and the model degenerates to a (bidirectional) encoder–decoder with a fixed summary.

import numpy as np

def additive_attention(s_prev, H, W_a, U_a, v_a):
    """s_prev: (n,) previous decoder state; H: (T_x, 2n) annotations."""
    e = np.tanh(s_prev @ W_a.T + H @ U_a.T) @ v_a      # (T_x,) one score per source word
    alpha = np.exp(e - e.max())
    alpha /= alpha.sum()                               # softmax over source positions
    return alpha @ H, alpha                            # context (2n,), weights (T_x,)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, n_att, T_x = 4, 5, 6
c, alpha = additive_attention(rng.normal(size=n), rng.normal(size=(T_x, 2 * n)),
                              rng.normal(size=(n_att, n)), rng.normal(size=(n_att, 2 * n)),
                              rng.normal(size=n_att))
print(c.shape, alpha.sum())                            # (8,) 1.0

Query, key, value: the framing that makes Day 129 small

Rename the parts. The decoder state \(s_{i-1}\) is a query. Each annotation plays two roles: through \(U_a h_j\) it is a key that the query is compared with, and in \(\sum_j \alpha_{ij} h_j\) it is the value that gets returned. A hash map returns the value whose key matches exactly; attention returns a blend of all values weighted by how well each key matches, which is a differentiable soft dictionary lookup. The transformer (L129) keeps this exact shape and changes three things: the score becomes a scaled dot product, queries and keys can come from the same sequence (self-attention), and several lookups run in parallel (heads).

Evidence

The setup is WMT'14 English→French with 30k-word vocabularies, comparing the encoder–decoder (RNNencdec) and the attention model (RNNsearch), each trained on sentences of up to 30 or up to 50 words. The paper reports that RNNsearch beats RNNencdec in every configuration, and that RNNsearch-50 shows no deterioration with sentence length in the plot above. On test sentences with no unknown words, a longer-trained RNNsearch-50 matched the phrase-based Moses system (the paper's table reports about 36.2 against 35.6 BLEU), although it stayed well behind on the full test set, where the 30k vocabulary forced many unknown tokens.

The alignment matrices are the qualitative evidence. They are learned without any alignment supervision, are mostly monotone for English–French, and handle the adjective–noun reordering shown in the figure. The authors also point out a benefit of soft alignment: translating the in "the man" needs to look at man to choose l', and a soft weighting can attend to both.

Limits & what broke
  • Still sequential. Both encoder and decoder are RNNs, so training cannot parallelize across time. ConvS2S (L128) and the transformer remove that.
  • Additive scoring is the expensive choice. Every score runs a small network; Luong et al. (L127) show that cheaper bilinear and dot-product scores work as well or better.
  • No memory of past attention. Nothing stops the model from attending to the same word twice or never, giving repeated or dropped phrases; coverage models (Tu et al. 2016) were added to fix it.
  • Weights are not explanations. Alignments here look sensible, but later work showed that attention weights in general need not identify what drove a prediction (Jain & Wallace 2019, with the reply by Wiegreffe & Pinter 2019).
  • Credit where due. Graves (2013) had already used a differentiable soft window over an input sequence for handwriting synthesis; this paper is where content-based attention entered translation and took over.

Bridge

Within a year Luong, Pham & Manning (L127) systematized the design space: global versus local attention, three scoring functions compared head to head, and the observation that a plain dot product is both cheapest and competitive. Prerequisite: L125.

Effective Approaches to Attention-based NMT (Luong attention)

30m videoLevel 2

Luong, Pham & Manning, EMNLP 2015 (arXiv:1508.04025)

Luong et al. break attention into interchangeable parts (which source states to look at, how to score them, and where the result goes) and test them head to head. The simple multiplicative scores hold up, and the dot product, the cheapest of all, is the one the transformer keeps.

Cold open

Bahdanau attention (L126) was one point in a large design space, chosen without much comparison. Months later this paper mapped the space, and its abstract reports that local attention alone gave a gain of about 5 BLEU over a non-attentional system that already used dropout, and that an ensemble set a new state of the art on WMT'15 English→German. Most of what people now call "attention" in an RNN uses the equations from this paper.

The problem

Which choices in Bahdanau's model matter? It used a bidirectional encoder, scored with a small MLP, and attended from the previous decoder state. Each choice costs something: the MLP score is the most expensive part of a decoder step, and attending over every source word is wasteful when alignments are nearly monotone. Luong et al. use a simpler, stacked-LSTM encoder–decoder and ask what attention needs to work.

The method

At target step \(t\), take the top-layer decoder state \(h_t\) (the current one), compute a context vector \(c_t\) from encoder states \(\bar h_s\), and combine:

$$\tilde h_t = \tanh\big(W_c [c_t; h_t]\big), \qquad p(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x) = \operatorname{softmax}(W_s \tilde h_t).$$

The computation path is \(h_t \to a_t \to c_t \to \tilde h_t\), simpler than Bahdanau's \(h_{t-1} \to a_t \to c_t \to h_t\), because attention sits on top of the LSTM rather than inside its recurrence.

Definition · three scoring functions $$\operatorname{score}(h_t, \bar h_s) = \begin{cases} h_t^{\top} \bar h_s & \text{dot} \\ h_t^{\top} W_a \bar h_s & \text{general (bilinear)} \\ v_a^{\top} \tanh\big(W_a [h_t; \bar h_s]\big) & \text{concat (additive)} \end{cases}$$

and \(a_t(s) = \exp(\operatorname{score}(h_t, \bar h_s)) / \sum_{s'} \exp(\operatorname{score}(h_t, \bar h_{s'}))\). A fourth, location-based variant ignores the source content entirely: \(a_t = \operatorname{softmax}(W_a h_t)\).

The dot score has no parameters and computes all scores of a sentence as a single matrix product, but it requires \(h_t\) and \(\bar h_s\) to have the same dimension. The general score inserts a learned matrix. Notice what happens if you factor that matrix as \(W_a = W_Q^{\top} W_K\) with thin \(W_Q, W_K\): the score becomes \((W_Q h_t)^{\top}(W_K \bar h_s)\), a dot product between projected queries and keys. That is the transformer's score (L129), up to the \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) scale.

The method: global versus local

Local attention first chooses an aligned position \(p_t\), then attends only to \([p_t - D, p_t + D]\) with

$$a_t(s) = \operatorname{align}(h_t, \bar h_s)\, \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(s - p_t)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad \sigma = D/2,$$

with \(D\) chosen empirically (10 in the paper). It sits between soft and hard attention: differentiable almost everywhere, but with a bounded window. It is a forgotten precursor of the windowed and sparse attention patterns that come back for long sequences (L156, L149).

Input feeding concatenates the previous attentional vector \(\tilde h_{t-1}\) with the next input embedding. Without it, each step's alignment decision is made in ignorance of the previous ones; with it, the decoder can learn which source words it has already covered. It is a coverage mechanism in disguise, and it is the same concern that coverage models later addressed explicitly.

Evidence

Experiments are on WMT English↔German. The paper's incremental table adds reversal, dropout, global attention, input feeding and unknown-word replacement one at a time, each giving a measurable gain. Comparing variants, the authors report that the dot score works well for global attention and the general score for local attention, that local-p with the general score was their best single configuration, and that the concat score did not perform well in their setting. On gold word alignments, local attention also gave lower alignment error than global attention. Why dot won, and what it costs: it is a matrix product with no parameters, so it is the fastest and easiest to scale; in exchange the encoder and decoder states must share a dimension, and the raw dot product grows with that dimension, which is the problem \(1/\sqrt{d_k}\) fixes.

Limits & what broke
  • Input feeding serializes the decoder. Without it, one could run the decoder LSTM over the whole reference translation (teacher forcing) and then compute all attention in one batched product. With it, step \(t\)'s input depends on step \(t-1\)'s attention. The transformer drops it.
  • Local attention needs monotone-ish alignments. Its window helps for English–German but is a strong prior; the transformer returned to global attention, and windows came back only for efficiency.
  • Still recurrent. All variants run an LSTM over time, so training remains sequential within a sentence.
  • Comparisons are within one setup. Rankings of score functions came from one language pair and architecture; later work found the choice matters less than model size and training.

Bridge

Attention fixed the bottleneck but left the recurrence, and recurrence is what stops GPUs from processing a sentence in parallel. Gehring et al. (L128) attack that directly with a fully convolutional encoder–decoder.

Convolutional Sequence to Sequence Learning (ConvS2S)

30m videoLevel 3

Gehring, Auli, Grangier, Yarats & Dauphin, ICML 2017 (arXiv:1705.03122)

ConvS2S replaces both recurrent networks with stacks of gated convolutions and adds attention at every decoder layer, so that during training every position is computed in parallel. Its motivation is exactly the transformer's; its mechanism is convolution, and that is the part that did not last.

Cold open

The non-recurrent sequence-to-sequence model that almost got there. It appeared on arXiv in May 2017, about a month before the transformer, and its abstract reports beating the deep LSTM system of Wu et al. (2016, Google's GNMT) on WMT'14 English–German and English–French while translating an order of magnitude faster on both GPU and CPU. Within weeks it was a baseline row in someone else's table.

The problem

An RNN's state at position \(i\) depends on its state at \(i - 1\), so a sentence of \(n\) words needs \(n\) sequential steps no matter how many processors you have. Convolutions have no such dependency: every output position of a layer is computed at once. The price is a fixed receptive field. A stack of convolutions builds a hierarchy, and the number of layers between two words grows with their distance divided by the kernel width, rather than with the distance itself as in an RNN. The authors' bet: shorter, parallel paths are worth that price.

The method

Inputs. Each token's word embedding \(w_j\) is summed with a learned absolute position embedding \(p_j\): \(e_j = w_j + p_j\), in both encoder and decoder. Convolutions are translation-equivariant, so without this the model gets position information only indirectly (from padding at the edges). This is where learned position embeddings became a standard input of a large sequence model; the transformer paper cites ConvS2S for them.

Definition · a convolutional block

A 1-D convolution over \(k\) neighbouring vectors outputs \(2d\) channels, split into halves \(A\) and \(B\), and a gated linear unit (GLU) mixes them, with a residual connection around the block:

$$v([A\;B]) = A \otimes \sigma(B), \qquad h_i^{l} = v\big(W^{l}[h^{l-1}_{i-k/2}, \dots, h^{l-1}_{i+k/2}] + b^{l}\big) + h_i^{l-1}.$$

The gate \(\sigma(B)\) decides, per channel, how much of the linear path \(A\) passes, the same idea as an LSTM gate without the recurrence. In the decoder, inputs are padded with \(k - 1\) zeros on the left and the convolution never sees future tokens.

Receptive fields grow linearly: each layer adds \(k - 1\) positions, so the paper's example of 6 blocks with \(k = 5\) gives each output a context of 25 input elements.

Multi-step attention. Every decoder layer \(l\) has its own attention. Its query combines the layer's state with the embedding \(g_i\) of the previous target token, \(d_i^l = W_d^l h_i^l + b_d^l + g_i\); it scores the encoder's top-layer outputs \(z_j\) by dot products, \(a_{ij}^l = \operatorname{softmax}_j(d_i^l \cdot z_j)\); and it returns

$$c_i^l = \sum_j a_{ij}^l\, (z_j + e_j),$$

which is added back to \(h_i^l\). Two details are worth noticing: the values include the source input embedding \(e_j\) as well as \(z_j\) (point information next to broad context), and because each layer's context feeds the next layer, higher layers can take into account what lower layers attended to.

Keeping variance under control. There is no layer normalization. Instead the paper uses weight normalization, scales the sum of a residual block and its input by \(\sqrt{0.5}\), rescales attention outputs by a factor that depends on the source length, scales encoder gradients by the number of attention mechanisms that use them, and initializes weights so that activation variance stays roughly constant through layers, taking GLUs and dropout into account. This is a lot of careful engineering, and it is the part that the transformer replaced with residuals plus layer normalization.

Evidence

On WMT'16 English–Romanian the authors report a clear improvement over the previous best result; on WMT'14 English–German and English–French they report beating GNMT at much higher translation speed. Ablations show that multi-step attention beats attention at the last layer only, that deeper encoders help more than deeper decoders, and that the model still works reasonably without position embeddings, which therefore help but are not essential here. The evidence supports "convolutions can match deep LSTMs, faster"; it does not test long-range dependencies beyond a sentence.

Limits & what broke
  • Paths still grow with distance. \(O(n/k)\) layers between distant words is better than \(O(n)\) steps, but self-attention's \(O(1)\) path is better still. That single row of a complexity table is why the transformer won the argument.
  • Fragile normalization. The scaling constants are tuned to this architecture; change the depth or the attention layout and they must be re-derived.
  • Superseded within weeks. The transformer (L129) reported higher BLEU on the same benchmarks at a fraction of the training cost, with ConvS2S in its comparison table.
  • Not the end of convolutions. Wu et al. (2019) later showed that lightweight and dynamic convolutions can match transformer translation quality, so "attention is necessary" was never proved by this race.

Bridge

ConvS2S kills the sequential dependency but keeps a local operator. Vaswani et al. (L129) keep the parallelism, keep the dot-product attention and the position embeddings, drop the convolutions, and connect every position to every other in a single layer.

Attention Is All You Need

90m videoLevel 3THE keystone episode — make it your best

Vaswani et al., NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1706.03762)

Drop recurrence and convolution: build the encoder–decoder from stacked self-attention and position-wise feed-forward layers, with residuals, layer normalization and positional encodings. Every position reaches every other in one step, training parallelizes across the sequence, and translation got better at a fraction of the training cost.

Cold open

This architecture is behind essentially everything in Months 6–12: GPT, BERT, T5, ViT (L115), diffusion transformers. Yet it is a translation paper, headlined by 28.4 BLEU on WMT'14 English→German after 3.5 days on eight GPUs. Notice how little of it was specific to translation.

The problem

Attention (L126, L127) had fixed the bottleneck, but the networks around it were recurrent, so training needed \(n\) sequential steps per sentence. ConvS2S (L128) removed the recurrence but connected distant positions only through many layers. Can attention alone move information between positions?

The method: scaled dot-product attention

Derivation · from Day 126 to one matrix product

In L126 a query scored each key with \(v_a^\top \tanh(W_a s_{i-1} + U_a h_j)\), softmaxed, and averaged the values. Replace the additive score with a dot product of projected vectors \(q_i^\top k_j\) (Luong's bilinear score with a factored matrix). Stack queries, keys and values as rows of \(Q \in \R^{n \times d_k}\), \(K \in \R^{m \times d_k}\), \(V \in \R^{m \times d_v}\): all scores are \(QK^\top\), a row-wise softmax gives the weights, and one more product returns the outputs,

$$\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big) V .$$
Derivation · why divide by √d_k

Let the components of \(q, k \in \R^{d_k}\) be independent with mean 0 and variance 1. Each term \(q_i k_i\) has mean 0 and variance \(\E[q_i^2]\E[k_i^2] = 1\), so

$$\Var\big(q^{\top} k\big) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \Var(q_i k_i) = d_k .$$

Logits with standard deviation \(\sqrt{d_k}\) (8 when \(d_k = 64\)) push the softmax towards one-hot, where its Jacobian \(\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\) vanishes in every entry, and gradients through the weights die. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) restores unit variance. Simulating 10 random keys at \(d_k = 64\), the largest weight averages about 0.87 unscaled, 0.32 scaled. The paper states the mechanism as a suspicion, motivated by additive attention beating unscaled dot products at large \(d_k\).

The method: multi-head attention, with shapes

One attention averages what it sees into one vector per position. Multi-head attention runs \(h\) attentions in parallel in smaller subspaces, so heads can attend to different positions for different reasons:

$$\operatorname{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{Concat}(\text{head}_1, \dots, \text{head}_h) W^O, \qquad \text{head}_i = \operatorname{Attention}(QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V).$$

With \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\) and \(h = 8\), each head has \(d_k = d_v = 64\), so the cost is about that of one full-width head. Shape confusion is where implementations die, so track \((B, h, n, d_k)\):

import numpy as np

def mha(xq, xkv, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, h, causal=False):
    """xq: (B, n, d) source of queries; xkv: (B, m, d) source of keys and values."""
    B, n, d = xq.shape
    m, dk = xkv.shape[1], d // h
    split = lambda t, L: t.reshape(B, L, h, dk).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3)   # (B, h, L, d_k)
    Q, K, V = split(xq @ Wq, n), split(xkv @ Wk, m), split(xkv @ Wv, m)
    s = Q @ K.transpose(0, 1, 3, 2) / np.sqrt(dk)                         # (B, h, n, m)
    if causal:                                                             # -inf before softmax
        s = np.where(np.tril(np.ones((n, m), dtype=bool)), s, -np.inf)
    a = np.exp(s - s.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    a /= a.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)                                     # softmax over keys
    return (a @ V).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(B, n, d) @ Wo             # merge heads, project

Self-attention passes one tensor twice; cross-attention passes decoder states as xq, encoder outputs as xkv. Masked weights are exactly 0.

The method: feed-forward layers and positions

Position-wise FFN. Each position then passes through the same two-layer network: \(\operatorname{FFN}(x) = \max(0, xW_1 + b_1)W_2 + b_2\), \(512 \to 2048 \to 512\). Attention mixes across positions; the FFN transforms each one. Attention has \(4 \cdot 512^2 \approx 1.05\)M weights per layer, the FFN \(2 \cdot 512 \cdot 2048 \approx 2.1\)M: two thirds of each encoder layer (half of a decoder layer, which has two attentions), and two thirds of the non-embedding weights of the decoder-only LMs of Month 6. Interpretability work later finds much of the stored "knowledge" in these layers (L312).

Positional encoding. Self-attention is permutation-equivariant: shuffle the inputs and the outputs shuffle the same way. Order is injected by adding

$$PE_{(pos,\,2i)} = \sin\!\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\big), \qquad PE_{(pos,\,2i+1)} = \cos\!\big(pos / 10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}\big),$$

to the embeddings: a geometric ladder of wavelengths from \(2\pi\) to \(10000 \cdot 2\pi\).

Property · relative offsets are linear maps

For frequency \(\omega_i = 10000^{-2i/d_{\text{model}}}\), angle addition gives

$$\begin{pmatrix} \sin \omega_i(p + k) \\ \cos \omega_i(p + k) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos \omega_i k & \sin \omega_i k \\ -\sin \omega_i k & \cos \omega_i k \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \sin \omega_i p \\ \cos \omega_i p \end{pmatrix}.$$

The matrix depends on \(k\), not \(p\), so \(PE_{p+k} = M_k PE_p\) with \(M_k\) block-diagonal: hence the authors' hypothesis that the model could easily learn to attend by relative position. RoPE (L132) makes this a property of the score itself.

Training details and evidence

a linear warm-up to about \(7 \times 10^{-4}\), then inverse-square-root decay. Dropout 0.1 on every sub-layer output and on the embedding sums; label smoothing 0.1, which the authors note hurts perplexity but improves BLEU. Base: \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\), \(d_{ff} = 2048\), \(h = 8\), about 65M parameters, 100k steps in about 12 hours on 8 P100s. Big: 1024, 4096, \(h = 16\), dropout 0.3 for English–German, about 213M parameters, 300k steps in about 3.5 days.

Limits & what broke
  • What the title claims versus what is removed. Recurrence and convolution go; attention (not new), FFNs (most weights), residuals, layer norm and positional encodings stay. Dong et al. (2021) show pure self-attention without skips and MLPs loses rank doubly exponentially with depth. Attention is all you need instead of recurrence.
  • Quadratic cost in time and memory: fine for sentences, prohibitive for books and genomes (L148–L150, L153).
  • Post-norm needs warm-up. Xiong et al. (2020) trace this to post-LN gradients; pre-LN (GPT-2, L144) trains more stably and became the default.
  • Sinusoids did not extrapolate to longer sequences as hoped (L133), hence RoPE and ALiBi.
  • Generation is still sequential, one token at a time: the origin of KV caching (L338).

Bridge

The English–German vocabulary here is a byte-pair encoding, tomorrow's paper (L130). Then the month splits the architecture three ways: decoder-only (L136), encoder-only (L137), encoder–decoder (L142). Prerequisites: L126–L128.

Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units (BPE)

35m videoLevel 2

Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, ACL 2016 (arXiv:1508.07909)

Learn a vocabulary of subword units by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols, and translate sequences of those units. A fixed vocabulary of tens of thousands of pieces can then spell any word, and translation becomes open-vocabulary.

Cold open

Every NMT system so far (L125, L126) had a vocabulary of 30k–80k words and replaced everything else with UNK. Names, numbers, compounds and inflected forms all collapsed to one symbol, and systems patched the damage afterwards with dictionaries. Sennrich et al. observed that many rare words translate transparently: names by transliteration, German compounds piece by piece (Abwasser|behandlungs|anlage, "sewage water treatment plant"), cognates and loanwords by regular sound and spelling changes. If the units are smaller than words, the network can learn those regularities itself.

The problem

A word-level softmax over the full vocabulary is too expensive, and the full vocabulary is unbounded anyway. Character-level models avoid the problem but make sequences several times longer, which for 2015-era RNNs meant slow training and weaker long-range modelling. Back-off dictionaries need alignments and cannot produce words that were never in the dictionary. The paper wants units that are frequent enough to learn, short enough to cover everything, and long enough to keep sequences manageable.

The method: byte pair encoding

BPE is a 1994 compression algorithm (Gage): repeatedly replace the most frequent pair of adjacent bytes with a new, unused byte. The paper adapts it to words: start from characters, merge symbols rather than bytes, never merge across word boundaries, and add an end-of-word marker so that suffixes are distinguishable.

Algorithm · learning BPE merges
  1. Split every word of the training corpus into characters plus an end-of-word symbol </w>, and count word frequencies.
  2. Count every adjacent symbol pair, weighted by word frequency.
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol everywhere, and append it to the merge list.
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 for a chosen number of merges. The vocabulary is the characters plus one new symbol per merge.
  5. To segment new text, apply the learned merges in the order they were learned.
import collections, re

def learn_bpe(word_counts, num_merges):
    vocab = {" ".join(w) + " </w>": c for w, c in word_counts.items()}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = collections.Counter()
        for word, c in vocab.items():
            syms = word.split()
            for a, b in zip(syms, syms[1:]):
                pairs[a, b] += c
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)          # ties: the pair counted first
        pat = re.compile(r"(?<!\S)" + re.escape(" ".join(best)) + r"(?!\S)")
        vocab = {pat.sub("".join(best), w): c for w, c in vocab.items()}
        merges.append(best)
    return merges, vocab

Vocabulary size is a dial. The number of merges is the only hyperparameter. Evidence

On WMT'15 English→German and English→Russian, the abstract reports improvements of up to 1.1 and 1.3 BLEU over a back-off-dictionary baseline, with the larger gains on rare and unseen words, which the authors measure separately (unigram F1 on rare words and on OOVs). Subword systems also produce words that never appeared in the training data, for example new compounds, which no word-level system can do. The comparison includes character-bigram segmentation; BPE gives a better balance of vocabulary size and sequence length.

Limits & what broke
  • Frozen statistics. The merge list is learned once and fixed. It encodes the training corpus's language mix and domain permanently; text from elsewhere is split into many short pieces.
  • No notion of morphemes. Merges follow frequency, not linguistics, so splits are often unintuitive (low e r in the toy run; real tokenizers produce odd pieces for rarer words and treat the same word differently with and without a leading space). Bostrom & Durrett (2020) found that a unigram LM tokenizer (L131) aligns better with morphology.
  • Tokenization explains many LLM failures. Numbers split into irregular chunks hurt arithmetic, which is why some later models split digits individually. A model that sees tokens cannot easily count letters. And text in languages underrepresented in the tokenizer's corpus needs several times more tokens for the same content, so it costs more and fits less in context (Petrov et al. 2023).
  • Byte-level BPE. Unicode has too many characters to use as a base vocabulary. GPT-2 (L144) starts from the 256 byte values, so no input is ever unknown, and blocks merges across character categories to avoid tokens like dog. and dog!.

Bridge

BPE assumes the text is already split into words on whitespace, is greedy, and gives every word exactly one segmentation. Tomorrow's papers (L131) remove each assumption: SentencePiece works on raw text including spaces, WordPiece picks merges by likelihood, and the unigram model samples segmentations as a regularizer.

SentencePiece + WordPiece

35m videoLevel 2

Kudo & Richardson EMNLP 2018 (arXiv:1808.06226); Kudo ACL 2018 (arXiv:1804.10959)

Three refinements of subword tokenization. SentencePiece tokenizes raw text, spaces included, so it is language-independent and lossless; WordPiece chooses merges by likelihood gain instead of raw frequency; and the unigram language-model tokenizer works top-down, pruning a large vocabulary with EM, and can sample segmentations as a regularizer.

Cold open

Tokenize "Hello world." the classic way: a rule-based pre-tokenizer splits off punctuation, giving Hello world ., and BPE (L130) runs inside each word. Now detokenize: was there a space before the full stop? The information is gone, and the detokenizer has to guess with more language-specific rules. In Japanese or Chinese, which have no spaces between words, the pre-tokenizer itself is a word segmenter, a model in its own right. SentencePiece writes the same sentence as ▁Hello ▁world . and decoding is just concatenation with ▁ turned back into a space.

The problem

BPE as published assumes whitespace-separated words, a language-specific pre-tokenizer and detokenizer, a frequency-based greedy merge rule, and exactly one segmentation per word. Each assumption causes trouble: pipelines are hard to reproduce across languages, decoding is lossy, frequency is not the same as usefulness, and a model trained on one segmentation is brittle to others. The two Kudo papers attack these assumptions, and WordPiece, from Google's speech and translation systems, is the variant BERT made famous.

The method: SentencePiece

Definition · lossless tokenization

SentencePiece treats the input as a raw stream of Unicode characters and represents whitespace as an ordinary symbol, the meta character ▁ (U+2581). After an explicit normalization step (NFKC by default), encoding and decoding satisfy

$$\operatorname{Decode}(\operatorname{Encode}(\operatorname{Normalize}(T))) = \operatorname{Normalize}(T).$$

No pre-tokenizer, no detokenizer, and the vocabulary size is fixed in advance rather than as a number of merges. The model file stores the normalization rules and the vocabulary, so the same file reproduces the same tokenization anywhere. It implements both BPE and the unigram model below.

Be precise about reversibility: SentencePiece reconstructs the normalized input exactly, and BPE behind a word-level pre-tokenizer cannot reconstruct the original at all, because the pre-tokenizer threw away spacing. (Byte-level BPE, as in GPT-2, is also reversible, for the same reason: it encodes spaces as ordinary bytes.)

The method: WordPiece's likelihood criterion

WordPiece (Schuster & Nakajima 2012, later used in Google's NMT system and in BERT, L137) builds its vocabulary bottom-up like BPE, but at each step adds the candidate unit that most increases the likelihood of the training data under a language model over the current units. A first-order calculation shows what that prefers.

Derivation · what a likelihood-based merge rewards

Under a unigram model over units with counts \(c_u\) and total \(N\), the training log-likelihood is \(F = \sum_u c_u \log c_u - N \log N\). Merging \(a, b\) into \(ab\), which occurs \(p\) times, adds a unit with count \(p\), lowers \(c_a\) and \(c_b\) by \(p\), and lowers \(N\) by \(p\). For \(p\) small relative to \(c_a, c_b\), using \(\frac{d}{dc}(c \log c) = \log c + 1\):

$$\Delta F \approx p \log p - p(\log c_a + 1) - p(\log c_b + 1) + p(\log N + 1) = p\Big(\log \frac{p\,N}{c_a c_b} - 1\Big).$$

That is the pair count times its pointwise mutual information (minus a constant). BPE ranks by \(p\) alone, so it happily merges two very frequent symbols that merely co-occur often; the likelihood criterion prefers pairs whose parts rarely appear apart. Popular descriptions score pairs by \(c_{ab}/(c_a c_b)\), which keeps the PMI-like part.

At inference, BERT's WordPiece tokenizer splits each whitespace-separated word by greedy longest-match-first against the vocabulary, marking word-internal pieces with ## (playing → play ##ing).

The method: the unigram LM tokenizer and subword regularization

Kudo (2018) models a segmentation \(\mathbf x = (x_1, \dots, x_M)\) of a sentence \(X\) as independent draws, \(P(\mathbf x) = \prod_i p(x_i)\) with \(\sum_{x \in \mathcal V} p(x) = 1\). The best segmentation is \(\mathbf x^* = \argmax_{\mathbf x \in S(X)} P(\mathbf x)\), found by Viterbi over the lattice of all segmentations.

Algorithm · learning a unigram vocabulary (top-down)
  1. Start from a large seed vocabulary: all characters plus many frequent substrings.
  2. With the vocabulary fixed, fit \(p(x)\) by EM to maximize the marginal likelihood \(\mathcal L = \sum_s \log \sum_{\mathbf x \in S(X^{(s)})} P(\mathbf x)\), treating the segmentation as latent.
  3. For each piece, compute how much \(\mathcal L\) would drop if it were removed.
  4. Keep the top fraction (for example 80%) of pieces by that loss, always keeping single characters.
  5. Repeat 2–4 until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

BPE and WordPiece grow a vocabulary by merging (bottom-up); the unigram model shrinks one by pruning (top-down), and it keeps a full probability model over segmentations. That model enables subword regularization: during training, sample a segmentation from \(P(\mathbf x \mid X)\), sharpened or flattened by an exponent \(\alpha\), so the network sees many segmentations of the same sentence. It is data augmentation at the input-unit level.

Evidence

Kudo & Richardson report that SentencePiece trained directly on raw sentences matches pipelines built on language-specific pre-tokenizers in translation quality, and they document its speed. Kudo (2018) reports consistent BLEU gains from subword regularization across several language pairs, largest in low-resource and out-of-domain settings, where brittleness to a single segmentation hurts most. The same paper finds the unigram model without sampling roughly on par with BPE, so the gain comes from the sampling, not from the segmentation model alone.

Limits & what broke
  • Real gains, little use. Subword regularization helps, and so does its BPE analogue, BPE-dropout (Provilkov et al. 2020), which randomly skips merges. Yet large-scale LLM pretraining almost always uses one deterministic tokenization, partly for simplicity and partly because at that data scale the regularization benefit is less clear.
  • "Lossless" means after normalization. NFKC folds some characters together (full-width forms, ligatures), so the original bytes are not always recoverable.
  • WordPiece is under-documented. The original description is brief and Google did not release BERT's vocabulary-learning code, so "WordPiece" in libraries is a reconstruction; check which variant you are using.
  • All three inherit the fixed-vocabulary problems of L130: frozen corpus statistics and uneven cost across languages.

Bridge

Tokens are settled; positions are next. The transformer's sinusoids (L129) only hint at relative position. RoPE (L132) builds relative position directly into the query–key dot product, and it is the scheme in nearly every LLM you use.

RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding (RoPE)

45m videoLevel 3

Su, Lu, Pan, Wen & Liu, 2021 (arXiv:2104.09864)

Rotate each query and key by an angle proportional to its own absolute position. Rotations compose, so the query–key dot product depends only on the relative offset: relative in effect, absolute in application, and nearly free. The base of the frequency ladder (10000) then becomes the knob for extending context.

Cold open

This is the positional encoding in nearly every LLM you use: the LLaMA family, Mistral, Qwen, PaLM, GPT-NeoX. It replaced the transformer's sinusoids (L129) without adding a single learned parameter, and "RoPE scaling" now appears in every long-context release note. The core of it is a five-line derivation.

The problem

Absolute encodings are added to the token embeddings, so after projection the attention score mixes content and position in four cross terms, and relative position appears only indirectly (Transformer-XL makes the four terms explicit, L148). Relative schemes (Shaw et al. 2018, Transformer-XL, T5's bias buckets, L142) modify the score with an extra term per query–key pair, which complicates the attention kernel and does not fit linear-attention variants. The design requirement RoFormer states:

$$\big\langle f_q(x_m, m),\; f_k(x_n, n) \big\rangle = g(x_m, x_n, m - n),$$

an encoding applied to each vector separately whose inner product depends on positions only through \(m - n\).

The method: the 2-D case

Derivation · rotations make the score relative

Take \(d = 2\), let \(q = W_q x_m\), \(k = W_k x_n\), and write \(R(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix}\). Define

$$f_q(x_m, m) = R(m\theta)\, q, \qquad f_k(x_n, n) = R(n\theta)\, k .$$

Rotations satisfy \(R(a)^{\top} = R(-a)\) and \(R(a)R(b) = R(a + b)\), so

$$\big(R(m\theta) q\big)^{\top}\big(R(n\theta) k\big) = q^{\top} R(m\theta)^{\top} R(n\theta)\, k = q^{\top} R\big((n - m)\theta\big)\, k .$$

The absolute positions have cancelled; only \(n - m\) remains. In complex notation, with \(\tilde q = q_1 + i q_2\), the encoding is \(\tilde q\, e^{i m\theta}\) and the score is \(\operatorname{Re}\big[\tilde q\, \overline{\tilde k}\, e^{i(m - n)\theta}\big]\), which is how the paper writes it.

The method: d dimensions, and the implementation

Split the \(d\) dimensions of each head into \(d/2\) pairs and rotate pair \(i\) at its own frequency, \(\theta_i = 10000^{-2(i-1)/d}\). The rotation is a block-diagonal matrix \(R^{d}_{\Theta, m}\), and the score becomes \(x_m^{\top} W_q^{\top} R^{d}_{\Theta, n-m} W_k x_n\). Nobody builds the matrix; each pair is rotated elementwise.

import numpy as np

def rope(x, pos, base=10000.0):
    """x: (n, d) queries or keys of one head, d even; pos: (n,) positions."""
    d = x.shape[-1]
    theta = base ** (-np.arange(0, d, 2) / d)         # (d/2,) one frequency per pair
    ang = pos[:, None] * theta[None, :]               # (n, d/2)
    cos, sin = np.cos(ang), np.sin(ang)
    x1, x2 = x[:, 0::2], x[:, 1::2]                   # interleaved pairs, as in the paper
    out = np.empty_like(x)
    out[:, 0::2] = x1 * cos - x2 * sin
    out[:, 1::2] = x1 * sin + x2 * cos
    return out

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
q, k = rng.normal(size=(1, 64)), rng.normal(size=(1, 64))
s1 = rope(q, np.array([3])) @ rope(k, np.array([10])).T
s2 = rope(q, np.array([103])) @ rope(k, np.array([110])).T
print(np.allclose(s1, s2))                            # True: only n - m = 7 matters

Three facts to say out loud. It is applied to Q and K only, never V: position should decide who attends to whom, not alter the content being moved; rotating values would make the output depend on absolute positions. It is relative in effect but absolute in application: each token is rotated using only its own index, so there is no \(n \times n\) table, the cost is \(O(nd)\), rotated keys can sit in a KV cache unchanged, and it is compatible with linear attention, which the paper points out. It is applied inside every attention layer, after the projections, rather than once at the input.

The θ base as a frequency schedule

Pair \(i\) completes a full turn every \(2\pi / \theta_i = 2\pi \cdot 10000^{2(i-1)/d}\) positions. The first pairs turn quickly and resolve neighbouring tokens; the last turn so slowly that over a few thousand tokens they barely move and act as nearly position-free channels. The paper also derives a long-term decay: an upper bound on the score's magnitude, for fixed content, shrinks as \(|m - n|\) grows. Note that it is a bound for this frequency schedule, not a guarantee that every head attends locally.

Evidence

The paper's own experiments are modest: a small BLEU gain over the transformer baseline on WMT'14 English–German, faster convergence of the masked-LM loss in BERT-style pretraining, mixed GLUE results, and gains on a Chinese long-document task when the maximum length is raised. Adoption came from elsewhere: EleutherAI's comparisons in 2021 found faster convergence in GPT-style models, after which GPT-J, GPT-NeoX, PaLM and LLaMA used it. RoPE's case is simplicity and good behaviour at scale more than any single table in this paper.

Context extension: changing the clock

A model trained to 4k positions has never seen the rotation angles of position 20k, and its perplexity explodes there. The fixes all edit the angles:

  • Position interpolation (Chen et al. 2023) rescales positions, \(m \to m L / L'\), so a longer context maps into the trained range; a short fine-tune recovers quality. It compresses every frequency equally, which blurs the fine-grained pairs.
  • NTK-aware scaling (proposed in the open-source community in 2023) raises the base instead, \(b \to b \cdot s^{d/(d-2)}\) for a scale factor \(s\), leaving the fast pairs almost unchanged and stretching the slow ones.
  • YaRN (Peng et al. 2023) interpolates differently per wavelength band and adds an attention temperature, extending context with little fine-tuning data.
  • Many models simply train with a larger base during a long-context stage, which is why release notes list base values in the hundreds of thousands or more.
Limits & what broke
  • No free extrapolation. Without scaling, RoPE models degrade beyond their training length; the ALiBi paper (L133) measured rotary extrapolating only a little further than sinusoids.
  • Two layouts. The paper rotates interleaved pairs \((x_1, x_2), (x_3, x_4), \dots\); GPT-NeoX-style code rotates the first half against the second half. They are equivalent up to a fixed permutation of dimensions, but loading weights trained with one into code using the other silently breaks the model.
  • Precision. Angles \(m\theta_i\) for large \(m\) lose precision in fp16/bf16; compute them in fp32.
  • Variants. Some models rotate only a fraction of each head's dimensions; check the config before assuming full RoPE.

Bridge

RoPE makes position relative but still needs tricks to go beyond the trained length. ALiBi (L133) asks for extrapolation directly: no embeddings at all, just a fixed penalty proportional to distance added to each score.

Train Short, Test Long: ALiBi

30m videoLevel 2

Press, Smith & Lewis, ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2108.12409)

Use no position embeddings at all. Add a fixed, head-specific penalty proportional to the query–key distance to every attention score, and a language model trained on short sequences keeps its perplexity on much longer ones.

Cold open

The abstract's headline: a 1.3B-parameter model trained on sequences of 1,024 tokens, evaluated on 2,048, matches the perplexity of a sinusoidal model trained on 2,048, while training about 11% faster and using about 11% less memory. The method that does this has no learned parameters and fits in one line.

The problem

Training cost grows with sequence length, so it would be useful to train short and run long. The paper names the property extrapolation: performance on inputs longer than any seen in training, as opposed to interpolation within the trained range. Measured directly, the transformer's sinusoids (L129) fail almost as soon as the input exceeds the training length, rotary embeddings (L132) extrapolate a little further, and T5's learned relative bias (L142) further still but at a noticeable speed cost. The authors argue the position method, not the architecture, is the culprit.

The method

Definition · attention with linear biases

For query position \(i\) in a causal model, the scores over keys \(j \le i\) become

$$\operatorname{softmax}\Big(q_i K^{\top} + m \cdot \big[-(i-1),\, \dots,\, -2,\, -1,\, 0\big]\Big),$$

that is, \(q_i^{\top} k_j - m\,(i - j)\). Nothing is added to the input embeddings. Each head has its own fixed slope \(m\); for \(n\) heads the slopes form the geometric sequence

$$m_h = 2^{-8h/n}, \qquad h = 1, \dots, n,$$

so 8 heads get \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \dots, \tfrac1{256}\), and 16 heads get \(2^{-0.5}, 2^{-1}, \dots, 2^{-8}\).

Exponentiate to see what it does: the unnormalized attention weight becomes \(e^{q_i^\top k_j} \cdot e^{-m(i-j)}\), an exponential discount on distance. A head with \(m = \tfrac12\) effectively looks at the last few tokens; a head with \(m = \tfrac1{256}\) can still reach hundreds of tokens back. The bias is the same at every length, so a longer input simply presents more, increasingly discounted, keys; there is no position the model has "never seen".

Evidence

On WikiText-103, the authors train models at several lengths and evaluate on longer inputs. Sinusoidal models' perplexity rises soon after the training length; ALiBi models' perplexity holds steady or improves as evaluation length grows to several times the training length (the extrapolation plots go to 3,072 tokens and beyond for models trained on 512 or 1,024). The same pattern appears for 1.3B-parameter models on a much larger web-and-books corpus. They also ablate the slopes: the geometric schedule works across head counts without tuning, and making the slopes learnable did not give strong extrapolation, so they kept them fixed.

Read the analysis section too. The authors examine the "early token curse": when long text is evaluated in non-overlapping chunks, the first tokens of each chunk have almost no context, and longer chunks reduce that penalty. Their analysis suggests that much of ALiBi's improvement at longer evaluation lengths comes from this effect rather than from exploiting information hundreds of tokens away. That is an honest and important qualifier: ALiBi does not break at long lengths, but that is not the same as using long context.

Limits & what broke
  • A hard-coded recency prior. The penalty is monotone in distance, so far tokens are always discounted. That suits language modelling, where most predictive information is local, and is wrong for tasks that need one specific distant token, such as retrieving a passage or a key buried in a long prompt.
  • Graceful is not useful. Flat perplexity at 3× length shows the model is not harmed by extra context; whether it uses it needs retrieval-style tests, which later long-context work made standard.
  • Where it went. Some large models adopted ALiBi (BLOOM and MPT, for example), while the LLaMA line and most later models chose RoPE plus context-extension tricks (L132). Present the choice as a real, still-live trade-off: ALiBi gives robust length generalization with no tuning; RoPE with scaling and long-context fine-tuning gives better use of distant tokens.
  • Kernel support. A per-head additive bias must be supported by the attention kernel; fused kernels added it later than plain causal masking.

Bridge

Positions and tokens are done; the architecture pieces of the transformer are in place. The second half of the month is about what you train it on and how. It starts one step before transformers took over pretraining: ELMo (L134), which showed that a language model's internal states make better word vectors than any static embedding.

Deep Contextualized Word Representations (ELMo)

35m videoLevel 2

Peters et al., NAACL 2018 (arXiv:1802.05365)

Run a large bidirectional LSTM language model over the sentence and represent each word by a learned, task-specific weighted sum of all of the model's layers. Context-dependent vectors, added to existing task models with the language model frozen, improved the state of the art on six quite different tasks.

Cold open

The same word, different vectors. GloVe's nearest neighbours of play are a mixture of senses (playing, game, players). The paper shows that ELMo's vector for play in a sentence about a baseball fielder is closest to other sports uses of play, and in a sentence about a Broadway contract it is closest to theatrical uses. Polysemy, the ceiling of every static embedding from L121 to L124, is gone, and the language model learned it without any sense labels.

The problem

Static vectors give a word one representation regardless of context. Earlier contextual attempts used the top layer of a translation encoder (CoVe, McCann et al. 2017) or of a language model (TagLM, Peters et al. 2017). ELMo's two claims are that language-model pretraining is the right source, since unlabeled text is unlimited, and that all layers matter, not just the top one, because different layers encode different kinds of information.

The method

Definition · the biLM objective

A forward LM models \(p(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1})\) and a backward LM models \(p(t_k \mid t_{k+1}, \dots, t_N)\). ELMo maximizes their joint log-likelihood,

$$\sum_{k=1}^{N} \Big( \log p(t_k \mid t_1, \dots, t_{k-1};\, \Theta_x, \overrightarrow{\Theta}_{\text{LSTM}}, \Theta_s) + \log p(t_k \mid t_{k+1}, \dots, t_N;\, \Theta_x, \overleftarrow{\Theta}_{\text{LSTM}}, \Theta_s) \Big),$$

tying the token representation \(\Theta_x\) and the softmax \(\Theta_s\) across directions while keeping separate LSTM parameters for each.

The released model reads characters: a character CNN with highway layers produces each token's input vector (so there is no out-of-vocabulary problem), followed by two biLSTM layers with 4,096 units and 512-dimensional projections and a residual connection between them. It was trained on the One Billion Word Benchmark. For each token \(k\) an \(L\)-layer biLM yields \(L + 1\) vectors: \(h_{k,0}\), the token layer, and \(h_{k,j} = [\overrightarrow{h}_{k,j}; \overleftarrow{h}_{k,j}]\) for \(j = 1, \dots, L\).

Definition · the task-specific scalar mix $$\text{ELMo}_k^{\text{task}} = \gamma^{\text{task}} \sum_{j=0}^{L} s_j^{\text{task}}\, h_{k,j}, \qquad s^{\text{task}} = \operatorname{softmax}(w^{\text{task}}).$$

Each downstream task learns \(L + 1\) mixing weights and one scale \(\gamma\), which the authors found important for optimization because biLM activations and task-model activations have different distributions. That is the entire interface: a handful of scalars per task.

This is the feature-based recipe: pretrain, freeze, extract features, and keep each task's own architecture (a BiDAF-style model for SQuAD, an ESIM-style model for SNLI, and so on). The alternative, fine-tuning the pretrained network itself, arrives in the next three lessons.

Evidence

Six tasks: question answering (SQuAD), textual entailment (SNLI), semantic role labelling, coreference resolution, named-entity recognition (CoNLL 2003) and fine-grained sentiment (SST-5). Adding ELMo to a strong baseline improved every one, with absolute gains mostly in the 2–5 point range (under one point on SNLI, close to five F1 on SQuAD); the authors summarize them as relative error reductions of roughly 6% to 20%. Ablations show that a learned mix over all layers beats using the top layer alone, which in turn beats no ELMo.

The probing results explain why. Used directly for word-sense disambiguation by nearest neighbour, the top biLSTM layer does better than the first; used for part-of-speech tagging, the first layer does better than the top. Lower layers carry more syntax, higher layers more context-dependent meaning, so different tasks want different mixtures, which is exactly what \(s^{\text{task}}\) learns. The paper also reports that ELMo makes task models far more sample-efficient, reaching the baseline's performance with a fraction of the training data or updates.

Limits & what broke
  • Two one-sided models, not one two-sided model. The forward LSTM never conditions on the right context and the backward one never on the left; they are concatenated after the fact. BERT's masked LM (L137) conditions every layer on both sides at once, and its paper calls ELMo's approach a shallow concatenation.
  • Feature-based costs. The frozen LM cannot adapt to the task, and every task still needs its own architecture. ULMFiT (L135) and GPT (L136) fine-tune the whole network instead, with a trivial task head.
  • LSTMs. A two-layer 4,096-unit biLSTM with a character CNN is slow to run, and the recurrence limits how far it scales; transformers took over within a year.
  • What survived. The learned scalar mix is a simple idea worth stealing: probing studies of BERT later used the same per-layer weighting to see which layers a task relies on.

Bridge

ELMo is the "pretraining works for NLP" proof of concept, but it keeps the pretrained model at arm's length. ULMFiT (L135), published the same year, fine-tunes the language model itself and supplies the playbook for doing it without destroying what pretraining learned.

Universal Language Model Fine-tuning (ULMFiT)

35m videoLevel 2

Howard & Ruder, ACL 2018 (arXiv:1801.06146)

Fine-tune a pretrained language model in three stages (general-domain pretraining, language-model fine-tuning on the target text, then classifier fine-tuning), with per-layer learning rates, a slanted triangular schedule and gradual unfreezing to avoid catastrophic forgetting. The playbook for transfer learning in NLP, written before it was standard.

Cold open

The abstract's claim: with only 100 labelled examples, ULMFiT matches the performance of training from scratch on 100 times more data. In early 2018 that was a provocative statement about NLP. Computer vision had fine-tuned ImageNet models for years; NLP transferred only its first layer, the word vectors, and the common belief was that fine-tuning a whole language model on a small dataset would simply overfit or forget.

The problem

Earlier attempts to fine-tune language models (for example Dai & Le 2015) needed large amounts of in-domain data, and fine-tuning aggressively erased what pretraining had learned: catastrophic forgetting, the failure mode where updating a network on a new task destroys its old capabilities. ELMo (L134) sidesteps the issue by freezing the LM. Howard & Ruder argue that the problem was not fine-tuning itself but how it was done, and give a procedure that makes it reliable.

The method: three stages

Algorithm · ULMFiT
  1. General-domain LM pretraining. Train an AWD-LSTM (a 3-layer LSTM with heavy dropout regularization, no attention) as a language model on Wikitext-103, about 103M words. Done once, reused for every task.
  2. Target-task LM fine-tuning. Continue language-model training on the task's own text, without labels, so the model adapts to the target domain's vocabulary and style. Use discriminative fine-tuning and slanted triangular learning rates.
  3. Classifier fine-tuning. Add two linear blocks (batch norm, dropout, ReLU, then softmax) on top of a concatenation of the last hidden state, a max-pool and a mean-pool over all hidden states. Train with gradual unfreezing, plus the same two techniques.

Stage 2 is the underrated one. It needs no labels, costs a fraction of pretraining, and moves the model towards the target distribution before any label is seen. Later work confirmed the effect at transformer scale: Gururangan et al. (2020) found that continued pretraining on domain and task text gives consistent gains even for strong pretrained models. It is still free performance that many projects skip.

The method: three tricks against forgetting

Definition · discriminative fine-tuning and STLR

Discriminative fine-tuning gives each layer \(l\) its own learning rate, \(\theta^l_t = \theta^l_{t-1} - \eta^l \nabla_{\theta^l} J(\theta)\), with \(\eta^{l-1} = \eta^l / 2.6\): lower layers, which hold more general features, move less.

Slanted triangular learning rates rise linearly for a short fraction of training and then decay linearly for the rest. With \(T\) iterations, \(\mathit{cut} = \lfloor T \cdot \mathit{cut\_frac} \rfloor\),

$$p = \begin{cases} t / \mathit{cut} & t < \mathit{cut} \\ 1 - \dfrac{t - \mathit{cut}}{\mathit{cut} \cdot (1/\mathit{cut\_frac} - 1)} & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} \qquad \eta_t = \eta_{\max} \cdot \frac{1 + p\,(\mathit{ratio} - 1)}{\mathit{ratio}},$$

with \(\mathit{cut\_frac} = 0.1\), \(\mathit{ratio} = 32\) and \(\eta_{\max} = 0.01\). The schedule starts and ends at \(\eta_{\max}/32\).

Gradual unfreezing trains only the last layer for one epoch, then unfreezes the next lower layer and trains all unfrozen layers for another epoch, and so on until the whole network is training.

Evidence

Six text-classification datasets: IMDb and Yelp sentiment (binary and five-class), TREC-6 question classification, and AG News and DBpedia topic classification. The authors report error reductions of 18–24% over the previous state of the art on most of them, with one architecture and one set of hyperparameters. The data-efficiency experiment is the memorable one: with 100 labelled examples (and, in the semi-supervised setting, the task's unlabelled text for stage 2), ULMFiT matched from-scratch models trained on 100 times as many labels. The ablations remove one component at a time and show each helping, with stage 2 and the three fine-tuning tricks mattering most on the smaller datasets.

Limits & what broke
  • A fussy recipe. The factor 2.6, the schedule constants, five kinds of dropout and the unfreezing order are all tuned by hand. Modern transformer fine-tuning replaced most of it with "AdamW, short warm-up, low learning rate, a few epochs", so present ULMFiT as historically pivotal rather than currently optimal.
  • Classification only. The paper covers text classification; sequence labelling and generation were left open.
  • LSTM backbone. AWD-LSTM was the right choice for 2018 but scales poorly. The same year, GPT (L136) ran the same pretrain-then-fine-tune idea on a transformer and covered many more task types.
  • What survived. Stage 2 (domain-adaptive LM training), and the idea that fine-tuning is a procedure that needs care rather than a single step. Layer-wise learning-rate decay reappears in later BERT-style fine-tuning practice.

Bridge

ULMFiT showed that fine-tuning a whole pretrained LM works. GPT (L136) swaps the LSTM for a decoder-only transformer, pretrains on books, and handles entailment, similarity and multiple choice by rewriting each task's input as a single token sequence.

Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training (GPT-1)

40m videoLevel 2

Radford, Narasimhan, Salimans & Sutskever, 2018

Pretrain a 12-layer decoder-only transformer as a language model on books, then fine-tune the same network on each task with a linear head and an auxiliary language-modelling loss, rewriting structured inputs as single token sequences. One task-agnostic model improved the state of the art on 9 of the 12 benchmarks it was tested on.

Cold open

Put the paper's Figure 2 (right) on screen. With no fine-tuning at all, the authors use the pretrained LM through simple heuristics (for sentiment, append the word very and compare the probabilities of positive and negative) and plot performance on four tasks against the number of pretraining updates. Every curve rises steadily, and the transformer's curves are higher and steadier than an LSTM's. The implication, which GPT-2 and GPT-3 (L144, L145) turn into the whole method: language-model pretraining is quietly learning to do the tasks.

The problem

ELMo (L134) supplied frozen features but each task kept its own architecture; ULMFiT (L135) fine-tuned an LSTM but only for classification. The goal here is one pretrained network that transfers to entailment, similarity, question answering and classification with almost no architectural change, and a backbone that captures longer-range structure than an LSTM: the transformer decoder (L129) with its cross-attention removed.

The method

Definition · pretraining and fine-tuning objectives

Pretraining on an unlabelled corpus \(\mathcal U = (u_1, \dots, u_n)\) maximizes the standard LM likelihood with context window \(k\):

$$L_1(\mathcal U) = \sum_i \log P(u_i \mid u_{i-k}, \dots, u_{i-1}; \Theta), \qquad h_0 = U W_e + W_p,\quad P(u) = \operatorname{softmax}(h_n W_e^{\top}).$$

Fine-tuning on labelled data \(\mathcal C\) feeds the input tokens \(x^1, \dots, x^m\) through the pretrained model and applies a linear layer to the last token's final state, \(P(y \mid x^1, \dots, x^m) = \operatorname{softmax}(h_l^m W_y)\). The fine-tuning loss keeps the LM objective as an auxiliary term:

$$L_3(\mathcal C) = L_2(\mathcal C) + \lambda\, L_1(\mathcal C), \qquad \lambda = 0.5 .$$

The model: 12 layers, 768-dimensional states, 12 heads, 3,072-unit feed-forward layers, learned position embeddings, GELU activations, a 512-token context and a 40,000-merge BPE vocabulary, about 117M parameters in all. Pretraining ran for 100 epochs on minibatches of 64 sequences of 512 tokens, with Adam, a warm-up over the first 2,000 updates to a peak of \(2.5 \times 10^{-4}\), and cosine decay. The corpus is BooksCorpus, about 7,000 unpublished books, chosen because it contains long stretches of contiguous text, unlike sentence-shuffled corpora, so the model has to learn long-range structure. Fine-tuning is short: typically 3 epochs, batch 32, learning rate \(6.25 \times 10^{-5}\).

The method: traversal-style input transformations

This is how you did multi-task transfer before prompting existed: change the input, not the network. The contrast with GPT-3 (L145) is the story of the next two years. There the task is also written into the input, but in natural language, and nothing is fine-tuned at all.

Evidence

Twelve datasets: natural-language inference (SNLI, MultiNLI, QNLI, SciTail, RTE), question answering and commonsense reasoning (RACE, Story Cloze), semantic similarity (MRPC, QQP, STS-B) and classification (SST-2, CoLA). The abstract reports new state-of-the-art results on 9 of the 12, including absolute improvements of 8.9% on commonsense reasoning (Story Cloze), 5.7% on question answering (RACE) and 1.5% on textual entailment (MultiNLI), beating ensembles and task-specific architectures. The ablations are worth reading: the auxiliary LM objective helps on the larger datasets but not the smaller ones; an LSTM in the same framework is clearly worse on average; removing pretraining costs far more than either; and transferring more pretrained layers keeps helping, so every layer carries useful features.

Limits & what broke
  • Left-to-right only. Every token sees only its left context, a handicap for understanding tasks where the meaning of a word depends on what follows. BERT (L137) beat these numbers months later by making the encoder bidirectional.
  • Still one fine-tuned model per task. The input transformations are clever plumbing, but each task needs labels and a separate copy of the network.
  • BooksCorpus provenance. The corpus was scraped from a self-publishing site, and a later retrospective datasheet (Bandy & Vincent 2021) documented duplicated books, a strong genre skew and licensing problems. A famous pretraining corpus whose contents were never carefully documented is a recurring pattern.
  • Why decoder-only paid off anyway. A causal LM learns from every token, can generate text, and has one objective for pretraining and use. That decision is what scaled into GPT-2, GPT-3 and the models of Month 6, while the bidirectional encoders it lost to in 2018 plateaued.

Bridge

GPT-1's weakness is exactly its unidirectionality. BERT (L137) keeps the pretrain-then-fine-tune recipe but trains a bidirectional transformer encoder with a masked-language-model objective, and sets eleven new records.

BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers

55m videoLevel 2

Devlin, Chang, Lee & Toutanova, NAACL 2019 (arXiv:1810.04805)

Pretrain a deep bidirectional transformer encoder by masking tokens and predicting them from both sides, plus a next-sentence task, then fine-tune the whole model with one small output layer per task. Eleven new state-of-the-art results, and the field reorganized around pretrain-then-fine-tune.

Cold open

The abstract lists the damage: GLUE to 80.5 (a 7.7-point absolute improvement), MultiNLI accuracy to 86.7, SQuAD v1.1 test F1 to 93.2 and SQuAD v2.0 test F1 to 83.1, eleven tasks in all, each with the same pretrained network and a different one-layer head. Within a year "a BERT" was the default starting point for almost every NLP system.

The problem

GPT (L136) pretrains left to right, so each token's representation ignores everything after it, a handicap for understanding tasks such as question answering, where the answer span's meaning depends on both sides. ELMo (L134) only concatenates two one-sided models. Why not train a bidirectional language model directly? Because in a multi-layer bidirectional network each word could see itself through the other positions' representations, and predicting a word you can see is trivial. The fix is to hide the words being predicted.

The method: masked language modelling

Definition · MLM with the 80/10/10 corruption

Choose 15% of the WordPiece token positions at random. For each chosen position, replace the token with [MASK] 80% of the time, with a random token 10% of the time, and leave it unchanged 10% of the time. The model predicts the original token at the chosen positions only, with a cross-entropy loss; all other positions contribute nothing to the MLM loss.

Each part of the 80/10/10 scheme exists for a reason, and explaining them is the point of the episode. The mismatch: [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning time, so a model trained only on [MASK] positions might learn useful representations only where it sees that symbol. Unchanged 10%: because some chosen tokens are left as they are, the model cannot assume the observed token is irrelevant to the prediction, which biases representations towards the actual word. Random 10%: because some observed tokens are wrong, it cannot simply copy the input either, so it must keep a contextual representation of every token, not knowing which ones will be tested. The random replacements touch only 1.5% of all tokens, which the authors argue does not harm language understanding. Their appendix compares other mixtures; 80/10/10 does well on both fine-tuning and feature-based evaluation, and using [MASK] alone hurts the feature-based setting most.

There is a real cost: only 15% of tokens produce a training signal per sequence, against 100% for a left-to-right LM. The paper's appendix shows MLM converging slightly more slowly in pretraining steps than a left-to-right model, while overtaking it in downstream accuracy almost immediately. ELECTRA (L140) is built around fixing this inefficiency.

The method: next-sentence prediction, input format, sizes

Next-sentence prediction trains the [CLS] state to classify whether segment B actually followed segment A in the corpus (50% of the time) or is a random sentence from elsewhere (50%). The authors added it for tasks that relate two sentences, such as QA and NLI. Pretraining used BooksCorpus (about 800M words) and English Wikipedia (about 2,500M words), sequences of up to 512 tokens (90% of steps at length 128 for speed), batches of 256 sequences, 1M steps, and Adam with warm-up and linear decay. BERT-base has 12 layers, hidden size 768 and 12 heads (110M parameters, chosen to match GPT's size); BERT-large has 24 layers, 1,024 and 16 heads (340M).

Fine-tuning adds almost nothing: a linear layer on [CLS] for classification, per-token layers for tagging, and for SQuAD two learned vectors \(S\) and \(E\) that score each token as a span start or end, with span score \(S \cdot T_i + E \cdot T_j\). Everything is trained end to end. The recommended search is small: batch size 16 or 32, learning rate 5e-5, 3e-5 or 2e-5, and 2 to 4 epochs.

Evidence and ablations

On GLUE, BERT-large improved the previous best average (GPT's) by a wide margin, and BERT-base already beat GPT at the same size, which isolates bidirectionality from scale. The ablation table (BERT-base) asks which component mattered. Removing NSP lowered QNLI noticeably, and MNLI and SQuAD slightly, in their setup. A left-to-right model without NSP, essentially GPT's objective on BERT's data, was much worse, especially on MRPC and SQuAD, and adding a BiLSTM on top recovered part of the SQuAD gap but not GLUE. Larger models were better on every dataset, even the 3.6k-example MRPC. And a feature-based use of BERT (concatenating its top four layers into a NER tagger) came within a few tenths of a point of full fine-tuning. Read together: bidirectional MLM and size did most of the work; NSP was a small effect at best. The authors also note that fine-tuning BERT-large was sometimes unstable on small datasets, so they took the best of several random restarts on the dev set.

Limits & what broke
  • NSP was a mistake. RoBERTa (L138) found that removing NSP, with a different input packing, matches or improves results, and ALBERT (L139) explained why: random-document negatives can be detected from topic alone.
  • BERT was undertrained. The same architecture trained longer on more data with better choices beats it comfortably (L138).
  • Masked tokens are predicted independently given the unmasked ones, which XLNet (L141) targets.
  • BERT cannot generate text. It has no left-to-right factorization of the sequence probability, so there is no natural way to sample from it; coaxing it into iterative sampling gives poor text. Kill the BERT/GPT confusion explicitly:

RoBERTa: A Robustly Optimized BERT Pretraining Approach

35m videoLevel 2

Liu et al., 2019 (arXiv:1907.11692)

BERT was undertrained. Keep the BERT-large architecture unchanged; use dynamic masking, drop next-sentence prediction, pack full sentences, train with much larger batches on ten times more data for longer, and switch to byte-level BPE. Every gain comes from training decisions, and the result matched or beat every post-BERT model of its time.

Cold open

By mid-2019 a stream of papers claimed to improve on BERT (L137) with new objectives and architectures, XLNet (L141) the most prominent. Each also trained on more data, for longer, with different settings. RoBERTa asks the unglamorous question: how good is BERT itself, trained properly? Answer: good enough to match XLNet with none of its machinery. That's the paper.

The problem

Pretraining is expensive, so few runs are done, hyperparameters are rarely tuned, and papers compare against the original BERT checkpoint rather than a re-tuned one. Different groups use different private data. The result is that a reported improvement can come from the proposed idea or from any of a dozen uncontrolled training differences. RoBERTa is a replication study that varies one training decision at a time.

The method: one decision at a time

Dynamic masking. BERT masked its data once during preprocessing; to vary the masks it duplicated the data 10 times with different masks, so over 40 epochs each sequence was seen with the same mask four times. RoBERTa generates a new mask every time a sequence is fed to the model. In their comparison this is comparable to or slightly better than static masking, and it matters more as training gets longer and data bigger.

Bigger batches. BERT-base trained with batches of 256 sequences for 1M steps. At equal compute, RoBERTa compares 2K sequences for 125K steps and 8K for 31K steps, each with its learning rate re-tuned. The large batches improved masked-LM perplexity and end-task accuracy, and are easier to parallelize. Scaling the learning rate with the batch, and lowering Adam's \(\beta_2\) to 0.98 for stability, are part of the recipe: a large batch with the small-batch learning rate is simply a slower optimizer.

Byte-level BPE. BERT used a 30K character-level vocabulary built after heuristic pre-tokenization. RoBERTa switches to GPT-2's 50K byte-level BPE (L130), which can encode any input without unknown tokens. The authors note it was slightly worse on some tasks in early experiments and adopt it anyway for its universality, an honest trade-off.

More data, longer. BERT's 16GB (BookCorpus and Wikipedia) grows to about 160GB by adding CC-News (76GB), OpenWebText (38GB) and Stories (31GB). With the large-batch setup, training for 100K, 300K and 500K steps keeps improving, and even the longest run shows no sign of overfitting.

Evidence

Algorithm · the final RoBERTa recipe
  1. BERT-large architecture, unchanged (24 layers, hidden size 1,024, 16 heads).
  2. Masked LM only, with a fresh mask each time a sequence is used; no NSP; FULL-SENTENCES inputs of up to 512 tokens.
  3. Byte-level BPE with a 50K vocabulary.
  4. About 160GB of text; batches of 8K sequences for 500K steps; Adam with \(\beta_2 = 0.98\), learning rate tuned for the batch size, warm-up then linear decay.

The paper reports state-of-the-art results on GLUE (best single-task dev results and the top test-leaderboard average at the time), SQuAD v2.0 and RACE, matching or exceeding XLNet-large, which had more elaborate machinery and its own extra data. The ablation path through the paper is the real evidence: each change is added one at a time with the architecture fixed, so the attribution of the gains to training choices is direct.

The recurring lesson

This is the most important recurring lesson of the curriculum, and it has two halves. Tune the baseline before you believe the delta: a new method's gain over an under-tuned baseline measures the tuning gap, not the idea. Data and training beat architecture: at fixed design, more and better data, longer training and correct batch/learning-rate scaling moved the numbers more than a year of architectural proposals. Name both every time they recur (L142, L147, L168).

Limits & what broke
  • It invalidated a widely copied design. NSP had been carried into many BERT variants because the original ablation suggested it helped. That ablation changed NSP and input packing together; separating them showed the input format, not the task, was doing the work.
  • Compute-heavy. The full recipe needs a large GPU cluster, which is itself a barrier to the replication culture the paper advocates.
  • Data confounds. The 160GB comparison changes size and source mix at once, so "more data" and "more diverse data" are not separated.
  • Not the end of encoders. Better objectives did eventually help at matched compute (ELECTRA, L140), and architecture changes such as DeBERTa's disentangled attention later moved past RoBERTa. The claim is about attribution in 2019, not that objectives never matter.

Bridge

RoBERTa makes BERT better by spending more. ALBERT (L139) goes the other way, cutting parameters by an order of magnitude through embedding factorization and layer sharing, and replaces NSP with a task that cannot be solved by topic alone.

ALBERT: A Lite BERT

30m videoLevel 2

Lan, Chen, Goodman, Gimpel, Sharma & Soricut, ICLR 2020 (arXiv:1909.11942)

Two parameter-reduction techniques, factorized embeddings and sharing every parameter across layers, plus a harder replacement for next-sentence prediction. ALBERT cuts BERT's parameter count by an order of magnitude, but not its compute: parameter count is not speed.

Cold open

ALBERT-large has about 18M parameters to BERT-large's 334M, and the largest ALBERT set new state-of-the-art results on GLUE, SQuAD 2.0 and RACE with fewer parameters than BERT-large. Then look at the training-speed column of the same table: the best ALBERT is much slower than the BERT it beats. Both facts are true, and the gap between them is the lesson.

The problem

Scaling BERT up runs into memory limits and, the authors report, into worse results: simply doubling BERT-large's hidden size made it perform worse in their experiments. Two sources of parameters look wasteful. The embedding matrix is \(V \times H\), tying the vocabulary embedding size to the hidden size even though embeddings are context-independent and hidden states are context-dependent. And each of the \(L\) layers has its own weights, although layers in a trained BERT do similar things.

The method: factorized embedding parameterization

Derivation · the embedding arithmetic

Project one-hot tokens to a small \(E\)-dimensional embedding first, then up to \(H\): \(V \times H\) parameters become \(V \times E + E \times H\). For \(V = 30{,}000\), \(H = 1{,}024\), \(E = 128\):

$$V H = 30{,}720{,}000 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad V E + E H = 3{,}840{,}000 + 131{,}072 = 3{,}971{,}072,$$

a saving of about 26.7M parameters, 87% of the embedding. At ALBERT-xxlarge's \(H = 4{,}096\) the full matrix would be about 122.9M parameters against 4.36M factorized.

The method: cross-layer sharing and sentence-order prediction

Cross-layer parameter sharing. By default ALBERT shares all parameters, attention and feed-forward, across layers, so the network is one transformer layer applied \(L\) times. Parameters stop growing with depth. The authors also observe a side effect: the input and output embeddings of each layer change more smoothly with depth than in BERT, which they read as sharing stabilizing the network's parameters, a regularizer as much as a compression.

Definition · sentence-order prediction (SOP)

Positive examples are two consecutive segments from the same document, in their original order; negative examples are the same two segments with their order swapped. The model must judge order, that is, discourse coherence.

Why NSP was accidentally trivial: its negatives were segments from different documents (L137), so a model could reject most of them by noticing a change of topic, something the MLM objective already teaches. SOP keeps the topic identical in both classes and removes the shortcut. The paper's cross-evaluation makes the point cleanly: a model trained with NSP is near chance on SOP, while a model trained with SOP does reasonably well on NSP. A task that can be solved by a shortcut will be, and you learn only the shortcut.

Evidence

The ablations come one technique at a time. The embedding size \(E\) matters little once parameters are shared, so 128 is a good default. Sharing all parameters costs a modest amount of accuracy averaged over their benchmarks, on the order of one to two points, while cutting parameters several-fold to tenfold; most of that drop comes from sharing the feed-forward layers, while sharing attention costs little. SOP beats NSP on downstream tasks, especially those involving multiple sentences. The final ALBERT-xxlarge, trained longer with additional data, reports state-of-the-art GLUE, SQuAD 2.0 and RACE results at the time. The authors also found that removing dropout helped their largest models.

Limits & what broke
  • Parameter count is not speed. Shared weights are reused, not skipped: a 12-layer shared network does exactly the arithmetic of a 12-layer unshared one. From the configurations (about \(12H^2\) weights per layer, each used once per token), ALBERT-xxlarge needs about \(12 \cdot 12 H^2 \approx 2.4\) billion multiply–adds per token with \(H = 4{,}096\), against BERT-large's \(24 \cdot 12 H^2 \approx 0.3\) billion with \(H = 1{,}024\): around eight times more work, and correspondingly slower training and inference. Fewer parameters help checkpoint size, memory for weights and communication in distributed training; they do not reduce latency.
  • Activations still dominate memory in training, and they scale with depth and width, not with unique parameters.
  • Sharing did not spread. Later large models mostly kept unshared layers; the factorized embedding and SOP-style coherence tasks had more influence than full sharing.
  • Comparisons mix effects. The headline model also used more data, longer training and different optimization (LAMB with large batches), so the gains are not all due to the two techniques.

Bridge

ALBERT reduces parameters but not compute. ELECTRA (L140) attacks compute directly by changing the pretraining task so that every token provides a training signal, not just the 15% that MLM predicts.

ELECTRA: Pre-training Text Encoders as Discriminators

35m videoLevel 2

Clark, Luong, Le & Manning, ICLR 2020 (arXiv:2003.10555)

Replace masked-token prediction with replaced-token detection: a small masked-LM generator fills masked positions with plausible samples, and the discriminator, the model you keep, classifies every token as original or replaced. Because all positions provide signal, it learns far more per unit of compute than BERT.

Cold open

Learning from every token instead of 15%. The authors report that ELECTRA-Small, trained on one GPU for four days, outperforms GPT (L136), which used roughly 30 times more compute, on GLUE; and that ELECTRA-Large matches RoBERTa and XLNet while using less than a quarter of their compute, and beats them at equal compute. The paper's argument is made with compute on the x-axis, which is how pretraining methods should be compared.

The problem

BERT's masked LM (L137) predicts only the 15% of positions it masked, so 85% of each sequence produces no loss. It also sees [MASK] during pretraining but never at fine-tuning. Masking more tokens gives more signal but destroys more context. ELECTRA wants a task defined at every position that still requires understanding the context.

The method: replaced-token detection

Definition · the ELECTRA objective

Mask a random set of positions \(m\) (about 15%), let the generator \(G\) predict them, and sample replacements \(\hat x_t \sim p_G(x_t \mid x^{\text{masked}})\) to form \(x^{\text{corrupt}}\). The discriminator outputs \(D(x^{\text{corrupt}}, t) = \sigma\big(w^{\top} h_D(x^{\text{corrupt}})_t\big)\), the probability that token \(t\) is original. Train both jointly:

$$\min_{\theta_G, \theta_D} \sum_{x} \mathcal L_{\text{MLM}}(x, \theta_G) + \lambda\, \mathcal L_{\text{Disc}}(x, \theta_D), \qquad \lambda = 50,$$ $$\mathcal L_{\text{Disc}} = \E\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{n} -\mathbb 1(x^{\text{corrupt}}_t = x_t) \log D(x^{\text{corrupt}}, t) - \mathbb 1(x^{\text{corrupt}}_t \ne x_t) \log\big(1 - D(x^{\text{corrupt}}, t)\big)\Big].$$

A sampled token that happens to equal the original counts as original.

The method: why it is not a GAN

The setup looks adversarial, and it is not. The generator is trained with maximum likelihood to predict the masked tokens, not to fool the discriminator. It could not easily be trained adversarially anyway: sampling a discrete token is not differentiable, so no gradient flows from the discriminator's loss back into the generator. The authors did try adversarial training through reinforcement learning (a policy gradient on the generator) and report it worked worse than maximum likelihood. There is also no noise input, and the generator is discarded, not used. Say this explicitly: it is a denoising objective with a learned noise process.

Generator size. Counterintuitively, a stronger generator is worse. In the paper's sweep the best generators are about a quarter to a half of the discriminator's size. The authors' explanation: a too-strong generator produces replacements that are too hard to detect, so the discriminator's task stops being informative, and the discriminator may spend capacity modelling the generator instead of the data. Weight sharing: tying all weights would force equal sizes, so they share only the token and position embeddings. That helps because the generator's softmax over the vocabulary updates every embedding, while the discriminator only updates embeddings of tokens that appear in its input.

Evidence

The core claim is sample efficiency, and the paper tests it with ablations that separate the two possible explanations. Training the discriminator on only the 15% masked positions loses much of ELECTRA's advantage, so learning from all positions is most of the gain. An MLM variant that replaces masks with generator samples (removing the [MASK] mismatch) improves on BERT slightly. An MLM variant that predicts all tokens closes most of the gap to ELECTRA. The gains are largest for small models and shrink, without vanishing, as models grow. Throughout, comparisons are plotted against pretraining FLOPs, not steps or epochs, which is the right axis and a habit worth copying.

Limits & what broke
  • Two models to tune. Generator size, \(\lambda\) and the masking rate interact; the method is less plug-and-play than MLM.
  • Discriminators do not generate. The kept model has a binary head; it cannot fill in blanks or produce text, so it is purely an encoder for fine-tuning.
  • Smaller gains at the top end. At large scale and full compute, ELECTRA-Large is competitive with rather than far ahead of RoBERTa and XLNet; the efficiency story is strongest when compute is limited.
  • What survived. Replaced-token detection lives on in later encoders such as DeBERTa-v3, which pairs it with a modified embedding-sharing scheme. But the field's attention moved to decoder-only models, where every token is a target anyway.

Bridge

ELECTRA attacks MLM's inefficiency; it does not touch MLM's other weakness, that masked tokens are predicted independently of each other. XLNet (L141) keeps an autoregressive objective, which models those dependencies, and gets bidirectional context by permuting the factorization order.

XLNet: Generalized Autoregressive Pretraining

45m videoLevel 3

Yang, Dai, Yang, Carbonell, Salakhutdinov & Le, NeurIPS 2019 (arXiv:1906.08237)

Train an autoregressive model under many random factorization orders of the same sequence, with every token keeping its original position. In expectation each token is predicted from context on both sides, without any [MASK]; a two-stream attention mechanism lets the model know which position it is predicting without seeing that position's content.

Cold open

Bidirectional context without masking. The abstract reports that XLNet outperforms BERT on 20 tasks, often by a large margin, and sets the state of the art on 18. It was the most elegant pretraining objective of 2019, and within weeks a plainly trained BERT (L138) had largely closed the gap. Both halves of that story are worth teaching.

The problem

Autoregressive (AR) language models like GPT (L136) factorize \(p(x) = \prod_t p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\), a proper likelihood, but see only one side. BERT's autoencoding (L137) sees both sides but pays twice. First, [MASK] appears in pretraining and never in fine-tuning. Second, it predicts the masked tokens independently given the unmasked ones. The paper's example: with New and York both masked in "New York is a city", BERT maximizes \(\log p(\text{New} \mid \text{is a city}) + \log p(\text{York} \mid \text{is a city})\), never learning that York is much likelier given New. XLNet wants the AR objective's proper factorization with BERT's two-sided context.

The method: permutation language modelling

Definition · the permutation LM objective

Let \(\mathcal Z_T\) be the set of permutations of \((1, \dots, T)\). XLNet maximizes

$$\max_\theta\; \E_{\mathbf z \sim \mathcal Z_T} \Big[ \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\big(x_{z_t} \mid \mathbf x_{\mathbf z_{<t}}\big) \Big].$$

For each sampled order \(\mathbf z\), token \(x_{z_t}\) is predicted from the tokens that come before it in that order. Because the parameters are shared across all orders, every token learns, in expectation, to use context from every other position, on both sides.

Now the single most misunderstood point in the paper, worth five minutes on its own. The permutation is over the factorization order, not over the words. The input sequence is never shuffled; every token keeps its original position encoding, and the model always reads "New York is a city" in that order. What changes is which tokens each prediction is allowed to look at, and that is implemented entirely with attention masks. Fine-tuning then uses the natural sequence with no masks at all, so there is no pretrain–fine-tune input mismatch.

With the order is, a, city, New, York, the objective contains \(\log p(\text{New} \mid \text{is a city}) + \log p(\text{York} \mid \text{New, is a city})\): the dependency BERT drops is modelled.

The method: two-stream self-attention

Derivation · why one stream is not enough

The standard softmax \(p_\theta(X_{z_t} = x \mid \mathbf x_{\mathbf z_{<t}}) = \exp\big(e(x)^\top h_\theta(\mathbf x_{\mathbf z_{<t}})\big) / \sum_{x'} \exp\big(e(x')^\top h_\theta(\mathbf x_{\mathbf z_{<t}})\big)\) does not depend on which position \(z_t\) is being predicted. Take two orders that share the same prefix \(\mathbf z_{<t}\) but differ in \(z_t\): the model would be forced to give the same distribution for, say, position 2 and position 5, which is wrong. The representation must be target-aware, \(g_\theta(\mathbf x_{\mathbf z_{<t}}, z_t)\).

But one representation cannot serve both of its roles. To predict \(x_{z_t}\), it may use the position \(z_t\) but must not see the content \(x_{z_t}\), or the task is trivial. To serve as context for later predictions \(x_{z_j}\), \(j > t\), it must encode the content \(x_{z_t}\). Hence two streams, updated in every layer \(m\):

$$g_{z_t}^{(m)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Attention}\big(Q = g_{z_t}^{(m-1)},\; KV = \mathbf h_{\mathbf z_{<t}}^{(m-1)}\big) \quad\text{(query stream: position, not content)}$$ $$h_{z_t}^{(m)} \leftarrow \operatorname{Attention}\big(Q = h_{z_t}^{(m-1)},\; KV = \mathbf h_{\mathbf z_{\le t}}^{(m-1)}\big) \quad\text{(content stream: sees itself)}$$

The query stream starts from a single learned vector \(w\), the content stream from the word embeddings, and both share all weights. Predictions are made from the last layer's query stream; at fine-tuning time the query stream is dropped and the content stream is an ordinary transformer.

Partial prediction. Predicting the first tokens of an order is hard, since they have little context, so XLNet only predicts the last part of each order, about \(1/K\) of the tokens with \(K\) around 6 or 7; the query stream is needed only for those. Backbone. XLNet is built on Transformer-XL (L148): segment-level recurrence for long contexts and relative positional encodings, which fit naturally because the content and query streams need positions decoupled from content. Pair inputs use a relative segment encoding instead of BERT's absolute A/B embeddings.

Evidence

The paper includes a fair comparison: XLNet-Large and BERT-Large trained on the same data with similar settings, where XLNet is better on SQuAD, RACE and GLUE tasks. The headline numbers then come from a much larger setup (more data, including web crawl text, and a large TPU budget), with state-of-the-art results across QA, NLI, sentiment and document ranking. The ablations show that Transformer-XL's memory matters most on long-context tasks such as RACE, and that a BERT-style next-sentence objective did not help, so XLNet drops it.

Limits & what broke
  • RoBERTa largely closed the gap with none of this. A BERT-large trained longer on more data with better settings (L138) matched XLNet on most benchmarks weeks later, which suggests much of XLNet's headline advantage came from data and training, not from the objective.
  • Complexity versus benefit. Permutation masks, two streams, partial prediction and Transformer-XL recurrence make it hard to implement, debug and scale. When a simpler method gets most of the gain, the simpler method wins adoption; this is a real theme, not an accident.
  • Expensive. Each training example covers only a fraction of tokens as targets, and the query stream adds computation; the large-scale runs needed hundreds of TPU chips for days.
  • What survived. The insight that an AR model can be bidirectional through masking of the factorization order, and the clean statement of BERT's independence assumption. T5 (L142) and later work moved to span corruption and decoder-only scaling instead.

Bridge

By late 2019 there were many pretraining objectives, architectures and datasets, each tested in a different setup. T5 (L142) runs the controlled comparison: one text-to-text framework, one codebase, and a systematic ablation of architectures, objectives, corpora and training strategies.

Exploring the Limits of Transfer Learning with a Unified Text-to-Text Transformer (T5)

55m videoLevel 3

Raffel et al., JMLR 2020 (arXiv:1910.10683)

Cast every task as text in, text out, with a short task prefix, so one model, one loss and one decoding procedure cover classification, regression, translation and summarization. Then use that uniformity to run a large controlled study of architectures, objectives, corpora and training strategies. The ablation tables are the paper; the 11B-parameter model is the epilogue.

Cold open

The paper's first figure: the same model receives translate English to German: That is good. and writes Das ist gut.; receives cola sentence: The course is jumping well. and writes not acceptable; receives an stsb sentence1: … sentence2: … pair and writes the similarity score as a string, 3.8. Even regression is text: STS-B scores are rounded to the nearest 0.2 and predicted as tokens. Nothing task-specific is left in the architecture.

The problem

By 2019 every transfer-learning paper (L136–L141) changed several things at once: architecture, objective, data, model size and training length. RoBERTa (L138) showed how badly that confounds conclusions. T5 is, in effect, a survey disguised as a model: fix a baseline, change one factor at a time, and measure on a broad benchmark suite (GLUE, SuperGLUE, SQuAD, CNN/Daily Mail summarization, and WMT English to German, French and Romanian).

The method: the baseline and the format

The baseline is an encoder–decoder transformer (L129) in which each stack is about the size of BERT-base (12 blocks, \(d_{\text{model}} = 768\)), roughly 220M parameters in all. Three details depart from the original transformer. Layer normalization is simplified (no additive bias) and applied to each sub-layer's input, outside the residual path. The vocabulary is a 32k SentencePiece model (L131) covering English, German, French and Romanian. And positions are handled by relative position buckets: each attention head adds a learned scalar to the logit for each query–key offset, with offsets grouped into 32 buckets whose widths grow logarithmically up to a distance of 128, beyond which all offsets share a bucket. These scalars are shared across layers. Every task is trained with the same teacher-forced cross-entropy on the output text.

Definition · span corruption

Drop 15% of the tokens in contiguous spans of mean length 3. Replace each dropped span in the input with a unique sentinel token, and make the target the sequence of sentinels each followed by the tokens it replaced, ending with a final sentinel. The paper's example:

original Thank you for inviting me to your party last week.
input Thank you <X> me to your party <Y> week.
target <X> for inviting <Y> last <Z>

Targets contain only the dropped tokens, so they are short, which makes pretraining cheaper than reconstructing the whole input.

The method: four ablation tables, read properly

1. Architectures. Encoder–decoder, encoder–decoder with shared parameters, decoder-only LM and prefix LM, each trained with a denoising or an LM objective. Note the accounting: an encoder–decoder with \(L\) layers in each stack has about the parameters of a \(2L\)-layer decoder but the compute of an \(L\)-layer one, since each token passes through only one stack. The encoder–decoder with denoising wins; sharing encoder and decoder weights loses little; a prefix LM beats a causal LM. Keep the scale qualifier in view: this is at roughly 220M parameters with fine-tuning, and Month 6's decoder-only models were judged on different terms (zero- and few-shot use at much larger scale).

2. Objectives. At the top level, BERT-style denoising beats prefix language modelling and deshuffling. Among denoising variants (MASS-style, replacing spans with sentinels, dropping tokens), results are similar, so span replacement is chosen for its short targets. Corruption rates of 10–25% perform alike, 50% is worse; mean span lengths from 2 to 5 do about the same, longer spans slightly worse. The lesson the authors draw is that the objective matters less than one might expect once it is some form of denoising, so choose for cost.

3. Pretraining data. C4 against an unfiltered version (clearly worse), and against smaller domain-specific corpora (news-like, WebText-like, Wikipedia, Wikipedia plus books), which help on tasks from their own domain. Then repetition: truncating C4 so that the data is repeated 64 to 4,096 times during pretraining degrades performance as repetition grows; a few repeats are harmless. Data size matters once you would otherwise loop over it many times.

4. Training strategy and scale. Fine-tuning all parameters beats adapter layers and gradual unfreezing (L135); multi-task training alone is slightly worse than pretraining then fine-tuning, while multi-task pretraining followed by fine-tuning is comparable. Given four times the compute, both a bigger model and longer training help, and ensembling helps on top of either.

The method: C4

The Colossal Clean Crawled Corpus is one Common Crawl snapshot (April 2019) passed through simple heuristics, cited constantly ever since: keep only lines ending in terminal punctuation and with at least five words; drop pages with fewer than three sentences; drop any page containing a word from a public list of "dirty, naughty, obscene or otherwise bad words"; drop lines mentioning JavaScript and pages containing "lorem ipsum" or a curly brace; de-duplicate by discarding all but one occurrence of any three-sentence span; and keep pages classified as English with probability at least 0.99. About 750GB of text remains.

Evidence

Combining the winners (encoder–decoder, span corruption, C4, multi-task pretraining then fine-tuning, much longer training of about a trillion tokens) and scaling to 11B parameters, the paper reports state-of-the-art results at the time on GLUE, SuperGLUE (close to the human baseline), SQuAD and CNN/Daily Mail. It does not reach the state of the art on WMT translation, which the authors attribute to English-only pretraining and the absence of back-translation, an honest negative.

Limits & what broke
  • The filters have side effects. Dodge et al. (2021) documented that the bad-words filter disproportionately removed text by and about minority groups, including non-offensive content, and found unexpected sources such as machine-translated patents in C4. Heuristic cleaning encodes choices nobody audited.
  • One factor at a time. Each ablation varies one choice from the baseline, so interactions (for example between objective and model size) are mostly untested, and everything is at baseline scale.
  • The architecture verdict did not survive scale. Encoder–decoders with denoising won here; the field's frontier moved to decoder-only models used through prompting (Month 6 and L145). Both statements are true under their own conditions.
  • Cost. The study itself, plus an 11B-parameter final model, was far outside what most labs could replicate in 2019.

Bridge

T5's encoder–decoder denoising has a close cousin published the same month. BART (L143) pairs a BERT-like encoder with a GPT-like decoder and asks which kinds of noise teach the most, finding that text infilling wins.

BART: Denoising Sequence-to-Sequence Pre-training

30m videoLevel 2

Lewis et al., ACL 2020 (arXiv:1910.13461)

Pretrain a standard sequence-to-sequence transformer, a bidirectional encoder and an autoregressive decoder, to reconstruct documents corrupted by arbitrary noise. A head-to-head comparison of noising functions finds that text infilling, where each missing span is replaced by a single mask, teaches the most, and the resulting model is especially strong at generation.

Cold open

BERT's encoder (L137) plus GPT's decoder (L136). The abstract reports that BART matches RoBERTa on GLUE and SQuAD with comparable training, sets new state-of-the-art results on abstractive dialogue, question answering and summarization with gains of up to 6 ROUGE, and adds 1.1 BLEU over a back-translation system for translation using only target-language pretraining. Summarization is where it dominated for years.

The problem

Encoders pretrained with MLM are good at understanding but cannot generate; left-to-right decoders generate but see one side. Earlier seq2seq pretraining (MASS, for example) used specific masking schemes tied to the architecture. BART decouples the two: any corruption you can apply to text is a valid pretraining task, and the decoder always reconstructs the original document. That turns "which objective?" into an experiment.

The method

Architecture. A standard transformer encoder–decoder (L129) with GeLU activations: 6 layers in each stack for the base model, 12 for large, about 10% more parameters than an equally sized BERT. The loss is the cross-entropy of the decoder's reconstruction of the uncorrupted document.

Definition · text infilling

Sample spans with lengths drawn from a Poisson distribution with \(\lambda = 3\), and replace each span, whatever its length, with a single [MASK] token. Spans of length 0 are allowed, which amounts to inserting a mask. The model must therefore predict both the missing content and how many tokens are missing, which neither BERT's masking (one mask per token) nor plain deletion asks for.

Fine-tuning. For classification, the same input goes to the encoder and the decoder, and the final decoder token's state feeds the classifier, playing the role of BERT's [CLS] but placed at the end so that it can attend to the whole decoded input. Generation tasks such as summarization and abstractive QA fine-tune directly, since the decoder is already trained to produce text conditioned on the encoder.

Translation is the clever piece of engineering. BART is pretrained on English only, so the authors replace its input embedding layer with a new, randomly initialized encoder that maps foreign-language text into something BART can "de-noise" into English. Training proceeds in two steps: first freeze most of BART and train only the new encoder, the positional embeddings and the input projection of BART's first self-attention layer; then train all parameters for a small number of iterations. The pretrained model is used as a target-side language model attached to a learned source encoder.

Evidence

The noise ablation is the whole scientific content. At base size, the authors compare the noising functions (and language-modelling baselines) on SQuAD, MNLI, long-form QA (ELI5), summarization (XSum, CNN/Daily Mail) and dialogue (ConvAI2). Read qualitatively: performance of a given objective varies a lot across tasks; text infilling is consistently strong, and the best combination adds sentence permutation; sentence permutation and document rotation alone perform poorly; token deletion beats token masking on generation tasks; and a left-to-right decoder helps generation while the bidirectional encoder matters for SQuAD. The large model is trained like RoBERTa (L138), on about 160GB of text with batches of 8,000 for 500K steps, masking 30% of tokens by infilling and permuting all sentences.

At scale, BART-large is comparable to RoBERTa on GLUE and SQuAD, so the generative decoder does not cost understanding performance, and it sets the state of the art on CNN/Daily Mail and especially XSum, a highly abstractive dataset where the gain is largest.

Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (GPT-2)

45m videoLevel 2

Radford, Wu, Child, Luan, Amodei & Sutskever, 2019

Train a larger decoder-only language model on a curated, diverse web corpus, and it begins to perform tasks it was never trained on, zero-shot, when the task is written into the text. The absolute numbers are weak; the significance is that zero-shot performance rises log-linearly with model size, with no sign of saturation.

Cold open

Append TL;DR: to a news article and let the model continue: out comes something shaped like a summary. Write "English sentence = French sentence" pairs and then an English sentence followed by "=": out comes rough French. Nobody trained GPT-2 to summarize or translate. The paper's claim is that a language model trained on enough varied text has to learn these tasks implicitly, because the web is full of demonstrations of them.

The problem

Supervised systems are narrow: each needs its own labelled dataset and breaks under small distribution shifts. Multi-task training needs many curated datasets, and GPT-1 (L136) still fine-tuned per task. The paper reframes the goal. A general system should model \(p(\text{output} \mid \text{input}, \text{task})\), and in language the task, the input and the output can all be expressed as a sequence of symbols, for example (translate to french, english text, french text).

Claim · the task is a subset of language modelling

Because a supervised objective is the same as the unsupervised LM objective evaluated only on a subset of sequences (those that contain the task's input–output pairs), the global minimum of the LM objective is also a minimum of the supervised objective. A language model with enough capacity and data should therefore learn to perform tasks demonstrated in its text, whatever the training method. The paper is careful that this is about the optimum, not about whether practical optimization gets there; the experiments test how far it does.

The method

WebText. Instead of an unfiltered crawl, the authors scraped outbound links from Reddit posts with at least 3 karma, a heuristic for "some human found this interesting, educational or funny". After extraction, de-duplication and cleaning this gave slightly over 8 million documents, about 40GB of text. Wikipedia articles were removed, because they overlap with many evaluation sets.

Byte-level BPE. A byte-level base vocabulary of 256 values means any string can be encoded, with no unknown tokens and no lossy pre-processing (L130). To stop BPE wasting vocabulary on variants like dog., dog! and dog?, merges across character categories are blocked, except for spaces. The vocabulary has 50,257 tokens, the context 1,024 tokens.

Architecture. Essentially GPT-1 with a few changes that became standard: layer normalization moved to the input of each sub-block (pre-LN) with an extra layer norm after the final block, and a modified initialization that scales the weights of residual layers by \(1/\sqrt N\), where \(N\) is the number of residual layers.

Derivation · why scale residual weights by 1/√N

In a pre-LN network the residual stream is \(x_N = x_0 + \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i(\cdot)\). If at initialization each branch output is roughly independent with variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\Var(x_N) \approx \Var(x_0) + N\sigma^2\), which grows with depth. Scaling each branch's output projection by \(1/\sqrt N\) makes each contribute \(\sigma^2/N\), so the total stays \(O(1)\) however deep the network is.

Four sizes. 117M parameters (12 layers, width 768, the size of GPT-1), 345M (24 layers, 1,024, the size of BERT-large), 762M (36, 1,280) and 1,542M (48, 1,600). OpenAI later corrected the two smallest counts (to 124M and 355M) after a miscount; you will see both labels.

Evidence

Every result is zero-shot, with no gradient update on the task. The paper reports state-of-the-art perplexity on 7 of 8 language-modelling benchmarks without training on them, the exception being the One Billion Word Benchmark, whose shuffled sentences destroy long-range structure. On CoQA reading comprehension it reaches 55 F1, matching or beating 3 of 4 baselines that were trained on over 127k examples. The other tasks show how weak it was in absolute terms, and the paper says so: TL;DR: summaries score only slightly above taking three random sentences from the article; translation reaches about 5 BLEU from English to French and about 11.5 from French to English, far below supervised and even unsupervised systems; on Natural Questions it answers about 4% exactly.

The figure that matters plots each zero-shot task against model size on a log axis: performance rises steadily, roughly log-linearly, across all four sizes, and both training and held-out perplexity on WebText are still falling for the largest model, so it is underfitting. That trend, not any single number, is the result, and it is exactly what Kaplan et al. (L146) turn into scaling laws. The paper also checks contamination with 8-gram overlap between WebText and test sets, finding a few percent overlap for most benchmarks, an early example of an analysis every later paper needs.

The staged release

In February 2019 OpenAI released only the smallest model, citing concerns about misuse such as automated disinformation and spam, then released larger models over the year, with the full 1.5B model in November 2019 accompanied by a report on release strategies and social impacts. At the time, some researchers argued the caution was warranted and others that it was excessive or impeded replication. Since then, staged and structured release of capable models has become a common practice, and the debate about it continues.

Limits & what broke
  • Zero-shot was far from useful on most tasks; the paper's honesty about this is part of its value. Usable task performance without fine-tuning arrived with scale and few-shot prompting (L145).
  • The curation heuristic has a population. Reddit karma reflects the interests and demographics of Reddit users, which shape what "quality" WebText contains.
  • Decoding matters. Likelihood-maximizing decoding produces repetitive text; the paper's samples use top-\(k\) sampling, and later work proposed nucleus sampling to address degeneration (Holtzman et al. 2020).
  • What survived almost entirely: byte-level BPE, pre-LN with scaled residual initialization, decoder-only LM pretraining on curated web text, and contamination checks.

Bridge

GPT-2 says the task can live in the input. GPT-3 (L145) scales the same recipe by about a hundred and adds a few examples of the task to the input, and in-context learning becomes the main way to use a model.

Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)

60m videoLevel 3

Brown et al., NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2005.14165)

Scale the GPT-2 recipe to 175 billion parameters and use the model by putting a task description and a few worked examples in its context window, with no gradient updates. This in-context learning improves steadily with model size, giving competitive results on many tasks and clear, instructive failures on others.

Cold open

175 billion parameters, and a new way to use a model. The paper's Figure 2.1 shows the whole interface: the prompt Translate English to French: sea otter => loutre de mer, peppermint => menthe poivrée, plush giraffe => girafe peluche, cheese => and the model continues with the translation. No fine-tuning, no task head, no labelled dataset beyond the three examples in the prompt. Every prompting technique in Month 7 starts here.

The problem

Fine-tuning (L136–L143) needs thousands of labelled examples per task, and fine-tuned models can exploit spurious features of narrow datasets. People, by contrast, pick up a new task from an instruction and a couple of examples. GPT-2 (L144) showed zero-shot ability that was weak but improving with scale. The hypothesis tested here: the ability to learn from examples in the context also improves with scale, so a large enough model is useful without any task-specific training.

The method: in-context learning, defined precisely

Definition · zero-, one- and few-shot
  • Few-shot: the context holds a natural-language task description (optional), then \(K\) demonstrations of context plus correct completion, then one new context; the model's continuation is the answer. \(K\) is typically 10 to 100, as many as fit in the 2,048-token window.
  • One-shot: \(K = 1\).
  • Zero-shot: only the task description and the new context.

In all three, no weights are updated. Whatever "learning" happens is computed in the forward pass from the demonstrations. The paper reserves "fine-tuning" for gradient updates and does not use it for GPT-3's main results.

The method: what actually changed

The architecture is GPT-2's, with one change: layers alternate between dense attention and locally banded sparse attention patterns, similar to the Sparse Transformer (L156). That is all. The paper trains eight sizes, from 125M to 175B parameters (125M, 350M, 760M, 1.3B, 2.7B, 6.7B, 13B, 175B); the largest has 96 layers, width 12,288 and 96 heads of dimension 128. All are trained on 300B tokens drawn from a mixture: Common Crawl filtered by a quality classifier and fuzzy de-duplication, plus WebText2, two book corpora and Wikipedia. The mixture is sampled by quality rather than size, so the curated sets are seen two to three times while most of Common Crawl is seen less than once.

Derivation · the compute bill

A forward and backward pass costs about \(6N\) FLOPs per token for a model with \(N\) parameters (2 for the forward multiply–adds, 4 for the backward). So

$$C \approx 6ND = 6 \times (1.75 \times 10^{11}) \times (3 \times 10^{11}) \approx 3.15 \times 10^{23}\ \text{FLOPs}.$$

One petaflop/s-day is \(10^{15} \times 86{,}400 \approx 8.64 \times 10^{19}\) FLOPs, giving about 3,600 petaflop/s-days, consistent with the paper's figure. At that price there is one training run, and the paper says so in its own words: a bug in the filtering of benchmark overlaps was found after training, and "due to the cost of training it was not feasible to retrain the model".

Evidence

Smooth scaling. Validation loss falls as a smooth power law in compute across the eight models, and across dozens of tasks few-shot performance improves with size, typically faster than zero-shot: larger models make better use of the examples in their context. This is where the "emergence" framing starts, the idea that some abilities appear abruptly with scale; later work re-examines how much of that is real and how much is an artefact of the metric (L206).

Where it shines. The paper reports few-shot results on the LAMBADA cloze task and on TriviaQA that exceed the fine-tuned state of the art of the time, few-shot translation into English that is competitive with or better than prior unsupervised translation, and generated news articles that human raters distinguish from real ones only slightly better than chance.

A concrete capability boundary. In the arithmetic tasks, the 175B model in the few-shot setting adds two-digit numbers almost perfectly, gets roughly 80% of three-digit additions right, and then falls off: about a quarter of four-digit and around one in ten five-digit additions. Smaller models fail much earlier. The authors check that most correct answers do not appear verbatim in the training data, so it is not pure lookup, but it is also clearly not a learned algorithm.

Where it fails. Natural-language inference is weak (adversarial NLI near chance), WiC, which asks whether a word is used in the same sense in two sentences, is at chance, and several reading-comprehension tasks remain far below fine-tuned models. The authors note the pattern: tasks that require comparing two sentences or passages are hard in the few-shot setting.

Contamination. Because the training data is a web crawl, test sets may be in it. The paper's own section on this is honest: they measured overlap for every benchmark, compared performance on clean subsets with performance on the full sets, found little effect for most, and flagged the affected results.

Limits & what broke
  • Few-shot is not fine-tuned. On many benchmarks (SuperGLUE among them) few-shot GPT-3 stayed well below fine-tuned models, some much smaller.
  • Prompts are fragile. Results moved with the wording, the choice of demonstrations and even their order; later work proposed calibration to reduce the bias (Zhao et al. 2021).
  • What is "learned" in context? Min et al. (2022) found that randomizing the labels in demonstrations often hurts less than expected, suggesting that demonstrations largely specify the format and label space rather than teach the mapping. That is the seed of a long debate.
  • One run, closed weights. The data bug could not be fixed, and access was through an API, so outside researchers could not inspect or replicate the model.
  • The paper's own warnings. It includes analyses of gender, race and religion biases in generations and of misuse potential, which the field has had to take further since.

Bridge

Why did GPT-3 use 175B parameters and 300B tokens, rather than, say, 20B parameters and 3T tokens? The answer came from a paper published four months earlier: Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws (L146), which said that at fixed compute you should prefer bigger models trained on relatively less data.

Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

55m videoLevel 3

Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan et al., 2020 (arXiv:2001.08361)

A language model's test loss falls as a smooth power law in its non-embedding parameter count, its dataset size and its training compute, across many orders of magnitude, while architectural shape barely matters. Fitted together, the laws say that at a fixed compute budget you should train very large models on comparatively little data and stop well before convergence.

Cold open

Three log–log plots, three straight lines. Test loss against compute, against dataset size, against parameters, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude, as the abstract puts it. Before this paper, "make it bigger" was a hunch with GPT-2's four points behind it (L144). After it, it was a forecast you could put in a budget.

The problem

Given a compute budget, how big should the model be, how much data should it see, what batch size and how many steps? Without a quantitative model each choice is a guess, and wrong guesses at large scale cost months. The authors train many decoder-only transformers on WebText2 (from about \(10^3\) to \(10^9\) non-embedding parameters, on up to about 23B tokens) and measure cross-entropy loss in nats per token, looking for regularities.

The method: the three power laws

Two conventions make the trends clean. \(N\) counts non-embedding parameters, about \(12\, n_{\text{layer}}\, d_{\text{model}}^2\); counting embeddings makes small models look different and the curves bend. Compute is \(C \approx 6NBS\) FLOPs for batch size \(B\) tokens and \(S\) steps (the \(6N\)-per-token rule of L145), quoted in petaflop/s-days. When the other two factors are not limiting, the paper's fits are

$$L(N) = \Big(\frac{N_c}{N}\Big)^{\alpha_N}, \quad L(D) = \Big(\frac{D_c}{D}\Big)^{\alpha_D}, \quad L(C_{\min}) = \Big(\frac{C_c}{C_{\min}}\Big)^{\alpha_C},$$

with \(\alpha_N \approx 0.076\), \(\alpha_D \approx 0.095\) and \(\alpha_C \approx 0.050\) (\(N_c \approx 8.8 \times 10^{13}\) parameters, \(D_c \approx 5.4 \times 10^{13}\) tokens, \(C_c \approx 3.1 \times 10^8\) petaflop/s-days). Here \(C_{\min}\) is compute used efficiently, that is at the optimal model size for each budget and at the critical batch size discussed below.

Shape barely matters. At fixed \(N\), varying depth, width, the ratio of feed-forward to model width and the number of heads changes the loss by only a few percent over a wide range. This is the strongest claim in the paper and the reason the laws can be stated in terms of \(N\) alone. It also explains a methodological habit of later labs: tune the architecture once, then spend effort on scale and data.

The method: overfitting, training time and allocation

Derivation · how much data a bigger model needs

The paper fits a joint form, \(L(N, D) = \big[(N_c/N)^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D} + D_c/D\big]^{\alpha_D}\). Divide by the infinite-data loss \(L(N, \infty) = (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N}\):

$$\frac{L(N, D)}{L(N, \infty)} = \Big[1 + \frac{D_c}{D}\Big(\frac{N}{N_c}\Big)^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D}\Big]^{\alpha_D}.$$

The overfitting penalty depends on \(N\) and \(D\) only through \(N^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D}/D\). To keep it fixed while growing the model, grow the data as \(D \propto N^{\alpha_N/\alpha_D}\). With the joint fit's exponents the ratio is about 0.74, which the paper states as \(D \gtrsim (5 \times 10^3)\, N^{0.74}\): an 8× larger model needs only about \(8^{0.74} \approx 5\)× more data. Data requirements grow sublinearly in model size.

A similar fit describes learning curves, \(L(N, S) = (N_c/N)^{\alpha_N} + (S_c/S_{\min})^{\alpha_S}\) with \(\alpha_S \approx 0.76\), and it shows large models are more sample-efficient: they reach a given loss in fewer steps and with fewer tokens. Combining this with \(C = 6NBS\) and minimizing loss at fixed compute, the paper derives the compute-efficient frontier:

$$N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.73}, \qquad B \propto C^{0.24}, \qquad S \propto C^{0.03}, \qquad\text{so}\quad D = BS \propto C^{0.27}.$$

Their conclusion: at fixed compute, prefer much bigger models trained on relatively less data, and stop significantly short of convergence. Almost all extra compute should go into size; the number of serial steps barely grows. This is the allocation that tomorrow's paper overturns (L147).

Result · the critical batch size (after McCandlish et al. 2018)

For a target loss, steps and examples trade off as \(\big(S/S_{\min} - 1\big)\big(E/E_{\min} - 1\big) = 1\), where \(S = S_{\min}(1 + B_{\text{noise}}/B)\) and \(E = E_{\min}(1 + B/B_{\text{noise}})\), and \(B_{\text{noise}} = \operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)/|G|^2\) is the gradient noise scale (per-example gradient covariance over squared true gradient). At \(B = B_{\text{crit}} \approx B_{\text{noise}}\) training takes twice the minimum steps and twice the minimum data, the natural compromise. Kaplan et al. find \(B_{\text{crit}}\) depends on the loss reached, not the model size, growing as the loss falls (\(B_{\text{crit}} \propto L^{-1/\alpha_B}\), \(\alpha_B \approx 0.21\)): better models can use bigger batches.

Evidence

The evidence is the fits themselves: hundreds of training runs whose final losses lie on the lines across the measured ranges, with the same exponents recovered from different slices of the data, and generalization to other text distributions that shifts the loss by a roughly constant offset. The paper also notes where the trends must break. Extrapolating \(L(C_{\min})\) and \(L(D)\) together, they intersect at a point far beyond the measured range, which the authors read as a sign that the laws cannot continue indefinitely. Why the paper changed lab strategy overnight: it turned "is a 100× bigger model worth it?" into "how much lower will the loss be?", with an answer. GPT-3 (L145), with 175B parameters on 300B tokens, is roughly this allocation in practice.

Limits & what broke
  • Loss is not capability. The laws predict average next-token loss. Downstream abilities can improve smoothly, abruptly or not at all as loss falls (L206). Hold that distinction firmly: a power law in loss says nothing direct about when a model can do arithmetic.
  • The allocation was wrong. Hoffmann et al. (L147) found that data and parameters should grow roughly equally. Among the reasons they and later analyses give: a learning-rate schedule held fixed across runs of different lengths, the counting of non-embedding parameters, and fits dominated by small models.
  • Constants are not universal. \(N_c\), \(D_c\) and even the exponents depend on tokenizer, data distribution and architecture family, so the numbers must be re-fit for each setup; the form is what transfers.
  • No repeated data. The laws assume each token is seen roughly once; behaviour with many epochs, now relevant as high-quality data runs short, was not studied here.

Bridge

Two years later, DeepMind trained over 400 models with learning-rate schedules matched to each run's length and reached a different answer: scale parameters and tokens equally, about 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla (L147) is that paper.

Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla)

50m videoLevel 3

Hoffmann et al., NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2203.15556)

Train over 400 models with learning-rate schedules matched to each run's length, estimate the compute-optimal trade-off three independent ways, and all three say the same thing: grow parameters and training tokens in roughly equal proportion, about 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla, 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, uses Gopher's compute and beats it almost everywhere.

Cold open

Everyone had been training models too big on too little data. Gopher (L172) has 280B parameters trained on 300B tokens; Chinchilla has a quarter of the parameters trained on more than four times the tokens, at the same training compute. The abstract reports that Chinchilla outperforms Gopher, GPT-3 (L145) and other larger models on a large range of evaluations, including an average of 67.5% on MMLU, more than 7 points above Gopher. And because it is four times smaller, it is also cheaper to fine-tune and to serve.

The problem

Kaplan et al. (L146) had concluded that optimal model size grows as \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{0.73}\) while data grows only as \(C^{0.27}\), and the field followed: GPT-3, Gopher and others were trained on about 300B tokens regardless of size. If that allocation is wrong, those models wasted a large fraction of their compute. Hoffmann et al. re-estimate the trade-off with a design aimed at the obvious confound: every run uses a cosine learning-rate schedule whose length matches that run's number of training tokens.

The method: three approaches, one answer

Algorithm · three estimates of N_opt(C)
  1. Training curves, minimum envelope. For each of a range of model sizes, train with several different numbers of tokens (schedules matched to each). At each FLOP count, take the lowest loss achieved by any run, and fit how the model size and token count at that envelope grow with compute.
  2. IsoFLOP profiles. Fix nine compute budgets. For each, train models of many sizes, each for exactly as many tokens as the budget allows (\(D = C / 6N\)), plot final loss against model size, fit a parabola in \(\log N\), and read off its minimum.
  3. Parametric fit. Fit all final losses to \(L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\), with a Huber loss on log-loss, and minimize it in closed form under \(C = 6ND\).
Derivation · the optimum of the parametric form

Substitute \(D = C/(6N)\) into \(L = E + AN^{-\alpha} + BD^{-\beta}\):

$$L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{-\beta} N^{\beta}.$$

Set \(dL/dN = -\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} + \beta B (C/6)^{-\beta} N^{\beta - 1} = 0\), so \(N^{\alpha+\beta} = \frac{\alpha A}{\beta B} (C/6)^{\beta}\):

$$N_{\text{opt}} = G \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} = G^{-1} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad G = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}}.$$

The exponents \(a = \beta/(\alpha+\beta)\) and \(b = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) sum to 1. Equal scaling, \(a = b = 0.5\), happens exactly when \(\alpha = \beta\): when parameters and data are equally effective at reducing loss. With the paper's fitted \(\alpha \approx 0.34\), \(\beta \approx 0.28\), this gives \(a \approx 0.46\).

Evidence

The three approaches give \(N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{a}\) with \(a\) of about 0.50, 0.49 and 0.46, against Kaplan's 0.73; the data exponents are correspondingly about 0.50, 0.51 and 0.54. Agreement across three methods with different assumptions is the argument. In practical terms, the paper's table of compute-optimal settings puts a 1B-parameter model at about 20B tokens, 10B parameters at about 200B, and a 175B model at several trillion tokens, an order of magnitude more than GPT-3 saw. The ratio is roughly constant at about 20 tokens per parameter.

To test the prediction, the authors trained Chinchilla at Gopher's compute budget with the predicted allocation: 70B parameters, 1.4T tokens (20 per parameter). It outperforms Gopher on the large majority of their evaluation suite, language modelling, reading comprehension, question answering, common sense and MMLU, and outperforms larger models such as GPT-3 and Megatron-Turing NLG on most of them.

Why Kaplan differed. The authors' main explanation is the learning-rate schedule. Kaplan et al. used one schedule length for all runs, and read off losses at intermediate points of runs whose cosine schedule had not finished decaying, which overstates the loss of short runs and so favours bigger models trained for fewer tokens. They also note that Kaplan's models were mostly small, and that the compute frontier curves slightly at larger scale. This is subtle and important: a hyperparameter choice that looks like housekeeping changed the conclusion that shaped a generation of models.

Limits & what broke
  • Training-optimal is not deployment-optimal. The 20:1 ratio minimizes training compute for a target loss. A deployed model is run for many more tokens than it was trained on, and inference cost scales with its size, so it pays to over-train a smaller model past the Chinchilla point. LLaMA (L168) deliberately trained a 7B model on about 1T tokens, far more than 20 per parameter, and later families pushed further (L170); inference-aware scaling laws formalize this (L351).
  • Approach 3 is the shakiest. Taken as published, the parametric fit recommends a noticeably smaller model for Gopher's budget than approaches 1 and 2. A replication attempt (Besiroglu et al. 2024) refit the paper's data and found the published fit inconsistent with the other two approaches and its confidence intervals implausibly narrow; their refit agrees with roughly 20 tokens per parameter.
  • The Kaplan gap has more than one cause. Later re-analyses attribute much of it to other factors as well: counting only non-embedding parameters at small scale (Pearce & Song 2024), and the cost of the output layer, warm-up length and optimizer tuning (Porian et al. 2024).
  • Data becomes the binding constraint. At 20 tokens per parameter, a 1T-parameter model needs about 20T tokens of good text. That is the story of 2023–2026: data quality, filtering, multiple epochs (Muennighoff et al. 2023 found a few repetitions nearly as good as fresh data) and synthetic data.

Transformer-XL: Attentive Language Models Beyond a Fixed-Length Context

35m videoLevel 3

Dai, Yang, Yang, Carbonell, Le & Salakhutdinov, ACL 2019 (arXiv:1901.02860)

Cache the previous segment's hidden states, without gradient, and let the current segment attend to them, so context flows across segment boundaries. Make that consistent with a relative positional encoding derived from a four-term decomposition of the attention score. The result is longer effective context and far faster evaluation.

Cold open

Context fragmentation, illustrated: a transformer language model trained on fixed 512-token chunks of a book treats each chunk as a separate world. The first words of every chunk are predicted with no context at all, and chunks are cut wherever the count falls, mid-sentence. At evaluation, the standard workaround slides the window one token at a time and recomputes everything. The abstract reports that Transformer-XL learns dependencies about 80% longer than RNNs and 450% longer than vanilla transformers, and is up to 1,800+ times faster at evaluation.

The problem

A transformer (L129) attends only within its window, so its longest dependency is bounded by the segment length, and information never crosses segment boundaries during training. RNNs carry a state forward indefinitely but struggle to use it. The authors want both: the transformer's direct attention within a span, and a recurrent way to reach beyond it.

The method: segment-level recurrence

Definition · recurrence with a stop-gradient memory

Let \(h_\tau^{n-1}\) be layer \(n{-}1\)'s states for segment \(\tau\) (length \(L\)). For the next segment, extend the keys and values with the cached states:

$$\tilde h_{\tau+1}^{n-1} = \big[\operatorname{SG}(h_{\tau}^{n-1}) \circ h_{\tau+1}^{n-1}\big], \qquad q = h_{\tau+1}^{n-1} W_q, \quad k = \tilde h_{\tau+1}^{n-1} W_k, \quad v = \tilde h_{\tau+1}^{n-1} W_v,$$

where \(\circ\) concatenates along the length axis and \(\operatorname{SG}\) stops gradients. Queries come only from the current segment; keys and values span the cached segment too.

The stop-gradient is the design point to dwell on. The memory costs memory, \(O(\text{layers} \times M \times d)\) for \(M\) cached positions, but not gradient computation: backpropagation stays within the current segment. That is truncated backpropagation through time applied to a transformer, and it is the direct ancestor of the KV cache in every decoder serving system (L338), which reuses earlier keys and values in exactly this way.

The method: why absolute positions break, and the four terms

With absolute encodings, every segment uses positions \(U_{1:L}\). Token \(j\) of the cached segment and token \(j\) of the current one get the same encoding, so the model cannot tell which segment a key came from. Positions must be relative. Write the absolute-position score between query \(i\) and key \(j\) with embeddings \(E\) and position vectors \(U\), and expand it:

Derivation · the four-term decomposition $$A^{\text{abs}}_{i,j} = \underbrace{E_{x_i}^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_k E_{x_j}}_{(a)} + \underbrace{E_{x_i}^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_k U_j}_{(b)} + \underbrace{U_i^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_k E_{x_j}}_{(c)} + \underbrace{U_i^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_k U_j}_{(d)} .$$

Transformer-XL makes three substitutions: replace the key's absolute position \(U_j\) with a sinusoidal relative encoding \(R_{i-j}\) (no learned parameters); replace the query-side position terms \(U_i^\top W_q^\top\) with two learned vectors \(u\) and \(v\), since with relative positions the query's own absolute position should not matter; and give content and position separate key matrices \(W_{k,E}\) and \(W_{k,R}\):

$$A^{\text{rel}}_{i,j} = \underbrace{E_{x_i}^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_{k,E} E_{x_j}}_{\text{content–content}} + \underbrace{E_{x_i}^{\top} W_q^{\top} W_{k,R} R_{i-j}}_{\text{content–position}} + \underbrace{u^{\top} W_{k,E} E_{x_j}}_{\text{global content bias}} + \underbrace{v^{\top} W_{k,R} R_{i-j}}_{\text{global position bias}} .$$

Each term is readable: (a) is content-based addressing; (b) lets what a token is decide how much it cares about distance; (c) says some keys are important regardless of who asks; (d) is a learned preference over distances, such as recency. Computing (b) and (d) naively needs a vector for every \((i, j)\) pair; the paper computes them for each distance once and then shifts rows, so the cost is linear in length per query.

Evidence

The abstract reports state-of-the-art results at the time on five language-modelling benchmarks: 0.99 bits per character on enwik8, 1.08 on text8, perplexity 18.3 on WikiText-103, 21.8 on One Billion Word and 54.5 on Penn Treebank without fine-tuning. Ablations show the two pieces belong together: a cache with absolute encodings does poorly, and their relative scheme outperforms earlier relative encodings (such as Shaw et al.'s) when combined with recurrence. The authors also propose a measure of how far back context actually helps, on which Transformer-XL's effective context is far longer than an RNN's or a vanilla transformer's, and show coherent generated articles thousands of tokens long.

Limits & what broke
  • The memory is not learned as memory. With no gradient into the cache, the model cannot learn to store information for later use, only to read what happens to be there; effective context is still bounded by memory length and depth.
  • The 1,800× is against a weak baseline, a vanilla model that recomputes the whole window for every predicted token. Any decoder with a KV cache gets most of that reuse.
  • Order constraints. Training must process segments of each stream in order, which complicates shuffling and batching.
  • Superseded positions. The four-term scheme is expressive but heavy; RoPE (L132) gets relative behaviour with a rotation and no extra terms, and ALiBi (L133) with a single bias. Compressive memories (Rae et al. 2020) extended the cache idea.

Bridge

Recurrence extends context by carrying states forward. The other route is to make attention itself cheaper so the window can simply be longer: Longformer and Big Bird (L149) replace full attention with sparse patterns of local windows, global tokens and random links.

Longformer and Big Bird

40m videoLevel 3

Beltagy, Peters & Cohan 2020 (arXiv:2004.05150); Zaheer et al. NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2007.14062)

Replace full self-attention with a fixed sparse pattern: a local sliding window for every token, a few global tokens that see and are seen by everything, and, in Big Bird, a handful of random links. Cost becomes linear in sequence length, BERT-style models read 4,096 tokens instead of 512, and Big Bird proves the sparse model keeps the expressive power of the dense one, at a price in depth.

Cold open

The \(O(n^2)\) wall: at 512 tokens a BERT attention map has about 260k entries per head; at 4,096 it has about 16.8M, and memory runs out long before compute does. These two papers escape the wall the same way, by deciding in advance which query–key pairs to compute. Both are best understood as pictures, so draw the masks.

The problem

Long documents (scientific papers, legal texts, multi-paragraph question answering, genomes) exceed BERT's 512-token window (L137), and the usual fixes, truncating or chunking with separate encodings, lose cross-chunk information. Transformer-XL's recurrence (L148) suits left-to-right language modelling, but bidirectional encoders for QA and classification need every token to see the whole document in one pass. The goal is attention whose cost grows linearly with \(n\) while preserving long-range information flow.

The method: Longformer's patterns

Definition · Longformer attention
  • Sliding window: each token attends to \(w/2\) tokens on each side. Cost \(O(n \times w)\). Stacking \(\ell\) layers gives a receptive field of about \(\ell \times w\), the convolution argument of L128.
  • Dilated sliding window: the window skips \(d - 1\) positions between attended tokens, widening the reach to about \(\ell \times d \times w\) at the same cost. Used on some heads in higher layers, with different dilations per head, so some heads stay local and others look far.
  • Global attention: a few task-chosen positions (the [CLS] token for classification, all question tokens for QA) attend to every token, and every token attends to them. The relation is symmetric, and global attention uses its own projections \(Q_g, K_g, V_g\).

Global tokens are what preserve BERT-style behaviour: a classifier's [CLS] state can aggregate the whole document in one layer, and question tokens can be matched against every candidate answer span. Choosing them per task is the price; the pattern is not learned. Longformer is initialized from a RoBERTa checkpoint (L138), with its 512 learned position embeddings copied repeatedly to cover 4,096 positions, and then trained further with masked LM, a cheap way to get a long-document model.

The method: Big Bird's random links and the theory

Big Bird combines three parts: \(w\) window neighbours, \(g\) global tokens (either existing tokens made global, the ITC variant, or extra tokens added for the purpose, ETC), and \(r\) random keys per query. The motivation is graph-theoretic. Treat tokens as nodes and computed attention pairs as edges: a window alone is a long chain, where information needs many layers to travel; adding a few random edges makes a small-world graph in which any two nodes are a short path apart, and global tokens make every pair at most two hops apart.

Theorem · Big Bird's expressivity results (stated informally)

(1) Sparse attention with global tokens is a universal approximator of continuous sequence-to-sequence functions, like full attention. (2) With full attention replaced by such a sparse mechanism, transformers remain Turing complete. (3) But there is no free lunch: the authors exhibit a natural task that full attention solves with a constant number of layers, while any sparse mechanism with about \(n\) edges needs on the order of \(n\) layers, up to log factors.

Be more useful than the headline. The positive results show that sparsity does not limit what can be represented in principle; the constructions may need many more layers and say nothing about learnability or efficiency at practical depth. Result (3) is the honest part: some computations really do require dense interactions.

Evidence

Longformer reports state-of-the-art character-level language modelling on text8 and enwik8 at the time, and, as a pretrained encoder, consistent gains over RoBERTa on long-document tasks: WikiHop, TriviaQA and HotpotQA question answering, and long-document classification. Its encoder–decoder variant (LED) handles summarization of full scientific papers. Big Bird, with sequences of 4,096 tokens (eight times BERT's) on comparable hardware, reports strong results on question answering (Natural Questions, HotpotQA, TriviaQA, WikiHop), on summarization of long documents (arXiv, PubMed, BigPatent) and on genomics tasks, where DNA sequences are naturally very long. The evidence supports "long context helps on these tasks" and "sparse attention makes it affordable"; it does not show that sparse attention is as good as dense attention at the same length, which neither paper could afford to run.

Limits & what broke
  • Sparse is hard to make fast. GPUs are built for dense matrix products. Irregular patterns mean scattered memory access and little reuse, so Longformer needed custom kernels (written with TVM) for dilation, and a chunked implementation otherwise; Big Bird "blockifies" its pattern into dense blocks (of 64 tokens) so the hardware can cope. Asymptotic savings appear only at long lengths.
  • Dense attention came back. FlashAttention (L153) made exact dense attention fast and memory-efficient by reorganizing it around the memory hierarchy, and most later models kept dense attention, with sliding-window layers as the main surviving sparse pattern. The hardware is part of the algorithm.
  • Hand-designed, task-specific choices. Which tokens are global must be decided per task, and the random pattern is fixed, not learned.
  • Which tasks need 4,096+ tokens? Fewer than claimed. Many "long" benchmarks can be solved by finding one relevant passage, and strong truncation or retrieval baselines close much of the gap. Long-context evaluation itself became a research topic (the Long Range Arena of Tay et al. 2021, and later needle-in-a-haystack tests).

Bridge

Longformer and Big Bird fix the pattern in advance. Reformer (L150) lets the content choose: it hashes queries and keys so that similar vectors land in the same bucket and attends only within buckets, and adds two memory tricks that outlived it.

Reformer: The Efficient Transformer

40m videoLevel 3

Kitaev, Kaiser & Levskaya, ICLR 2020 (arXiv:2001.04451)

Three independent ideas for fitting very long sequences in memory: locality-sensitive-hashing attention, which lets similar queries and keys find each other in \(O(n \log n)\); reversible residual layers, which recompute activations instead of storing them for every layer; and chunked feed-forward computation. The memory savings are real; the speed savings appear only at very long lengths. Asymptotics are not speed.

Cold open

Three efficiency ideas in one paper, and only one of them is about attention. The authors begin with memory accounting: the weights of even a large transformer layer fit comfortably on one accelerator, but activations stored for backpropagation scale with depth times sequence length, the feed-forward activations are several times wider than the model, and the attention matrix grows with the square of the length. At 64K tokens, every one of these is a problem, and the paper attacks all three.

The problem

For a sequence of length \(n\), width \(d\), feed-forward width \(d_{ff}\) and \(N\) layers, training memory has three large terms: the \(n \times n\) attention scores, the \(n \times d_{ff}\) feed-forward activations, and the \(N \times n \times d\) stored layer inputs that backpropagation needs. Fixed sparse patterns (L149) address only the first and need the pattern chosen in advance. Reformer wants the content to decide which keys each query sees, and it wants memory that does not grow with depth.

The method: LSH attention

Softmax attention is dominated by the largest dot products, so each query only really needs its nearest keys. Finding them is a nearest-neighbour search, and locality-sensitive hashing does that approximately in linear time.

Algorithm · LSH attention (one hash round)
  1. Share queries and keys. Use one projection for both and normalize the keys, \(k_j = q_j / \lVert q_j \rVert\), so that "similar query and key" means "close in angle".
  2. Hash. Draw a random matrix \(R \in \R^{d_k \times b/2}\) and assign each vector to bucket \(h(x) = \argmax\big([xR;\, -xR]\big)\), one of \(b\) buckets. Nearby directions land in the same bucket with high probability.
  3. Sort positions by bucket (and by position within a bucket), and cut the sorted sequence into equal chunks of length \(m\).
  4. Attend within each chunk and to the previous chunk, applying the causal mask on original positions. A token may not attend to itself unless it has no other target, because with shared QK its own key always has the largest dot product.
  5. Repeat with \(n_{\text{rounds}}\) independent hash functions and combine the results, which lowers the chance that two similar vectors are separated by every hash.

The shared-QK constraint is what makes the hashing coherent: if queries and keys lived in different spaces, a query and its best key could hash to different buckets. Its cost is expressiveness, since a token's key can no longer differ from its query. The authors report that sharing did not hurt in their experiments, and that LSH attention with enough hash rounds approaches full attention, while a single round is noticeably worse.

The method: reversible layers and chunking

Derivation · reversible residual blocks

Following RevNets (Gomez et al. 2017), split the activations into two halves and compute

$$y_1 = x_1 + \operatorname{Attention}(x_2), \qquad y_2 = x_2 + \operatorname{FeedForward}(y_1).$$

The inputs can be recovered exactly from the outputs:

$$x_2 = y_2 - \operatorname{FeedForward}(y_1), \qquad x_1 = y_1 - \operatorname{Attention}(x_2).$$

So during the backward pass each layer's input is recomputed from its output instead of being stored. Only the final layer's activations need to be kept: activation memory drops from \(O(N n d)\) to \(O(n d)\), independent of depth, at the price of roughly one extra forward computation per layer.

Chunking. The feed-forward layer acts on each position independently, so it can be evaluated on blocks of positions one at a time, keeping only one block's \(d_{ff}\)-wide activations in memory; the same trick applies to the output softmax over a large vocabulary. Both reversibility and chunking trade compute for memory, the "recompute rather than store" theme that runs from automatic differentiation (L32) through gradient checkpointing (L341) to FlashAttention (L153).

Evidence

On a synthetic duplication task (copy a random sequence), LSH attention learns the task, and accuracy improves with the number of hash rounds used at evaluation, even beyond the number used in training. On enwik8 with sequences of 64K tokens and on ImageNet-64 image generation, the authors report that shared QK and reversible layers leave bits-per-character or bits-per-dimension essentially unchanged relative to a standard transformer, and that LSH attention with 8 hash rounds matches full attention. The speed plot is the honest figure: full attention's time per step grows with length while LSH attention's grows slowly, but at short and moderate lengths full attention is faster. LSH only pays off at sequences of several thousand tokens and beyond, and more hash rounds push the crossover further out.

Limits & what broke
  • Large constants. Hashing, sorting, gathering and scattering, and multiple rounds (each roughly another pass) cost real time; "\(O(n \log n)\)" hides all of it. For the 1K–4K lengths most models used, dense attention was simply faster.
  • Approximation and constraints. Similar items can still miss each other; shared QK and the no-self-attention rule restrict the model; random hashes add variance; causal generation with caching is awkward.
  • Why it is admired more than used. The LSH attention did not become standard: IO-aware dense attention (L153) made exact attention fast enough. The reusable parts were the memory ideas: reversible layers appear wherever activation memory is the bottleneck, and chunked feed-forward computation is a standard option in libraries.
  • Content-based sparsity lived on in other forms, for example clustering-based routing attention, and in retrieval-style attention over external memories.

Bridge

Month 5 ends where Month 6 begins. The transformer is the architecture; scaling laws say how big to make it; and its \(n^2\) attention is the problem that Linformer, Performers and FlashAttention (L151–L153) attack next, the last of them by changing how attention uses the memory hierarchy rather than what it computes.
