Month 1 · Days 1–30 · Lessons 1–30

Foundations: The Papers Everything Rests On

Month 1 reads the thirty papers that modern machine learning still stands on, from Turing's imitation game and Shannon's entropy to MCMC and Gaussian processes. In between come perceptrons, kernels, boosting, learning theory, EM, hidden Markov models, the Kalman filter and belief propagation, which supply the vocabulary the later months assume: margins, bias and variance, likelihoods and latent variables, conditioning and sampling. Month 2 picks up neural networks where Minsky and Papert left them, with backpropagation.

By the end of this month you can

  • Compute entropy and information gain and use them to grow a decision tree, and tell the two naive Bayes event models for text apart.
  • Derive the perceptron and LMS updates, state when the perceptron converges, and prove why a bounded-order perceptron cannot compute parity.
  • Set up the maximum-margin classifier, derive its dual, and explain why the kernel trick needs only inner products.
  • State PAC learnability and the VC bound, and use the bias–variance decomposition to diagnose a model.
  • Explain bagging and random forests as variance reduction and boosting as stagewise loss minimisation, and derive XGBoost's optimal leaf weights.
  • Derive EM for a Gaussian mixture, k-means as its hard-assignment limit, and PCA and Fisher's discriminant as eigenproblems.
  • Run HMM forward–backward and Viterbi, a Kalman predict–update step, belief propagation on a tree, Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs sampling and exact GP regression, and say what each one assumes.

L1 · DAY 1

Computing Machinery and Intelligence

35m videoLevel 1

Turing, Mind 1950

Turing replaces the unanswerable question "Can machines think?" with an operational one: can a machine do as well as a person in a game of conversation over a teleprinter? He then argues that the real route to such a machine is not programming an adult mind but educating a child one, which is machine learning stated in 1950.

Cold open

The paper opens with "I propose to consider the question, 'Can machines think?'" and, within a paragraph, refuses to answer it. If the meaning of "machine" and "think" is settled by how people use the words, Turing says, the answer becomes a matter for a statistical survey like a Gallup poll, which he calls absurd. Later he is blunter: the original question is "too meaningless to deserve discussion". What survives of the paper is the replacement he offers, and the two paragraphs near the end where he sketches how one might actually build the machine.

The problem

In 1950 there were a handful of stored-program computers and no field called artificial intelligence. Arguments about machine minds were arguments about definitions. Turing's move is the one every benchmark paper since has made: if a concept cannot be defined, specify a test whose outcome you would accept as evidence. Sections 3–5 restrict the "machines" to digital computers and explain why that is no restriction: a digital computer can mimic any discrete-state machine given enough storage and the right program. Universality makes the question about software, not hardware.

The method: the game as actually described

Definition · the imitation game (Section 1)

A man (A), a woman (B) and an interrogator (C) of either sex. C, in a separate room, knows the other two only as X and Y and communicates with them by teleprinter. C's goal is to say correctly "X is A and Y is B" or the reverse. A's goal is to cause a wrong identification; B's goal is to help C. Then: "What will happen when a machine takes the part of A in this game?" The new question is whether C will be wrong as often in the machine version as in the human one.

Two details matter and are usually lost. First, the criterion is relative: the machine is measured against how well a human deceiver does, not against an absolute fooling rate. Second, there is a human foil in the room, so C is comparing two live conversations rather than rating one transcript for human-likeness.

Prediction · Section 6

Turing believed that "in about fifty years' time" computers "with a storage capacity of about 109" could be programmed to play so well "that an average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent. chance of making the right identification after five minutes of questioning." He measures storage in binary digits, so 109 is roughly 125 MB.

Read it carefully. It is a forecast about the year 2000, for an average interrogator and a five-minute limit: "at most 70% right", that is, "at least 30% wrong". It is not a definition of passing. The popular "a machine passes if it fools 30% of judges" turns a hedged forecast into a pass mark Turing never set. He also predicts that by the century's end "one will be able to speak of machines thinking without expecting to be contradicted", a forecast about language use, not about machines.

The method: nine objections

Section 6 is the longest part of the paper. Turing states each objection to thinking machines in its strongest form and answers it.

#ObjectionTuring's reply, compressed
1Theological: thinking belongs to an immortal soul given only to humansThat restricts God's omnipotence: He could give a machine a soul. Theological arguments also have a poor record (Galileo).
2"Heads in the sand": the consequences would be too dreadfulNot an argument; consolation is more appropriate than refutation.
3Mathematical: Gödel-style limits on machinesNobody has shown that humans escape the same limits.
4Consciousness (Jefferson): a machine must feel, not merely sayTaken strictly this is solipsism; we grant other people minds on behavioural evidence.
5Various disabilities: "a machine can never do X"An induction from the few machines people have seen.
6Lady Lovelace: the Analytical Engine "has no pretensions to originate anything"Machines often surprise him; a learning machine is not limited to what its programmer foresaw.
7Continuity of the nervous systemA discrete machine can imitate a continuous one well enough.
8Informality of behaviourConfuses "rules of conduct" with "laws of behaviour".
9Extra-sensory perceptionTaken seriously; he proposes a "telepathy-proof room".

The Lovelace objection is the one that still bites, because it is the ancestor of "it only predicts the next token". Turing's real answer to it is not in Section 6 but in Section 7: build a machine whose behaviour comes from experience rather than from its programmer.

The method: the child machine

Algorithm · Turing's learning machine (Section 7)
  1. Do not try to program an adult mind. Program a child's mind, which Turing imagines as "like a notebook": little mechanism, lots of blank sheets.
  2. Split the problem into three parts: the initial state of the mind, the education it receives, and other experience.
  3. Educate it with a system of rewards and punishments, so that behaviour followed by punishment becomes less likely to recur and behaviour followed by reward more likely.
  4. Improve the child machine itself by trial and error, as evolution would: structure ≈ hereditary material, changes ≈ mutations, the experimenter's judgement ≈ natural selection.

Those paragraphs contain supervised teaching, reinforcement learning (Month 8) and search by variation and selection. Turing adds that a learning machine should include a random element, and that its teacher will often be "very largely ignorant of quite what is going on inside": Month 11's interpretability problem, stated in passing.

Evidence

This is an argument, not an experiment, so the evidence is the reasoning and how its forecast fared. Programs of the year 2000 did not meet it under serious conditions. Large language models changed that: recent three-party replications with modern chatbots report interrogators picking the machine as the human about as often as, or more often than, the real human. That is a result about deception under short time limits, and it says less about "thinking" than the headlines suggested.

Limits & what broke
  • The Loebner Prize (from 1991) ran yearly contests that drifted from Turing's design: in its early years, restricted topics and judges ranking terminals one conversation at a time, plus an annual medal for "most human-like" that rewarded tricks such as deliberate typos and evasive personas. Critics (Shieber's 1994 "Lessons from a Restricted Turing Test" is the classic) argued it measured gullibility under short conversations rather than capability.
  • Persona gaming. A 2014 event claimed a pass for a chatbot posing as a 13-year-old non-native speaker, using the "30% of judges" reading. The persona lowered expectations; the pass mark was a misreading. Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) had already shown how readily judges attribute understanding to pattern matching.
  • Behaviour versus mind. Searle's Chinese Room (1980) is the standard reply that passing tells you nothing about understanding. Turing's answer is objection 4: you grant other people minds on the same evidence.

Bridge

Turing reduces intelligence to what can be sent down a teleprinter line. Day 2 is the paper that made "what can be sent down a line" a quantity: Shannon's entropy, measured in bits, and the reason every loss function you will meet this year is a logarithm (Day 2).

  5. Derivation. An interrogator who is right 70% of the time is being compared with chance (50%). Using the normal approximation to the binomial, how many independent games do you need to detect the difference with a one-sided 5% test and 80% power?

L2 · DAY 2

A Mathematical Theory of Communication

55m videoLevel 1

Shannon, Bell System Tech J. 1948

Shannon defines information as resolved uncertainty, proves that \(H = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i\) is the only measure satisfying three natural requirements, and shows that every noisy channel has a capacity below which communication can be made arbitrarily reliable. The logarithm in every loss function you will train this year starts here.

Cold open

A language model's training loss is \(-\log q(\text{next token})\), averaged. A classifier's is \(-\log q(\text{true class})\). Why a logarithm, and not a squared error or a plain probability? Because \(-\log_2 p\) is the number of bits an optimal code spends on an event of probability \(p\), and "make the data cheap to encode" turns out to be the same as "make the data likely". That equivalence is this paper.

The problem

Before 1948, engineers had Nyquist's results on signalling speed and Hartley's 1928 proposal to measure information by the logarithm of the number of possible messages. Hartley's measure ignores probability: a message you could have predicted counts as much as a surprising one. Nobody could say how much a noisy channel could carry, and the working assumption was that reducing errors meant slowing down, with zero error requiring zero rate. Shannon showed that assumption to be false.

The method: the channel, with meaning removed

The key reframing is that a message is a selection. If the source picks among outcomes with probabilities \(p_1, \dots, p_n\), the information it produces is a property of that distribution, not of any particular message. Shannon then asks what function \(H(p_1, \dots, p_n)\) should measure "how much choice is involved".

Derivation · the uniqueness of entropy (Section 6, Appendix 2)

Require (1) continuity in the \(p_i\); (2) monotonicity: with \(n\) equally likely outcomes, \(A(n) := H(\tfrac1n, \dots, \tfrac1n)\) increases with \(n\); (3) decomposability: if a choice is broken into successive choices, \(H\) is the weighted sum of the parts. Shannon's example: \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16) = H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac12 H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\).

Step 1, equal probabilities. A choice among \(s^m\) equally likely outcomes is \(m\) successive choices among \(s\), so by (3) \(A(s^m) = m\,A(s)\). Fix \(s, t \ge 2\) and any \(n\); pick \(m\) with \(s^m \le t^n < s^{m+1}\). Taking logs, \(\frac mn \le \frac{\log t}{\log s} < \frac mn + \frac1n\). By (2), \(m A(s) \le n A(t) \le (m+1) A(s)\), so \(\big|\frac{A(t)}{A(s)} - \frac{\log t}{\log s}\big| \le \frac 1n\). Since \(n\) is arbitrary, \(A(t) = K \log t\) with \(K > 0\).

Step 2, rational probabilities. Let \(p_i = n_i / N\) with \(N = \sum_j n_j\). Choosing among \(N\) equally likely outcomes is the same as first choosing group \(i\) with probability \(p_i\), then one of its \(n_i\) members. By (3):

$$K \log N = H(p_1, \dots, p_n) + K \sum_i p_i \log n_i \;\Longrightarrow\; H = -K \sum_i p_i \log \frac{n_i}{N} = -K \sum_i p_i \log p_i .$$

Step 3. Continuity extends this to irrational \(p_i\). The constant \(K\) only fixes the unit.

Bits and nats. With base-2 logs the unit is the binary digit, "or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey". Shannon notes that base \(e\) is convenient for calculus; those units are now called nats, and \(1 \text{ nat} = 1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443\) bits. PyTorch's cross_entropy uses natural logs, so its losses are in nats.

The method: two variables, and capacity

For a pair of random variables Shannon defines the joint entropy \(H(x, y)\) and the conditional entropy \(H_x(y) = -\sum_{i,j} p(i, j) \log p_i(j)\) (his notation for \(H(Y \mid X)\)), and proves \(H(x, y) = H(x) + H_x(y)\) and \(H_x(y) \le H(y)\): conditioning never increases uncertainty on average. For a noisy channel with input \(x\) and output \(y\), the uncertainty about the input that remains after seeing the output is the equivocation \(H_y(x)\), and the rate of transmission is

$$R = H(x) - H_y(x) = H(y) - H_x(y) = H(x) + H(y) - H(x, y).$$

That quantity is what we now call the mutual information \(I(X;Y)\); the name came later, the definition is here. The capacity is \(C = \max_{p(x)} R\).

Theorem · noisy-channel coding (Theorem 11)

If a source's entropy rate \(H\) is at most \(C\), there is a coding system that transmits it over the channel with an arbitrarily small frequency of errors. If \(H > C\), the equivocation can be brought down to \(H - C + \varepsilon\) but not below \(H - C\).

Worked number: a binary channel flips each bit with probability 0.01. The equivocation per symbol is \(-0.99\log_2 0.99 - 0.01\log_2 0.01 \approx 0.081\) bits, so the capacity is about 0.919 bits per use. A 1% error rate costs 8% of the line, not "most of it", and the loss can be bought back by coding rather than by slowing down.

The method: English as a stochastic source

Section 3 builds a series of approximations to English: random letters, then letters with the right frequencies, then digram and trigram statistics, then first- and second-order word models. Shannon sampled them by opening a book at random and copying what followed an occurrence of the preceding letters or word. The second-order word sample begins "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH WRITER…". These are n-gram language models, sampled by hand in 1948. He estimates that English, counting structure over about eight letters, is roughly 50% redundant; his 1951 follow-up, using human guessing, put long-range English at around one bit per letter.

Redundancy is why compression works, and the noiseless coding theorem makes it exact: a source of entropy \(H\) bits per symbol cannot be encoded in fewer than \(H\) bits per symbol on average, and codes with lengths near \(-\log_2 p\) get within a bit of it. A subword tokenizer is a code of this kind. It spends one token on a frequent string such as "tion", and BPE (Day 130) began life as a compression algorithm.

import math
from collections import Counter

def entropy_bits(text):
    counts = Counter(text)
    n = len(text)
    return -sum(c / n * math.log2(c / n) for c in counts.values())

text = ("the fundamental problem of communication is that of reproducing "
        "at one point either exactly or approximately a message selected at another point")
H = entropy_bits(text)
print(f"{len(set(text))} symbols, H = {H:.3f} bits/char = {H * math.log(2):.3f} nats/char")
print(f"uniform over the same alphabet: {math.log2(len(set(text))):.3f} bits")
print(f"perplexity 2**H = {2 ** H:.1f} equally likely choices per character")

Unigram entropy of Shannon's own sentence: about 4.0 bits per character against 4.4 for a uniform choice over its 21 symbols. Context (digrams, words) lowers it much further.

Evidence

The paper's evidence is proof plus illustration. The theorems come with proofs that later mathematicians (McMillan, Feinstein, Khinchin) tightened to modern standards; the results survived intact. The English experiments are demonstrations, not measurements, and Shannon presents them that way. The capacity theorem's proof averages over randomly chosen codebooks and shows that the average code is good, so some code must be. It proves existence and constructs nothing.

Straight line to cross-entropy

Train a model \(q\) on data from \(p\). The expected code length when you encode with \(q\) is the cross-entropy \(H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x)\). Minimizing it over \(q\) is maximum likelihood, and it is at least \(H(p)\), with equality only at \(q = p\). The gap, \(\sum_x p \log (p/q)\), is the Kullback–Leibler divergence. KL is not in this paper: Kullback and Leibler published it in 1951. Perplexity is \(2^{H}\) with \(H\) in bits (equivalently \(e^{H}\) in nats): the number of equally likely choices that would produce the same uncertainty, since a uniform choice among \(k\) has \(H = \log_2 k\). A language model's reported perplexity uses its cross-entropy on held-out text, which upper-bounds the true entropy rate.

Limits & what broke
  • Existence is not construction. Practical codes close to capacity took about 45 years: turbo codes (1993) and the rediscovery of Gallager's 1960s LDPC codes in the 1990s.
  • Meaning is excluded by design. A random string has maximal entropy and no content. Shannon's 1956 editorial "The Bandwagon" warned against applying the theory loosely to psychology, linguistics and biology.
  • Entropy estimates depend on the model. Any figure for "the entropy of English" is an upper bound from some predictor, and better predictors keep lowering it.

Bridge

Shannon's unit is the binary digit. Five years earlier, two neuroscientists had argued that a neuron is an all-or-none device computing with exactly such digits, and that networks of them compute logic. That is Day 3.

  2. Verify Shannon's example \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16) = H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac12 H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\) numerically. Which axiom is it illustrating?
  3. Show that \(R = H(x) - H_y(x)\) equals \(H(y) - H_x(y)\) using only \(H(x, y) = H(x) + H_x(y) = H(y) + H_y(x)\).
  4. A model reports a validation loss of 2.3 nats per token. What is its perplexity, and how many bits per token is that?
    Answer

    Perplexity \(e^{2.3} \approx 10.0\); \(2.3 / \ln 2 \approx 3.32\) bits, and \(2^{3.32} \approx 10\) as well.

  5. Derivation. Prove \(H(p, q) \ge H(p)\) with equality iff \(q = p\), using \(\ln z \le z - 1\).

    \(H(p) - H(p,q) = \sum_x p \ln \frac qp \le \sum_x p \big(\frac qp - 1\big) = \sum_x q - 1 \le 0\), with equality iff \(q/p = 1\) wherever \(p > 0\).

L3 · DAY 3

A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity

30m videoLevel 1

McCulloch & Pitts, 1943

If a neuron is an all-or-none threshold device, then a network of neurons is a machine for computing logical propositions, and with feedback loops it is as powerful as any finite automaton. The paper invents the formal neuron and the idea of a neural network, and contains no learning at all.

Cold open

A neurophysiologist (Warren McCulloch) and a self-taught logician barely out of his teens (Walter Pitts) publish a paper in the Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics whose proofs are written in the notation of Carnap and of Principia Mathematica. Almost nobody can read it. Two years later, von Neumann's draft report on the EDVAC describes the computer's logic using McCulloch–Pitts neurons as the elements. The stored-program computer and the neural network share a 1943 ancestor.

The problem

Neurophysiology in the 1930s knew that nerve impulses are all-or-none and that synapses can excite or inhibit. Logic knew, from Turing (1936), what computation is. Nobody had connected the two: could a brain built from such cells compute anything a logician would recognise? The paper's question is about expressive power, not about how brains come to compute what they do.

The method: five assumptions

Definition · the McCulloch–Pitts neuron (the paper's assumptions)
  1. The activity of the neuron is an all-or-none process.
  2. A fixed number of synapses must be excited within the period of latent addition to excite a neuron, independent of previous activity and position on the neuron (a fixed threshold).
  3. The only significant delay is synaptic delay, so time advances in discrete steps.
  4. The activity of any inhibitory synapse absolutely prevents excitation of the neuron at that time.
  5. The structure of the net does not change with time.

In modern form: \(y(t+1) = 1\) if no inhibitory input is active at \(t\) and \(\sum_{j \in \text{exc}} x_j(t) \ge \theta\); otherwise \(y(t+1) = 0\).

Assumption 4 is not a large negative weight. A single active inhibitory synapse vetoes the cell whatever the excitation, so inhibition here is a logical NOT gate, not a term in a sum. Excitatory synapses all count equally; a cell can only weigh one input more than another by receiving several synapses from it. Assumption 5 rules out learning by fiat.

The method: from cells to logic to automata

With AND, OR and NOT available, any Boolean function can be built, so the paper's first main result follows: every net without circles (a feed-forward net) computes a temporal propositional expression, a logical formula about which inputs fired how many steps ago, and every such expression is realised by some net. The second half treats nets with circles. A loop lets a cell's activity re-excite itself, so the net can remember that something happened at an unspecified time in the past. The authors argue that a net, given a tape and suitable sensors and effectors, computes exactly what a Turing machine computes.

Result · nets with circles are finite automata

A McCulloch–Pitts net with \(n\) cells has at most \(2^n\) activity states and updates them deterministically from its inputs, so it is a finite-state machine; conversely, any finite automaton can be wired up as such a net. Kleene made this precise in 1956 ("Representation of Events in Nerve Nets and Finite Automata"), and the regular expressions he introduced to describe what these nets recognise are the ones in your text editor.

def mp_unit(excite, inhibit, theta):
    """McCulloch-Pitts neuron: any active inhibitory input vetoes firing."""
    if any(inhibit):
        return 0
    return int(sum(excite) >= theta)

AND = lambda a, b: mp_unit([a, b], [], 2)
OR = lambda a, b: mp_unit([a, b], [], 1)
A_AND_NOT_B = lambda a, b: mp_unit([a], [b], 1)
NOT = lambda a: mp_unit([1], [a], 1)          # needs a constantly active input

def XOR(a, b):                                 # two layers, so two synaptic delays
    return OR(A_AND_NOT_B(a, b), A_AND_NOT_B(b, a))

for a in (0, 1):
    for b in (0, 1):
        print(a, b, AND(a, b), OR(a, b), NOT(a), XOR(a, b))

Every weight here was chosen by the designer. Nothing in the paper says how a net could arrive at them by itself.

Evidence

The paper is a set of theorems with proofs, no experiments. What it shows is a sufficiency result: cells obeying the five assumptions are enough for any logical function and, with loops, any finite automaton. It does not show that real brains work this way, and the authors' discussion leans on philosophical consequences (knowledge, perception, psychiatry) more than on physiological tests.

Limits & what broke
  • No learning. Assumption 5 fixes the structure. The authors suggest that apparent learning could be mimicked by a fixed net with circles, but they give no rule for changing a net. Hebb (1949) proposed one; Rosenblatt (Day 4) built one.
  • Inhibition is not absolute. Real inhibitory synapses add graded negative contributions; they do not veto. Replacing the veto with a signed weight is exactly the step to the perceptron.
  • Biology was idealised away. Neurons are not synchronous, thresholds adapt, synaptic strengths vary and change, dendrites compute nonlinearly, and firing rates and spike timing carry information that a single binary bit per step cannot. Lettvin, Maturana, McCulloch and Pitts's own 1959 study of the frog retina found retinal cells doing analogue feature detection, far from the paper's clean logic.
  • Unreadable proofs. The notation kept the results obscure; most later readers learned them through von Neumann and Kleene.

Bridge

Day 3 gives you the unit and a proof that networks of units can compute anything finite. The missing piece is how the weights get set without a designer. Day 4 keeps the threshold unit, replaces the absolute veto with real-valued weights, and adds a rule that adjusts them from errors.

Practice

  1. Read the introduction and the five assumptions closely, and study the paper's Figure 1 (the small example nets). Skim the formal theorems; read Kleene's restatement if the notation defeats you.
  2. Why does an absolute inhibitory veto make "weights" unnecessary in this model, and what can a signed weighted sum express that the veto cannot?
  3. Design a single MP unit computing 3-input majority. Can one unit compute 3-input parity? Why not?
  4. Draw a net with one circle that outputs 1 at every step after input \(a\) has fired at least once. What finite automaton is it?
  5. Derivation. Prove that every Boolean function of \(n\) inputs is computed by a two-layer MP net.
    Proof sketch

    Write \(f\) in disjunctive normal form. For each input pattern with \(f = 1\), make a first-layer unit with excitatory synapses from the inputs that are 1, inhibitory synapses from those that are 0, and threshold equal to the number of 1s (adding a constant excitatory input if that number is 0). It fires exactly on that pattern. A second-layer unit with threshold 1 ORs them.

L4 · DAY 4

The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage

40m videoLevel 1

Rosenblatt, Psych. Review 1958

Rosenblatt keeps the threshold neuron but makes its input weights adjustable and lets errors adjust them, so that a randomly wired network learns to classify from examples. The learning rule that came out of this programme provably finds a separating hyperplane whenever one exists, in at most \((R/\gamma)^2\) mistakes.

Cold open

July 1958: the New York Times reports a Navy-funded "embryo of an electronic computer" that learns by doing, and relays the expectation that such machines would one day walk, talk, see, write, reproduce themselves and be conscious of their existence. The demonstration underneath was an IBM 704 simulation learning to tell whether a card was marked on its left or its right. The hype was absurd; the algorithm was not. It is the direct ancestor of every linear layer you will train.

The problem

McCulloch and Pitts (Day 3) showed that fixed nets of threshold units can compute logic, but a designer had to choose every connection. Hebb (1949) suggested that connections strengthen with use, without a precise rule. Rosenblatt's paper opens with three questions: how is information about the world sensed, in what form is it stored, and how does what is stored influence recognition and behaviour? He rejects the idea that memories are coded symbolic records and argues that information is stored in connection strengths, as a preference for some responses over others.

The method: S-units, A-units, R-units

"Probabilistic" in the title refers to the wiring, not the output. Rosenblatt argued that the brain's detailed connections are effectively random, so its behaviour must be analysed statistically: given random connections, what is the probability that a stimulus activates a given A-unit, and how does the probability of a correct response grow with training? Much of the paper derives such expressions and compares the predicted learning curves with psychology. The 1958 paper describes reinforcement in terms of changing "values" of A-units under several schemes. The clean error-correction rule below is the version Rosenblatt and others made precise around 1962.

The method: the rule and its proof

Take labels \(y \in \{-1, +1\}\), inputs \(x \in \R^d\) (append a constant 1 to absorb the threshold), and prediction \(\hat y = \operatorname{sign}(w \cdot x)\). If the unit errs on a positive example, \(w \cdot x \le 0\), and adding \(x\) raises the score by \(\|x\|^2 > 0\). Symmetrically for negatives. Hence

$$w \leftarrow w + \eta\,(y - \hat y)\,x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w \leftarrow w + 2\eta\, y\, x \ \text{ on a mistake, unchanged otherwise.}$$

Starting from \(w_0 = 0\), \(\eta\) only rescales \(w\) and never changes a prediction, so set \(2\eta = 1\).

Theorem · perceptron convergence (Block 1962, Novikoff 1962)

Suppose some unit vector \(w^*\) separates the data with margin \(\gamma > 0\), meaning \(y_i\, w^* \cdot x_i \ge \gamma\) for all \(i\), and \(\|x_i\| \le R\). Then the perceptron makes at most \((R/\gamma)^2\) mistakes, in any order of presentation.

Proof

Let \(w_k\) be the weights after \(k\) mistakes, \(w_0 = 0\), and suppose the \(k\)-th mistake is on \((x, y)\), so \(w_k = w_{k-1} + y x\) and \(y\, w_{k-1} \cdot x \le 0\).

Alignment grows linearly. \(w_k \cdot w^* = w_{k-1} \cdot w^* + y\, x \cdot w^* \ge w_{k-1} \cdot w^* + \gamma\). By induction, \(w_k \cdot w^* \ge k\gamma\).

Length grows only like \(\sqrt k\). \(\|w_k\|^2 = \|w_{k-1}\|^2 + 2y\, w_{k-1} \cdot x + \|x\|^2 \le \|w_{k-1}\|^2 + R^2\), because the middle term is \(\le 0\) on a mistake. By induction, \(\|w_k\|^2 \le kR^2\).

Combine with Cauchy–Schwarz. \(k\gamma \le w_k \cdot w^* \le \|w_k\|\,\|w^*\| \le \sqrt k\, R\). Squaring, \(k \le R^2/\gamma^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

The proof is a counting argument. Each mistake moves \(w\) a fixed amount towards \(w^*\) but can lengthen it only a little, so the angle between them must close, and it cannot close forever. Note what it needs: linear separability (\(\gamma > 0\)) is the precondition, and the bound depends on the geometry \(R/\gamma\), not on the dimension or the number of examples.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
w_star = np.array([0.6, -0.8])                     # unit-norm separator through the origin
X = rng.uniform(-1, 1, size=(400, 2))
margin = X @ w_star
X, y = X[np.abs(margin) > 0.05], np.sign(margin[np.abs(margin) > 0.05])

gamma = np.min(y * (X @ w_star))                   # margin achieved by w_star
R = np.max(np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1))
w, mistakes, changed = np.zeros(2), 0, True
while changed:                                     # sweep until a clean pass
    changed = False
    for xi, yi in zip(X, y):
        if yi * (w @ xi) <= 0:                     # mistake (w = 0 counts as one)
            w += yi * xi
            mistakes, changed = mistakes + 1, True
print(f"mistakes = {mistakes}, bound (R/gamma)^2 = {(R / gamma) ** 2:.0f}")

This run makes 12 mistakes against a bound of about 717. The bound is a worst case over orderings and datasets; typical runs are far below it.

Evidence

The 1958 paper's evidence is analytical: probability calculations for idealised random networks, with predicted learning curves and a discussion of how they match qualitative facts from animal learning. There are no benchmark experiments in the modern sense. The engineering evidence came next. The Mark I Perceptron at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory had a camera-like array of 20×20 photocells, a patch-board for the random wiring, and weights stored in potentiometers turned by electric motors: learning was physically a motor turning a knob. It learned to recognise simple shapes and letters.

Limits & what broke
  • No differentiable loss. The rule is error-driven, not the gradient of a smooth objective. You can write it as stochastic subgradient descent on \(\sum_{\text{mistakes}} -y\, w \cdot x\), but that criterion is minimised by \(w = 0\) and knows nothing about margin. The convergence proof never uses it.
  • Non-separable data. If no separating hyperplane exists the weights never settle; they keep cycling, bounded but not converging. Which separating hyperplane you get when one exists depends on the order of the examples.
  • One trainable layer. The fixed random A-layer limits what the unit can learn. Rosenblatt studied multi-layer and cross-coupled variants but had no effective way to train hidden units.
  • The hype. The gap between the newspaper claims and a linear classifier set up the backlash of Day 5.

Bridge

A perceptron can only learn what a hyperplane can separate. Day 5 is the book that made that limitation precise, and much sharper than the "it can't do XOR" slogan it is remembered for.

L5 · DAY 5

Perceptrons (Ch. 1–2, 13)

45m videoLevel 1

Minsky & Papert, MIT Press 1969

Minsky and Papert prove that a perceptron whose features each look at only a bounded part of the input cannot compute global properties such as parity or connectedness, however its weights are chosen. The result is about locality, far sharper than "it can't do XOR", and its reception is still argued over.

Cold open

The standard story: a 1969 book proved perceptrons cannot learn XOR, funding for neural networks collapsed, and the field slept until backpropagation in 1986. Almost every clause is wrong or contested. An order-2 perceptron computes XOR easily. The book's real theorems concern how the required feature size grows with the retina. Whether the book caused the funding decline is disputed by historians.

The problem

After Rosenblatt (Day 4) there were convergence theorems but no theory of representation: which predicates can a perceptron compute at all? Learning cannot find weights that do not exist. Minsky and Papert set out to answer this with mathematics, treating the perceptron as an object of "computational geometry", their subtitle.

The method: perceptrons of bounded order

Definition · perceptron and order (Ch. 1)

A retina \(R\) is a finite set of points; a figure \(X \subseteq R\) is the set of points that are on. Given a family \(\Phi\) of partial predicates \(\varphi(X) \in \{0, 1\}\), a predicate \(\psi\) is linear with respect to \(\Phi\) if \(\psi(X) = \big[\sum_{\varphi \in \Phi} \alpha_\varphi\, \varphi(X) > \theta\big]\) for some real \(\alpha_\varphi, \theta\). The support of \(\varphi\) is the set of points it depends on. The order of \(\psi\) is the smallest \(k\) such that \(\psi\) is linear with respect to some family whose members all have support of size at most \(k\).

Each \(\varphi\) may be any Boolean function of its support, computed by any machine. So an order-\(k\) perceptron is already a two-layer network with arbitrary local feature detectors in the first layer. The constraint is that every feature is local; the linear threshold only combines them. A key Chapter 1 fact: every Boolean function of \(k\) points is a linear combination of masks \(\varphi_S(X) = [S \subseteq X]\) with \(|S| \le k\), so one may assume all features are masks.

The method: XOR, twice, then parity

Proof · XOR is not linearly separable

Algebra. Suppose \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 > \theta\) exactly on \((0,1)\) and \((1,0)\). From \((0,0)\): \(0 \le \theta\). From the two positives: \(w_1 > \theta\) and \(w_2 > \theta\), so \(w_1 + w_2 > 2\theta \ge \theta\), which says \((1,1)\) is classified positive. Contradiction.

Geometry. If a half-plane contains two points it contains the segment between them. The segment joining the positives and the segment joining the negatives meet at \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), which would have to lie on both sides of the line.

Theorem · parity has order |R|

The predicate "\(|X|\) is odd" cannot be computed by any perceptron all of whose features have support smaller than the whole retina.

Proof sketch · via the group-invariance theorem (Ch. 2)

Parity is unchanged by any permutation of the points. The group-invariance theorem says that if \(\psi\) is invariant under a group \(G\) and representable with a \(G\)-closed family, it is representable with coefficients that are constant on each \(G\)-orbit, by averaging over \(G\). Under all permutations, masks of the same size form one orbit, so an order-\(k\) representation becomes

$$\psi(X) = \Big[\sum_{j=0}^{k} \beta_j \!\!\sum_{|S| = j}\! [S \subseteq X] > \theta\Big] = \Big[\sum_{j=0}^{k} \beta_j \binom{|X|}{j} > \theta\Big].$$

Then \(P(n) = \sum_j \beta_j \binom{n}{j} - \theta\) is a polynomial in \(n = |X|\) of degree at most \(k\), and it must change sign between every consecutive \(n = 0, 1, \dots, |R|\). That is \(|R|\) sign changes, so \(P\) has at least \(|R|\) roots and \(k \ge |R|\).

The method: connectedness, the real target

Rosenblatt's hope was a perceptron that could see. Minsky and Papert pick the most basic visual predicate, "is the figure connected?", and show it is not of bounded order: as the retina grows, the order needed grows without bound (Ch. 5). For diameter-limited perceptrons, whose features each see a small patch, the argument is XOR again. Take four figures that differ only at their left end (two variants) and right end (two variants), arranged so that exactly the "mixed" pairs are connected. Each local feature sees one end or the middle, never both ends, so the weighted sum splits into a left part plus a right part, and the four sums are forced into the contradiction from the XOR proof.

Evidence: what Chapter 13 actually says

The evidence is a sequence of theorems, and the authors were explicit about scope. The passage everyone half-remembers is in Chapter 13, on multilayer machines. They write that the perceptron has many attractive features (its linearity, its learning theorem, its simplicity) and that there is no reason to suppose these "carry over to the many-layered version", and they call it an important research problem "to elucidate (or reject) our intuitive judgment that the extension is sterile." That is a conjecture, labelled as an intuition, and it turned out to be wrong in practice. The 1988 expanded edition added a prologue and an epilogue responding to the new connectionism of backpropagation. In it they argue that the scaling and representation problems they identified had not gone away just because hidden layers could now be trained.

Limits & what broke
  • The multilayer intuition. Backpropagation (Day 31) trains hidden units, and a two-layer net computes \(n\)-bit parity with about \(n\) hidden units. Bounded-order limits do not bind networks whose features are learned and global.
  • Did it kill funding? One side: the book gave intellectual cover for moving money to symbolic AI, and neural-network work in the US thinned out in the 1970s. The other side: US military research was being narrowed to mission-relevant work anyway (the Mansfield Amendment, 1969), Britain's Lighthill report (1973) hit all of AI, Rosenblatt died in 1971, and neural research continued (Amari, Fukushima, Grossberg, Kohonen, Werbos's 1974 thesis). Olazaran's 1996 sociological study traces how the "official history" was constructed.
  • Rosenblatt was already past single layers. His 1962 Principles of Neurodynamics analyses multi-layer and cross-coupled perceptrons, and proposes heuristic procedures for training them. What nobody had was a reliable credit-assignment rule for hidden units.
  • The lesson that survived. Global properties are hard for architectures built from local, bounded pieces. Parity remains a standard stress test for transformers today.

Bridge

Perceptrons are judged here by what they can represent. In the same year as Rosenblatt's machine, Widrow and Hoff chose a different learning rule, one that minimises squared error on the unit's analogue output and therefore behaves like gradient descent. That small change is Day 6.

L6 · DAY 6

Adaptive Switching Circuits (ADALINE / LMS)

30m videoLevel 1

Widrow & Hoff, 1960

Widrow and Hoff train a threshold unit by measuring its error before the threshold, on the analogue weighted sum. That turns learning into stochastic gradient descent on squared error: the least-mean-squares (LMS) rule, which converges on data no hyperplane separates and still runs in modems and echo cancellers.

Cold open

Stanford, 1959–60: Bernard Widrow and his graduate student Ted Hoff (who a decade later would help design Intel's first microprocessor) derive an adaptation rule and build it as a small analogue circuit. The perceptron and the ADALINE look almost identical on paper: weights, a sum, a threshold. They differ in one wire, the place the error signal is taken from, and that wire decides whether the learning rule is a gradient method.

The problem

Signal processing already had an optimal linear filter: Wiener's, \(w^* = R^{-1}p\), where \(R = \E[xx^\top]\) is the input correlation matrix and \(p = \E[d\,x]\) the cross-correlation with the desired response \(d\). It needs \(R\) and \(p\) in advance and a matrix inverse. Real channels are unknown and drift. The perceptron rule (Day 4) adapts online but needs separable classes and never settles on noisy data. The goal was a cheap, local, online rule that tracks the Wiener solution.

The method: ADALINE and the error that matters

Derivation · LMS is SGD on squared error

Let \(e = d - w^\top x\) be the error of the linear output, and \(J(w) = \E[e^2] = \E[d^2] - 2w^\top p + w^\top R w\), a convex quadratic bowl with gradient \(\nabla J = -2(p - Rw) = -2\E[e\,x]\). Setting it to zero gives the Wiener solution \(w^* = R^{-1} p\). LMS replaces the expectation by the current sample, \(\widehat{\nabla J} = -2\,e_k x_k\), and steps downhill:

$$w_{k+1} = w_k + 2\mu\, e_k\, x_k, \qquad e_k = d_k - w_k^\top x_k.$$

One multiply–add per weight per sample, no matrix, no inverse.

This is stochastic gradient descent, decades before the name became standard, and nine years after Robbins and Monro's stochastic approximation paper (Day 34) supplied the theory of noisy steps. The paper states the procedure as adjusting all weights to reduce the error on each presented pattern; later expositions (Widrow and Lehr's 1990 survey) distinguish this original "α-LMS" normalised form, \(w \leftarrow w + \alpha\, e\, x / \|x\|^2\), from the "μ-LMS" form above.

The method: why it converges, and the step-size limit

Result · convergence in the mean

Assume the inputs are independent of the current weights (the standard "independence assumption"). Then \(v_k = w_k - w^*\) satisfies \(\E[v_{k+1}] = (I - 2\mu R)\,\E[v_k]\), so the mean weights converge to \(w^*\) if and only if \(0 < \mu < 1/\lambda_{\max}(R)\). Because \(\lambda_{\max} \le \tr R = \sum_i \E[x_i^2]\), the total input power, the practical rule is \(0 < \mu < 1/\tr R\).

Each eigen-direction of \(R\) decays like \((1 - 2\mu\lambda_i)^k\), so the slowest mode has time constant about \(1/(2\mu\lambda_{\min})\): convergence speed is governed by the eigenvalue spread \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\). With constant \(\mu\) the weights do not stop at \(w^*\); they jitter around it, and the excess squared error (the misadjustment) is roughly \(\mu \tr R\) for small \(\mu\). Small steps buy precision with speed. That trade-off, the conditioning of the input covariance and the noise floor of a constant step, reappears in every optimiser paper in Month 2.

The contrast with the perceptron is now clear. On non-separable data the perceptron's error is large and discrete on some examples forever, so its weights keep jumping. The LMS objective has a unique minimum whatever the data, so the weights settle into a small neighbourhood of it.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
n = 500                                            # two overlapping classes: not separable
d = np.where(rng.random(n) < 0.5, 1.0, -1.0)
X = np.c_[rng.normal(0.8 * d[:, None], 1.0, size=(n, 2)), np.ones(n)]

R, p = X.T @ X / n, X.T @ d / n
w_wiener = np.linalg.solve(R, p)                   # minimiser of E[(d - w.x)^2]

w_lms, w_per, mu = np.zeros(3), np.zeros(3), 0.01
for epoch in range(50):
    w_old = w_per.copy()
    for x, t in zip(X, d):
        w_lms += mu * (t - w_lms @ x) * x          # error before the threshold
        y_hat = 1.0 if w_per @ x > 0 else -1.0
        w_per += 0.5 * (t - y_hat) * x             # error after the threshold
print("LMS distance to Wiener solution:", np.linalg.norm(w_lms - w_wiener).round(3))
print("perceptron movement in last epoch:", np.linalg.norm(w_per - w_old).round(3))

After 50 epochs LMS sits about 0.01 from the Wiener weights; the perceptron's weights still move by more than 1 per epoch.

Evidence

The paper demonstrates the hardware unit learning to classify binary patterns and analyses how the error falls; the convincing evidence came from deployment. LMS became the workhorse of adaptive signal processing: channel equalisers in data modems, adaptive antennas, noise cancelling, and echo cancellers on long-distance telephone lines. Popular histories often call the echo-cancelling filter the first real-world application of a neural network. Strictly it is LMS on a single linear unit, which is exactly the point: the algorithm needed no layers to be useful. MADALINE ("many ADALINEs") put several ADALINEs in front of a fixed logic unit such as a majority vote, trained with a "minimal disturbance" heuristic that adapts the unit whose sum is closest to zero.

Limits & what broke
  • Squared error is not classification error. Points far on the correct side still produce large errors and drag the boundary, so LMS can misclassify data that the perceptron would separate perfectly.
  • Ill-conditioning. Correlated inputs give a large eigenvalue spread and very slow convergence. The fix is to whiten the input or precondition the step.
  • No rule for hidden units. MADALINE heuristics did not generalise to deep networks; Widrow's group revisited them in the late 1980s, after backpropagation.
  • What superseded it, where it was superseded: recursive least squares (RLS) replaces \(\mu I\) with a running estimate of \(R^{-1}\), converging in a few times \(d\) steps whatever the conditioning, at \(O(d^2)\) cost per step. RLS is a Kalman filter whose state is a constant weight vector (Day 26).

Bridge

Days 3–6 all fit a weight vector. Day 7 fits nothing: store every example and answer by looking up the closest one. Cover and Hart prove that this lazy rule is, asymptotically, never worse than twice the best possible error.

L7 · DAY 7

Nearest Neighbor Pattern Classification

35m videoLevel 1

Cover & Hart, IEEE IT 1967

With unlimited data, classifying a point by the label of its single nearest neighbour has error at most twice the best achievable (Bayes) error, for any distribution with continuous class probabilities. In the authors' words, half the classification information in an infinite sample is contained in the nearest neighbour.

Cold open

Store the training set. To classify a new point, find the closest stored point and copy its label. No training, no parameters, no model. Fix and Hodges had proposed nearest-neighbour discrimination in a 1951 US Air Force report; what nobody had was a guarantee. Cover and Hart's answer is startlingly strong, and the conditions under which it holds explain both why k-NN is still the honest baseline for every embedding paper in Month 10 and why it so often disappoints.

The problem

Classical discriminant analysis (Fisher, Day 22) assumes a parametric form, such as Gaussian classes, and is only as good as that assumption. A nonparametric rule makes no such assumption, but then what can be said about its error? The benchmark is the Bayes risk \(R^*\): the error of the rule that knows the true posteriors \(\eta_c(x) = \Pr(Y = c \mid X = x)\) and predicts \(\arg\max_c \eta_c(x)\). No rule can beat it.

The method: Voronoi cells and the 1-NN rule

Definition · 1-NN rule and Voronoi cells

Given training pairs \((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)\) and a metric \(d\), the 1-NN rule predicts \(y_{(1)}\), the label of \(x_{(1)} = \arg\min_i d(x, x_i)\). The Voronoi cell of \(x_i\) is the set of points closer to \(x_i\) than to any other training point. The rule is constant on each cell, so its decision boundary is the union of cell faces separating differently labelled points: piecewise linear in Euclidean space, and as complicated as the data.

The \(k\)-NN rule takes a majority vote among the \(k\) nearest points. It is also a density estimator: if the ball around \(x\) reaching the \(k\)-th neighbour has volume \(V_k(x)\), then \(\hat p(x) = k / (n\, V_k(x))\), and the fraction of those \(k\) neighbours in class \(c\) estimates \(\eta_c(x)\). The vote is a plug-in Bayes rule with this estimate. If \(k \to \infty\) and \(k/n \to 0\), \(k\)-NN converges to the Bayes risk for every distribution (Stone, 1977). Cover and Hart ask what happens when \(k = 1\) is held fixed.

The method: the factor of two, proved

Theorem · Cover & Hart

For \(M\) classes, if the posteriors are continuous at almost every \(x\), the 1-NN risk \(R\) satisfies, as \(n \to \infty\),

$$R^* \;\le\; R \;\le\; R^*\Big(2 - \frac{M}{M-1}\,R^*\Big) \;\le\; 2R^*.$$
Proof · two classes

Step 1, the neighbour converges. For \(x\) in the support of the distribution, the nearest of \(n\) i.i.d. samples converges to \(x\) as \(n \to \infty\). By continuity, \(\eta(x_{(1)}) \to \eta(x)\), where \(\eta = \eta_1\).

Step 2, conditional error. Given \(x\), the test label and the neighbour's label are independent draws, with probabilities \(\eta(x)\) and \(\eta(x_{(1)}) \approx \eta(x)\). They disagree with probability \(\eta(1 - \eta) + (1 - \eta)\eta = 2\eta(1-\eta)\).

Step 3, compare with Bayes. The conditional Bayes risk is \(r^*(x) = \min(\eta, 1 - \eta)\), and \(\eta(1 - \eta) = r^*(1 - r^*)\) whichever is smaller. So the conditional 1-NN risk is \(2r^*(x)\big(1 - r^*(x)\big)\).

Step 4, average and use Jensen. \(R = \E\big[2r^* - 2(r^*)^2\big] = 2R^* - 2\,\E[(r^*)^2] \le 2R^* - 2(R^*)^2\), since \(\E[(r^*)^2] \ge (\E r^*)^2\). That is \(R \le 2R^*(1 - R^*)\), the \(M = 2\) case.

\(M\) classes. The conditional error is \(1 - \sum_c \eta_c^2\). With the largest posterior fixed at \(1 - r^*\), \(\sum_c \eta_c^2\) is smallest when the remaining mass \(r^*\) is spread evenly over \(M - 1\) classes, giving \(1 - \sum_c \eta_c^2 \le 2r^* - \frac{M}{M-1}(r^*)^2\). Average and apply Jensen again.

Evidence: what the asymptotics hide

The paper's evidence is the theorem; the authors note that the bound cannot be improved in general. Every word of the hypothesis is load-bearing. "As \(n \to \infty\)" hides the rate, and later work showed there is no distribution-free rate at all: for any rule and any sample size, some distribution makes convergence arbitrarily slow. Step 1 is where dimension enters. In \(d\) dimensions, getting the neighbour within distance \(\varepsilon\) of \(x\) needs on the order of \(\varepsilon^{-d}\) points, and in high dimensions distances concentrate:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    X = rng.random((2000, d))                      # uniform points in the unit cube
    q = rng.random(d)                              # a query point
    dist = np.linalg.norm(X - q, axis=1)
    print(f"d={d:5d}  nearest={dist.min():6.2f}  farthest={dist.max():6.2f}  "
          f"(far - near)/near = {(dist.max() - dist.min()) / dist.min():.2f}")

With 2,000 uniform points the relative gap between the nearest and farthest neighbour falls from hundreds at \(d = 2\) to about 3 at \(d = 10\), 0.5 at \(d = 100\) and 0.1 at \(d = 1000\). When everything is about equally far away, "nearest" carries little information.

Real data rescue k-NN when they lie near a low-dimensional manifold and the metric respects it. That is why metric choice matters more than \(k\): the theorem holds for any reasonable metric in the limit, but at finite \(n\) the metric decides which point is "nearest". Unscaled features, irrelevant dimensions and raw pixels all destroy neighbourhoods; a good embedding creates them. Changing \(k\) only trades variance for smoothing within whatever neighbourhoods the metric gives you.

Limits & what broke
  • Asymptotic only. The factor of two says nothing about 1,000 examples in 100 dimensions; finite-sample k-NN can be much worse than \(2R^*\).
  • Cost at query time. Exact search is \(O(nd)\) per query. Tree indexes help in low dimension and degrade towards brute force in high dimension, which is why approximate nearest-neighbour search (Month 10) exists.
  • No feature learning. The rule inherits all its intelligence from the representation. Learned metrics (and, today, learned embeddings) are the fix.
  • 1-NN is not consistent. With overlapping classes it never reaches \(R^*\); you need \(k\) growing with \(n\).

Bridge

Nearest neighbours partition space into Voronoi cells chosen by the data. Day 8 partitions space into axis-aligned boxes chosen by an information criterion from Day 2, and produces a model you can read out loud.

L8 · DAY 8

Induction of Decision Trees (ID3)

35m videoLevel 1

Quinlan, Machine Learning 1986

ID3 grows a decision tree top-down by repeatedly splitting on the attribute that most reduces the entropy of the class label, which is Shannon's measure applied to classification. Quinlan's paper also names the method's two weaknesses, its bias towards many-valued attributes and its tendency to fit noise, and patches the first with the gain ratio.

Cold open

A model you can read aloud: "If the outlook is sunny and the humidity is high, don't play." Expert systems of the early 1980s were built by interviewing experts and typing in rules, which was slow and brittle. ID3 induces the rules from examples. The paper appeared in volume 1, issue 1 of the journal Machine Learning, and the trees it describes are still the base learner inside the strongest tabular models (Days 10–13).

The problem

Given examples described by discrete attributes and labelled with a class, find a small tree consistent with them. Finding the smallest consistent tree is NP-hard (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976), so ID3 is greedy: choose the best single test, split, and recurse. Everything rests on what "best" means.

The method: information gain

Definition · entropy and information gain

For a set \(S\) with class proportions \(p_c\), \(H(S) = -\sum_c p_c \log_2 p_c\), exactly Shannon's \(H\) from Day 2. Splitting on attribute \(A\) with values \(v\) partitions \(S\) into \(S_v\). The expected entropy after the split and the gain are

$$H(S \mid A) = \sum_v \frac{|S_v|}{|S|} H(S_v), \qquad \operatorname{Gain}(S, A) = H(S) - H(S \mid A).$$

Gain is the mutual information between the attribute and the class within \(S\): the expected number of bits of uncertainty about the label that the test removes.

Algorithm · ID3
  1. If every example in \(S\) has the same class, return a leaf with that class.
  2. If no attributes remain, return a leaf with the majority class.
  3. Otherwise choose \(A^* = \arg\max_A \operatorname{Gain}(S, A)\), make a node testing \(A^*\), and create one branch per value \(v\).
  4. Recurse on each \(S_v\) with \(A^*\) removed from the candidate list.
Worked example · the 14-row weather table

Quinlan's running example has 14 Saturday mornings, attributes outlook (sunny, overcast, rain), temperature (hot, mild, cool), humidity (high, normal) and windy (true, false), and classes P (9) and N (5). So \(H(S) = -\tfrac9{14}\log_2\tfrac9{14} - \tfrac5{14}\log_2\tfrac5{14} \approx 0.940\) bits.

Outlook splits the rows into sunny (2 P, 3 N), overcast (4 P, 0 N) and rain (3 P, 2 N). The two mixed branches each have entropy 0.971 and overcast has 0, so \(H(S \mid \text{outlook}) = \tfrac5{14}(0.971) + 0 + \tfrac5{14}(0.971) \approx 0.694\) and the gain is about 0.247 bits. The same arithmetic gives humidity 0.152, windy 0.048 and temperature 0.029. Outlook goes at the root; recursing gives the tree in the figure.

import math
from collections import Counter

rows = """sunny hot high false N|sunny hot high true N|overcast hot high false P|rain mild high false P
rain cool normal false P|rain cool normal true N|overcast cool normal true P|sunny mild high false N
sunny cool normal false P|rain mild normal false P|sunny mild normal true P|overcast mild high true P
overcast hot normal false P|rain mild high true N"""
data = [r.split() for r in rows.replace("\n", "|").split("|")]
names = ["outlook", "temperature", "humidity", "windy"]

def H(labels):
    n = len(labels)
    return -sum(c / n * math.log2(c / n) for c in Counter(labels).values())

y = [r[-1] for r in data]
print(f"H(class) = {H(y):.3f} bits")
for j, name in enumerate(names):
    groups = {}
    for r in data:
        groups.setdefault(r[j], []).append(r[-1])
    remainder = sum(len(g) / len(data) * H(g) for g in groups.values())
    gain = H(y) - remainder
    split_info = H([r[j] for r in data])          # entropy of the attribute itself
    print(f"{name:12s} gain = {gain:.3f}  split info = {split_info:.3f}  ratio = {gain / split_info:.3f}")

The method: the many-values bias and the gain ratio

Add an attribute "date" with a different value on every row. Each branch holds one example and is pure, so its gain is the full 0.940 bits: the best attribute by far, and useless for prediction. Gain rewards fragmenting the data. Quinlan's patch divides by the entropy of the split itself:

$$\operatorname{SplitInfo}(S, A) = -\sum_v \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}\log_2 \frac{|S_v|}{|S|}, \qquad \operatorname{GainRatio}(S, A) = \frac{\operatorname{Gain}(S, A)}{\operatorname{SplitInfo}(S, A)}.$$

For outlook, split info is 1.577 and the ratio 0.156. The patch is only partial: the "date" attribute has split info \(\log_2 14 \approx 3.81\) and ratio \(0.940/3.81 \approx 0.247\), which still beats outlook. Hence the safeguard used with it in Quinlan's work: consider only attributes whose plain gain is at least average, then maximise the ratio. Split info can also be near zero for a very unbalanced split, making the ratio blow up.

Evidence

The paper is a synthesis of a line of work begun in the late 1970s, and its evidence is a mix of worked examples and experience reports from applications such as chess endgames, where induced trees classified large numbers of positions. It also discusses noise: with noisy labels, growing until every leaf is pure makes the tree memorise the noise, and Quinlan uses a chi-square test to stop splitting when an attribute's association with the class is not significant. The paper is honest that these choices are heuristics.

Gini versus entropy: a near non-difference

CART's Gini impurity is \(G = 1 - \sum_c p_c^2 = \sum_c p_c(1 - p_c)\). Using \(-\ln p \approx 1 - p\) near \(p = 1\), entropy in nats is \(\sum_c p_c(-\ln p_c) \approx \sum_c p_c(1 - p_c) = G\): Gini is entropy's first-order approximation. For two classes the scaled curves differ by at most about 0.055 (figure), and a theoretical comparison by Raileanu and Stoffel (2004) reported that the two criteria disagree on the chosen split in only a small percentage of cases. Gini is cheaper (no logarithm). Misclassification rate, by contrast, is a poor split criterion: it is piecewise linear, so many useful splits leave it unchanged.

Limits & what broke
  • Unpruned trees are overfitting machines. A tree grown to purity has zero training error on any consistent data, including pure noise. Pre-pruning with significance tests stops too early on interactions (XOR-like attributes have zero gain individually); post-pruning, which grows fully and then cuts back, won out.
  • Greedy and myopic. One-step lookahead cannot see attributes that only help in combination.
  • High variance. A small change in the data can change the root split and hence the whole tree. That instability is a liability for a single tree and an asset for an ensemble, which is the next three days.

Bridge

Trees are unstable: perturb the data and you get a different tree. Day 9 turns that weakness into a method. Train many trees on bootstrap resamples and average them.

L9 · DAY 9

Bagging Predictors

30m videoLevel 1

Breiman, Machine Learning 1996

Train the same unstable learner on many bootstrap resamples of one dataset and average (or vote) the results. Averaging removes variance and leaves bias untouched, so bagging helps exactly the learners whose predictions jump around when the data change, such as trees, and does nothing for stable ones.

Cold open

One paper, one idea, and an acronym: bootstrap aggregating. Breiman bags classification and regression trees on a range of standard datasets and reports substantial drops in test error on most of them, for the price of training the same tree a few dozen times. No new model class, no new loss. The whole effect comes from averaging.

The problem

If you could draw many independent training sets \(L_1, L_2, \dots\) from the same distribution, you could train a predictor on each and average them: the aggregated predictor \(\varphi_A(x) = \E_L[\varphi(x, L)]\). You have one training set. Efron's bootstrap (1979) gives a way to imitate the others: resample your own data with replacement.

The method: bootstrap, aggregate

Algorithm · Bagging
  1. For \(b = 1, \dots, B\): draw a bootstrap sample \(L^{(b)}\) of size \(n\) from \(L\) uniformly with replacement, and fit \(\varphi_b = \varphi(\cdot, L^{(b)})\).
  2. Regression: predict \(\varphi_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac1B \sum_b \varphi_b(x)\).
  3. Classification: predict the class with the most votes among \(\varphi_1(x), \dots, \varphi_B(x)\).
Derivation · the 63.2% number

A given example is missed by one draw with probability \(1 - \tfrac1n\), so it is missed by all \(n\) draws with probability \((1 - \tfrac1n)^n\). Then

$$\ln\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big)^{n} = n \ln\Big(1 - \frac1n\Big) = n\Big(-\frac1n - \frac{1}{2n^2} - \cdots\Big) \;\longrightarrow\; -1,$$

so \(\Pr(\text{example appears in the bootstrap sample}) = 1 - (1 - \tfrac1n)^n \to 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632\). Each bag contains about 63.2% of the distinct examples (some repeated), and leaves about 36.8% out.

import numpy as np

for n in [5, 10, 100, 1000, 10**6]:
    print(f"n = {n:>7}: P(point in bootstrap sample) = {1 - (1 - 1 / n) ** n:.4f}")
print(f"limit 1 - 1/e = {1 - np.exp(-1):.4f}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, B = 1000, 200
in_bag = np.zeros((B, n), dtype=bool)
for b in range(B):
    in_bag[b, rng.integers(0, n, n)] = True       # sample n indices with replacement
print("mean fraction of distinct points per bag:", in_bag.mean().round(3))
print("mean number of bags each point is out of:", (~in_bag).sum(axis=0).mean().round(1))

The convergence is fast: 0.651 at \(n = 10\), 0.634 at \(n = 100\). With 200 bags, each point is out-of-bag for about \(200/e \approx 74\) of them.

The method: why averaging helps unstable learners only

Derivation · Breiman's argument (regression)

Fix \(x\) and the target \(y\). Averaging over training sets,

$$\E_L\big[(y - \varphi(x, L))^2\big] = y^2 - 2y\,\varphi_A(x) + \E_L\big[\varphi(x, L)^2\big] \;\ge\; (y - \varphi_A(x))^2,$$

because \(\E_L[\varphi^2] \ge (\E_L \varphi)^2\). The gap is exactly \(\Var_L[\varphi(x, L)]\). The aggregated predictor never does worse on average, and the improvement equals the variance of the base predictor across training sets.

If the learner is unstable (small changes in \(L\) cause large changes in \(\varphi\)), that variance is large and averaging helps a lot. Breiman names trees, subset selection in linear regression and neural nets as unstable, and nearest-neighbour methods as stable. For a stable learner \(\varphi_A \approx \varphi\) and there is nothing to gain. It can even be slightly worse, because the bagged predictor averages over the bootstrap distribution, and each bag sees only about 63% of the distinct data. Breiman reports exactly this: bagging nearest-neighbour classifiers did not help.

Classification is subtler. Majority voting turns a classifier into an approximation of "the class this procedure most often predicts at \(x\)". Breiman calls a procedure order-correct at \(x\) if that most-predicted class is the Bayes class. Voting makes an order-correct procedure nearly optimal there, but where it is not order-correct, voting locks in the wrong answer. So bagging can make a poor classifier worse.

The method: the bias–variance ledger

Suppose each bagged predictor at \(x\) has variance \(\sigma^2\) and any two have correlation \(\rho\) (they share most of their training data, so \(\rho > 0\)). Then

$$\Var\Big[\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^{B}\varphi_b\Big] = \frac{1}{B^2}\big[B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\big] = \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 .$$

The ledger has three entries. Variance: falls towards \(\rho\sigma^2\). Bias: the same as one predictor fit to a bootstrap sample, which is close to (and sometimes slightly worse than) a predictor fit to all the data. Number of bags: once the second term is small, more bags cost compute and buy nothing, but they never cause overfitting. Deep, unpruned trees are therefore the ideal base learner: low bias, high variance, and the variance is what bagging removes.

Evidence

The experiments compare a single CART tree with a bagged ensemble on several classification and regression datasets, with error estimated over repeated random train/test splits. Bagging reduced error on almost all of them, often by a large fraction, with the largest gains where trees are most unstable. The paper also tries bagging other procedures, which is where the k-NN null result comes from. What the experiments do not show is why some datasets gain more than others; the instability explanation is argued, not measured.

Out-of-bag estimation. Each example is left out of about 37% of the bags, so you can predict it using only those bags and compare with its label. Averaging this over the training set gives a test-error estimate with no held-out data. Breiman studied it in a companion 1996 technical report and found it close to a test-set estimate. Because each out-of-bag prediction uses only about a third of the ensemble, it is if anything slightly pessimistic for small \(B\).

Limits & what broke
  • The correlation floor. Bootstrap trees share most of their data and tend to pick the same strong splits near the root, so \(\rho\) stays high and the variance floor \(\rho\sigma^2\) does not go away with more bags. Random forests attack \(\rho\) directly.
  • No help with bias. Bagging stumps or linear models gives roughly the same stump or linear model. Reducing bias needs a sequential method: boosting (Day 11).
  • Interpretability is gone. An average of 100 trees cannot be read aloud.

Bridge

The variance of a bag of trees is stuck at \(\rho\sigma^2\). Breiman's next idea, five years later, is to inject extra randomness into each tree so they disagree more, lowering \(\rho\) at a small cost in individual accuracy. That is Day 10, random forests.

L10 · DAY 10

Random Forests

40m videoLevel 1

Breiman, Machine Learning 2001

A random forest is bagging plus one more source of randomness: each split in each tree may only choose among a random subset of the features. That decorrelates the trees, and Breiman proves the forest's error is controlled by exactly two numbers, the strength of the individual trees and the correlation between them.

Cold open

Twenty-five years on, a random forest with default settings is still one of the hardest baselines to beat on tabular data. Benchmark studies such as Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (2022) still find tree ensembles ahead of deep networks on typical medium-sized tables. It needs almost no tuning, cannot be made worse by adding trees, and gives a free validation estimate. This paper explains the last two properties with a theorem.

The problem

Bagged trees (Day 9) hit a variance floor \(\rho\sigma^2\): bootstrap samples overlap heavily, every tree picks the same dominant feature at the root, and the trees end up correlated. Several groups had tried injecting more randomness: Ho's random subspace method (each tree uses a random subset of features), Amit and Geman's randomised feature selection for shape recognition, and Dietterich's random choice among the best splits. Breiman's paper unifies these as one family and gives it a theory.

The method: two kinds of randomness

Definition · random forest (Breiman's §1)

A classifier made of trees \(\{h(x, \Theta_k)\}_{k=1}^K\), where the \(\Theta_k\) are i.i.d. random vectors (here: which rows are bootstrapped, which features are offered at each node), and each tree casts one vote for the most popular class at \(x\). The margin of the forest at \((x, y)\) is

$$mg(x, y) = \operatorname{av}_k\, I(h_k(x) = y) - \max_{j \ne y} \operatorname{av}_k\, I(h_k(x) = j),$$

the excess of votes for the right class over the strongest wrong class; the forest errs where \(mg < 0\).

The method: strength, correlation, and the bound

By the strong law of large numbers, as \(K \to \infty\) the vote fractions converge to their expectations over \(\Theta\), so the forest's error converges almost surely to that of the limiting margin \(mr(x, y) = P_\Theta(h(x,\Theta) = y) - \max_{j\ne y} P_\Theta(h(x,\Theta) = j)\) (Breiman's Theorem 1.2). Adding trees therefore cannot cause overfitting: it only makes the forest closer to its limit. Define the strength \(s = \E_{X,Y}[mr(X, Y)]\), assumed positive.

Derivation · \(PE^* \le \bar\rho\,(1 - s^2)/s^2\) (Theorem 2.3)

Let \(\hat\jmath(x, y)\) be the strongest wrong class and \(\operatorname{rmg}(\Theta, x, y) = I(h(x,\Theta) = y) - I(h(x,\Theta) = \hat\jmath)\), the raw margin of one tree, so \(mr = \E_\Theta[\operatorname{rmg}]\).

Chebyshev. The forest errs when \(mr < 0\), which requires \(|mr - s| \ge s\). So \(PE^* \le \Var(mr)/s^2\).

Variance as correlation. Since \(mr^2 = \E_{\Theta,\Theta'}[\operatorname{rmg}(\Theta)\operatorname{rmg}(\Theta')]\) for independent \(\Theta, \Theta'\),

$$\Var(mr) = \E_{\Theta,\Theta'}\big[\rho(\Theta,\Theta')\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta)\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta')\big] = \bar\rho\,\big(\E_\Theta\,\mathrm{sd}(\Theta)\big)^2 \le \bar\rho\, \E_\Theta \Var(\Theta),$$

where \(\rho\) is the correlation over \((X, Y)\) of two trees' raw margins and \(\bar\rho\) its weighted mean.

Bound the per-tree variance. \(\E_\Theta \Var(\Theta) \le \E_\Theta\big[\E_{X,Y}\operatorname{rmg}^2\big] - s^2 \le 1 - s^2\), since \(|\operatorname{rmg}| \le 1\) and \(\E_\Theta[\E_{X,Y}\operatorname{rmg}] = s\). Combining the three lines gives the result.

The bound is loose, and Breiman says so, but its shape is the whole theory: the forest wants strong trees (large \(s\)) that are weakly correlated (small \(\bar\rho\)). Fewer candidate features per split lowers \(\bar\rho\) and also lowers \(s\). The ratio \(\bar\rho / s^2\) is what to minimise, and both quantities can be estimated out-of-bag, which the paper does.

The method: choosing mtry

The paper's own experiments use just two settings, \(F = 1\) and \(F = \lfloor \log_2 M + 1 \rfloor\) features per split (\(M\) inputs), and find accuracy surprisingly insensitive to the choice, with even \(F = 1\) competitive. The defaults everyone uses, \(\text{mtry} = \sqrt{p}\) for classification and \(p/3\) for regression, come from Breiman and Cutler's software and the R randomForest package. They are rules of thumb for the strength/correlation trade-off: regression trees lose accuracy faster when offered few features, so they get more. Tune mtry by out-of-bag error when it matters; it is the one parameter that does.

Evidence

Breiman compares forests with AdaBoost (Day 11), then the strongest off-the-shelf classifier, on a suite of benchmark datasets, and reports error rates that are comparable and often slightly better. The more striking experiment adds label noise: with a few percent of training labels flipped, AdaBoost degrades markedly while forests barely move, because they never concentrate weight on hard (here, mislabelled) points. He also shows out-of-bag estimates of strength and correlation tracking the test error as \(F\) varies. What the evidence does not show is that the bound predicts the error quantitatively; it is a guide to direction only.

Variable importance. The paper measures a variable's importance by permuting its values in the out-of-bag data and recording the increase in error (permutation importance). Implementations also report Gini importance, the total impurity decrease from splits on the variable. Gini importance is strongly biased towards continuous and high-cardinality features, which offer more candidate split points and so win splits by chance (Strobl et al. 2007). Permutation importance is much less affected but is not immune: with bootstrap sampling and CART splitting it inherits some of that bias, and with correlated features it spreads or inflates importance (conditional permutation importance is the fix). Proximities, the fraction of trees in which two cases land in the same leaf, give a supervised similarity matrix for imputation, outlier detection and visualisation; few people use them now.

Limits & what broke
  • "Doesn't overfit" means "more trees doesn't overfit". Each tree is fully grown, so a forest can still fit noise; leaf size and depth still matter on noisy regression problems.
  • Theory lagged practice. The bound is for an idealised forest. Consistency of Breiman's actual algorithm was proved only in special settings, much later (Scornet, Biau and Vert, 2015).
  • No extrapolation. Predictions are averages of training targets, so a forest is flat outside the training range.
  • Superseded on accuracy, not on convenience. Tuned gradient-boosted trees (Day 12, Day 13) usually win on accuracy; forests win on effort.

Bridge

Forests reduce variance by averaging many independent, deep trees. Day 11 does the opposite: shallow trees trained in sequence, each one focused on the examples its predecessors got wrong. That reduces bias, and comes with a training-error proof.

L11 · DAY 11

A Decision-Theoretic Generalization of On-Line Learning (AdaBoost)

45m videoLevel 2

Freund & Schapire, JCSS 1997

AdaBoost turns any learner that is slightly better than chance on every weighting of the data into an arbitrarily accurate one, by repeatedly re-weighting the training examples towards the ones it gets wrong. Its training error falls exponentially in the number of rounds, and the proof is ten lines long.

Cold open

In 1988 Kearns and Valiant asked a question from learning theory (Day 16): if you can efficiently learn a concept a little better than guessing, can you learn it to any accuracy? Schapire proved yes in 1990, and Freund improved the construction, but both schemes needed to know in advance how much better than chance the weak learner was. This paper's "adaptive boosting" needs no such knowledge. Breiman later called boosted trees the "best off-the-shelf classifier in the world".

The problem

The paper has two halves. The first studies on-line allocation: spread a budget over \(N\) strategies each round, suffer their losses, and do almost as well as the best strategy in hindsight. Its algorithm, Hedge(\(\beta\)), multiplies each strategy's weight by \(\beta^{\text{loss}}\) and has total loss at most \(\big(\ln(1/\beta)\min_i L_i + \ln N\big)/(1 - \beta)\). The second half reverses the roles: the training examples become the strategies, and a weight is kept on each one. That reversal is AdaBoost. The weights are on examples, not features.

The method: the weak-learning assumption and the algorithm

Definition · weak learner

A procedure that, given any distribution \(D\) over the \(m\) training examples, returns a hypothesis \(h: \mathcal X \to \{-1, +1\}\) with weighted error \(\varepsilon = \sum_i D(i)\,[h(x_i) \ne y_i] \le \tfrac12 - \gamma\) for some fixed \(\gamma > 0\). "Better than chance on every reweighting" is a strong requirement; a decision stump usually meets it in practice, until the weighting becomes adversarial.

Algorithm · AdaBoost (binary, modern form)
  1. Start with \(D_1(i) = 1/m\).
  2. For \(t = 1, \dots, T\): train \(h_t\) on \(D_t\); compute \(\varepsilon_t\); set \(\alpha_t = \tfrac12 \ln\frac{1 - \varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\).
  3. Reweight \(D_{t+1}(i) = D_t(i)\, e^{-\alpha_t y_i h_t(x_i)} / Z_t\), with \(Z_t\) the normaliser: mistakes are multiplied by \(e^{\alpha_t}\), correct answers by \(e^{-\alpha_t}\).
  4. Output \(H(x) = \operatorname{sign}\big(F(x)\big)\), \(F(x) = \sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x)\).

The paper's AdaBoost.M1 writes the same thing with \(\beta_t = \varepsilon_t/(1 - \varepsilon_t)\): correct examples are multiplied by \(\beta_t\), and \(h_t\) votes with weight \(\ln(1/\beta_t) = 2\alpha_t\). The factor 2 does not change the sign of the vote.

The method: the training-error bound, proved

Proof · \(\text{train error} \le \prod_t 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1 - \varepsilon_t)}\)

Unroll the weights. Multiplying the update over all rounds, \(D_{T+1}(i) = \dfrac{e^{-y_i F(x_i)}}{m \prod_t Z_t}\). Since the \(D_{T+1}(i)\) sum to 1, \(\frac1m \sum_i e^{-y_i F(x_i)} = \prod_t Z_t\).

Bound the 0–1 loss. If \(H(x_i) \ne y_i\) then \(y_i F(x_i) \le 0\), so \(e^{-y_i F(x_i)} \ge 1\). Hence \(\frac1m \sum_i [H(x_i) \ne y_i] \le \frac1m \sum_i e^{-y_i F(x_i)} = \prod_t Z_t\).

Minimise each \(Z_t\) greedily. \(Z_t(\alpha) = (1 - \varepsilon_t)e^{-\alpha} + \varepsilon_t e^{\alpha}\). Setting \(Z_t'(\alpha) = 0\) gives \(e^{2\alpha} = (1 - \varepsilon_t)/\varepsilon_t\), that is \(\alpha_t = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}\), and then

$$Z_t = (1-\varepsilon_t)\sqrt{\tfrac{\varepsilon_t}{1-\varepsilon_t}} + \varepsilon_t\sqrt{\tfrac{1-\varepsilon_t}{\varepsilon_t}} = 2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t(1-\varepsilon_t)} = \sqrt{1 - 4\gamma_t^2} \le e^{-2\gamma_t^2},$$

with \(\varepsilon_t = \tfrac12 - \gamma_t\) and \(1 + x \le e^x\). So the training error is at most \(\exp\big(-2\sum_t \gamma_t^2\big)\): with a constant edge \(\gamma\), it reaches zero after \(O(\ln m / \gamma^2)\) rounds. \(\blacksquare\)

Two facts fall out. After reweighting, \(h_t\) has error exactly \(\tfrac12\) under \(D_{t+1}\), so the next round is forced to find something new. And the choice of \(\alpha_t\) is not arbitrary: it is the step that most decreases \(\sum_i e^{-y_i F(x_i)}\).

import numpy as np

def best_stump(X, y, D):
    """Weighted-error-minimising stump h(x) = s * sign(x[j] - t)."""
    best = (np.inf, None)
    for j in range(X.shape[1]):
        for t in np.unique(X[:, j]):
            for s in (1, -1):
                pred = s * np.where(X[:, j] > t, 1, -1)
                err = D[pred != y].sum()
                if err < best[0]:
                    best = (err, (j, t, s))
    return best

The modern reading: exponential loss

Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2000) showed that AdaBoost is forward stagewise additive modelling: at each round it adds the \(\alpha h\) that most reduces the empirical exponential loss \(\sum_i e^{-y_i F(x_i)}\), exactly the quantity minimised in the proof. Its population minimiser is \(F^*(x) = \tfrac12\ln\frac{P(y = 1 \mid x)}{P(y = -1 \mid x)}\), half the log-odds, so \(1/(1 + e^{-2F})\) recovers a probability. This reading turns boosting into an optimisation method that can use other losses, which is Day 12.

Evidence: why test error keeps falling

The JCSS paper is theory; the experiments were in Freund and Schapire's companion 1996 ICML paper, which boosted stumps and C4.5 trees on benchmark datasets with large gains. Practitioners then saw something the theory did not explain: test error kept decreasing after training error reached zero. A VC-style bound (Day 17) charges for the complexity of the combined classifier, which grows with \(T\), and so predicts eventual overfitting. Schapire, Freund, Bartlett and Lee (1998) explained it with margins: after training error is zero, AdaBoost keeps increasing the normalised margins \(y_i F(x_i)/\sum_t \alpha_t\), and they proved a test-error bound in terms of the margin distribution that does not grow with \(T\). The argument was contested at once. Breiman's arc-gv maximised the minimum margin more aggressively than AdaBoost yet tested worse, and Reyzin and Schapire (2006) replied that arc-gv's trees were more complex, so the comparison was not like for like.

Limits & what broke
  • Label noise. A mislabelled example can never be classified "correctly", so its weight grows exponentially and later rounds chase it. Dietterich's 2000 comparison found boosting degraded more than bagging under label noise, and Long and Servedio proved that random classification noise defeats every convex-potential booster.
  • The weak learner must be just right. Too weak (\(\varepsilon_t \to \tfrac12\)) and progress stalls; too strong (deep trees) and it fits the training set in one round, leaving nothing to boost.
  • Multi-class. AdaBoost.M1 needs \(\varepsilon_t < \tfrac12\) on \(K\) classes, a hard requirement for weak learners; later variants relax it.

Bridge

AdaBoost minimises one loss with one kind of weak learner. Friedman's gradient boosting machine (Day 12) treats boosting as gradient descent in function space, so any differentiable loss works, and recovers AdaBoost as a special case.

L12 · DAY 12

Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine

50m videoLevel 2

Friedman, Annals of Statistics 2001

Friedman recasts boosting as steepest descent in function space: at each step, fit a small regression tree to the negative gradient of the loss at the current predictions, then choose the step size by line search, separately in each leaf. Any differentiable loss now works, and AdaBoost becomes the special case of exponential loss.

Cold open

XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost, behind most winning tabular models of the last decade, all implement this paper's algorithm with engineering on top. The same paper introduced the partial dependence plot.

The problem

AdaBoost (Day 11) was a specific recipe for binary classification. Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2000) had shown it was stagewise minimisation of exponential loss, which raised the obvious question: why that loss? Regression needs squared error, robust regression needs absolute or Huber loss, and classification is better served by the logistic deviance. What was missing was a general procedure that takes a loss and a base learner and returns a boosting algorithm.

The method: gradient descent where the parameters are function values

We want \(F^* = \arg\min_F \sum_i L(y_i, F(x_i))\). Treat the \(n\) numbers \(F(x_1), \dots, F(x_n)\) as the parameters. The steepest-descent direction is the negative gradient, component by component:

$$r_{im} = -\left[\frac{\partial L(y_i, F(x_i))}{\partial F(x_i)}\right]_{F = F_{m-1}}, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n.$$

That vector is only defined at the training points. To get a direction that generalises to new \(x\), fit a base learner \(h(x; a)\) to the "pseudo-responses" \(r_{im}\) by least squares, the closest member of the base-learner family to the gradient. Then take a step of the best length.

Algorithm · Gradient_Boost and TreeBoost
  1. \(F_0(x) = \arg\min_\rho \sum_i L(y_i, \rho)\), the best constant.
  2. For \(m = 1, \dots, M\): compute the pseudo-responses \(r_{im}\).
  3. Fit a \(J\)-leaf regression tree to \(\{(x_i, r_{im})\}\) by least squares, giving leaf regions \(R_{1m}, \dots, R_{Jm}\).
  4. Line search per leaf: \(\gamma_{jm} = \arg\min_\gamma \sum_{x_i \in R_{jm}} L\big(y_i, F_{m-1}(x_i) + \gamma\big)\).
  5. Update \(F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu \sum_j \gamma_{jm}\, [x \in R_{jm}]\), with shrinkage \(0 < \nu \le 1\).

Step 4 is the key refinement over a generic gradient method. The tree is used only to choose the partition; the value in each leaf is then optimised for the true loss, not for the squared-error fit to the gradient. For squared error the two coincide; for other losses they do not.

The LAD row shows why the leaf line search matters: the gradient is just a sign, so the tree learns where residuals are positive or negative, and the median then supplies a robust magnitude. The deviance row already uses second-order information in each leaf, which is the step XGBoost generalises tomorrow.

import numpy as np

def fit_stump(x, r):
    """Least-squares stump on 1-D x: returns (threshold, left value, right value)."""
    order = np.argsort(x)
    xs, rs = x[order], r[order]
    csum, n = np.cumsum(rs), len(rs)
    k = np.arange(1, n)
    sse = -(csum[:-1] ** 2) / k - (csum[-1] - csum[:-1]) ** 2 / (n - k)
    i = np.argmin(sse)
    return (xs[i] + xs[i + 1]) / 2, rs[: i + 1].mean(), rs[i + 1:].mean()

def gradient_boost(x, y, M=200, nu=0.1):
    F = np.full_like(y, y.mean())               # F_0: best constant
    stumps = []
    for m in range(M):
        r = y - F                                # negative gradient of (y - F)^2 / 2
        t, a, b = fit_stump(x, r)                # fit the base learner to it
        F += nu * np.where(x <= t, a, b)         # shrunken step (leaf means = line search)
        stumps.append((t, a, b))
    return y.mean(), stumps

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.random(200)
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, 0.3, 200)

grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 5)
pred = F0 + 0.1 * sum(np.where(grid <= t, a, b) for t, a, b in stumps)

The method: recovering AdaBoost

Derivation · exponential loss gives AdaBoost

Take \(L = e^{-yF}\) and base learners \(h \in \{-1, +1\}\). The stagewise problem is \(\min_{\beta, h} \sum_i e^{-y_i(F_{m-1}(x_i) + \beta h(x_i))} = \sum_i w_i\, e^{-\beta y_i h(x_i)}\), with \(w_i = e^{-y_i F_{m-1}(x_i)}\) fixed. Splitting into correct and incorrect examples,

$$\sum_i w_i e^{-\beta y_i h(x_i)} = e^{-\beta}\!\!\sum_{y_i = h(x_i)}\!\! w_i + e^{\beta}\!\!\sum_{y_i \ne h(x_i)}\!\! w_i = \Big(\sum_i w_i\Big)\big[(1 - \text{err})e^{-\beta} + \text{err}\, e^{\beta}\big].$$

For any \(\beta > 0\) the best \(h\) minimises the weighted error \(\text{err}\); then \(\beta = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1 - \text{err}}{\text{err}}\), and the next weights are \(w_i e^{-\beta y_i h(x_i)}\). That is AdaBoost's weak learner, its \(\alpha_t\), and its reweighting. The pseudo-response is \(y_i w_i\): AdaBoost's weights are the gradient.

Evidence: shrinkage, subsampling, depth

Friedman's experiments use a "random function generator" to create many regression and classification targets of varying complexity, and compare the losses and settings across them. The findings that lasted:

  • Shrinkage trades against the number of trees. Smaller \(\nu\) needs proportionally more iterations \(M\) but reaches a lower test error, and Friedman recommends small values (\(\nu \le 0.1\)) with \(M\) chosen by validation.
  • Robust losses pay off under heavy-tailed noise, and cost little when the noise is Gaussian.
  • Stochastic gradient boosting (Friedman, 2002) fits each tree on a random subsample drawn without replacement, typically around half the data, which improves both accuracy and speed.
  • Tree size is interaction order. A tree of depth \(d\) uses at most \(d\) variables along any path, so it can represent interactions of order at most \(d\); stumps give an additive model. Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman's textbook reports that \(4 \le J \le 8\) leaves usually works well.
Definition · partial dependence

For a subset \(S\) of variables with complement \(C\), the partial dependence of \(F\) on \(x_S\) is \(\bar F_S(x_S) = \frac1n\sum_i F(x_S, x_{iC})\): set \(x_S\) to a value in every training row, keep each row's other variables, and average. Plotted against \(x_S\) it shows the model's average response. The same section defines the relative influence of each variable, the summed squared-error improvement from splits on it.

Limits & what broke
  • Partial dependence lies when features are correlated. Setting \(x_S\) in every row creates impossible combinations (a 2-metre child), and averaging hides heterogeneous effects. ICE curves and accumulated local effects were later fixes.
  • Sequential by construction. Trees cannot be built in parallel; only the work inside each tree can be. On large data, split finding over sorted features dominates the cost.
  • First-order plus heuristics. Leaf values need a separate line search for each loss, and regularisation is by \(\nu\), \(M\), \(J\) and subsampling rather than in the objective.
  • Overfitting is real. Unlike a forest, more boosting iterations eventually overfit (the \(\nu = 1\) curve), so \(M\) must be chosen by early stopping.

Bridge

The deviance row used a Newton step inside each leaf. Day 13 makes second-order information the whole method: expand any loss to second order, put the regularisation into the objective, and derive the optimal leaf weight and the split gain in closed form. Then make it fast.

XGBoost: A Scalable Tree Boosting System

45m videoLevel 2

Chen & Guestrin, KDD 2016 (arXiv:1603.02754)

XGBoost puts regularisation into the boosting objective and expands the loss to second order, which yields a closed-form optimal value for every leaf and a closed-form gain for every split. The other half of the paper is systems work (sparsity-aware splits, a weighted quantile sketch, cache-aware column blocks, out-of-core training) that made gradient boosting fast on data far larger than memory.

Cold open

The authors count the winning solutions published on Kaggle's blog during 2015 and report that 17 of 29 used XGBoost, and that every team in the top ten of KDD Cup 2015 used it. Read it as two papers: a one-page derivation and a systems paper.

The problem

Friedman's gradient boosting (Day 12) needs a separate line search for each loss and controls overfitting only through shrinkage, tree size and early stopping. Existing implementations were largely single-machine and in-memory and handled sparse or missing features poorly, while industrial datasets (click logs, insurance claims) were sparse and huge.

The method: the regularised objective and its second-order expansion

Definition · objective

The model is \(\hat y_i = \sum_{k=1}^{K} f_k(x_i)\), a sum of regression trees. Each tree has \(T\) leaves with weights \(w \in \R^T\) and a structure \(q\) mapping inputs to leaves, \(f(x) = w_{q(x)}\). The objective is

$$\mathcal L = \sum_i l(y_i, \hat y_i) + \sum_k \Omega(f_k), \qquad \Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac12 \lambda \|w\|^2 .$$
Derivation · optimal leaf weight and split gain

At round \(t\), add \(f_t\) and expand the loss to second order around the current prediction, with \(g_i = \partial_{\hat y} l(y_i, \hat y_i^{(t-1)})\) and \(h_i = \partial^2_{\hat y} l(y_i, \hat y_i^{(t-1)})\). Dropping constants,

$$\tilde{\mathcal L}^{(t)} = \sum_i \big[g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac12 h_i f_t(x_i)^2\big] + \gamma T + \tfrac12\lambda\sum_{j=1}^{T} w_j^2 .$$

Group the examples by leaf, \(I_j = \{i : q(x_i) = j\}\), and write \(G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i\), \(H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i\):

$$\tilde{\mathcal L}^{(t)} = \sum_{j=1}^{T}\big[G_j w_j + \tfrac12 (H_j + \lambda) w_j^2\big] + \gamma T .$$

Each bracket is a one-dimensional quadratic in \(w_j\), minimised at

$$w_j^* = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}, \qquad \tilde{\mathcal L}^{(t)}(q) = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{T} \frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda} + \gamma T .$$

Splitting a leaf with sums \((G, H)\) into left and right children replaces one term by two and adds one leaf, so the reduction in objective is

$$\text{Gain} = \frac12\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma .$$

Why second order wins here. First-order boosting fits the direction and then needs a loss-specific line search for the magnitude. The Newton expansion supplies both: any twice-differentiable loss is handled by supplying \(g\) and \(h\), and the split criterion is consistent with the leaf values. The curvature also weights the examples. For logistic loss \(h_i = p_i(1 - p_i)\), so examples the model is already sure about barely move the leaf, which is the same Newton step Friedman used inside L2_TreeBoost leaves. Completing the square shows the expansion is a weighted least-squares problem with targets \(-g_i/h_i\) and weights \(h_i\), a fact the quantile sketch uses below. \(\lambda\) shrinks leaf weights towards zero; \(\gamma\) is the minimum gain a split must earn, so negative-gain branches are pruned. The paper keeps Friedman's shrinkage and borrows column subsampling from random forests.

The method: split finding with missing values

Algorithm · sparsity-aware split finding (the paper's Algorithm 3)
  1. For each feature, visit only the rows where it is present, in sorted order, accumulating \(G_L, H_L\).
  2. For each candidate threshold, evaluate the gain twice: once with all missing rows added to the right child, once with them added to the left.
  3. Keep the best (feature, threshold, default direction). At prediction time, a missing value follows the stored default.

Cost is linear in the number of non-missing entries, so one-hot and other sparse encodings become cheap.

import numpy as np

def score(G, H, lam):
    return G * G / (H + lam)

def best_split(x, g, h, lam=1.0, gamma=0.0):
    """Exact greedy split on one feature with missing values (NaN), XGBoost-style."""
    miss = np.isnan(x)
    G, H = g.sum(), h.sum()
    Gm, Hm = g[miss].sum(), h[miss].sum()        # statistics of the missing rows
    order = np.argsort(x[~miss])                 # only non-missing values are scanned
    xs, gs, hs = x[~miss][order], g[~miss][order], h[~miss][order]
    best = (0.0, None, None)
    GL = HL = 0.0
    for k in range(len(xs) - 1):
        GL, HL = GL + gs[k], HL + hs[k]
        if xs[k] == xs[k + 1]:
            continue
        for default, (gl, hl) in (("left", (GL + Gm, HL + Hm)), ("right", (GL, HL))):
            gain = 0.5 * (score(gl, hl, lam) + score(G - gl, H - hl, lam) - score(G, H, lam)) - gamma
            if gain > best[0]:
                best = (gain, (xs[k] + xs[k + 1]) / 2, default)
    return best

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=500)
y = (x + 0.5 * rng.normal(size=500) > 0).astype(float)
x[(rng.random(500) < 0.4) & (y == 1)] = np.nan   # missingness that carries signal
p = np.full(500, 0.5)                            # prediction before the first tree
g, h = p - y, p * (1 - p)                        # logistic loss: gradient and Hessian
gain, thr, default = best_split(x, g, h)
print(f"gain={gain:.2f} threshold={thr:.3f} missing go {default}")
left = x < thr                                   # NaN compares False, so it goes right
for name, m in (("left", left), ("right", ~left)):
    print(name, "leaf weight w* = -G/(H+lambda) =", round(-g[m].sum() / (h[m].sum() + 1.0), 3))

Missing values occur only among positives here, and the search learns to send them right, with the positives (leaf weights −1.78 and +1.54 in log-odds).

Approximate splits and the weighted quantile sketch. Exact greedy search needs every sorted value. The approximate algorithm proposes candidate thresholds at quantiles of each feature, either once per tree (global) or after every split (local). Because the objective is a least-squares problem weighted by \(h_i\), the quantiles should be of the \(h\)-weighted distribution: candidates are chosen so each bucket holds about the same total Hessian. The paper contributes a mergeable, prunable sketch with provable guarantees for weighted data, which existing quantile sketches did not handle.

The method: the systems half

  • Column blocks. Data are stored in compressed sparse column format, each column pre-sorted once and reused across all iterations. Split finding then parallelises over features, and the approximate algorithm becomes a linear scan.
  • Cache-aware access. Scanning a sorted column fetches the gradient statistics of rows in a non-contiguous order, causing cache misses. Each thread prefetches them into a contiguous buffer first; the block size is tuned so the buffers fit in cache.
  • Out-of-core. Blocks too large for memory live on disk, with block compression (decompressed on the fly by a separate thread) and block sharding across several disks to multiply read throughput.

Evidence

The experiments run on an insurance-claims dataset (very sparse), a physics event-classification dataset, a learning-to-rank benchmark and a terabyte-scale click log. The authors report that the sparsity-aware algorithm is more than an order of magnitude faster than a naive version on sparse data, that exact-greedy XGBoost is much faster than scikit-learn and faster than R's gbm at comparable accuracy, that cache-aware prefetching roughly doubles speed on large datasets, and that compression plus sharding let a single machine train out-of-core on data that otherwise needed a cluster. These are 2015-era baselines on 2015-era hardware: the ranking held up, the speed ratios are not durable facts.

Limits & what broke
  • Histograms beat sorted columns. LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) bins features into histograms, grows trees leaf-wise, and adds GOSS (keep the examples with large gradients, subsample the rest and up-weight them) and EFB (bundle mutually exclusive sparse features into one). XGBoost later added its own histogram method.
  • Categoricals and target leakage. CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) encodes categories with ordered target statistics, computed for each example only from examples before it in a random permutation, and uses ordered boosting to remove the resulting prediction shift.
  • Newton steps need positive curvature. With non-convex or nearly flat losses \(h_i\) can be tiny or negative and leaf weights explode; implementations clip, and min_child_weight (a minimum \(H\) per leaf) exists for this reason.
  • Many knobs. Depth, \(\eta\), \(\lambda\), \(\gamma\), subsampling and child weight interact; untuned XGBoost can lose to a default random forest.

Bridge

Three days of trees fitted greedily, with no guarantee of a global optimum. Day 14 returns to a single linear separator, picks the one with the largest margin, and gets a convex problem whose solution depends only on a few support vectors.

L14 · DAY 14

Support-Vector Networks

55m videoLevel 2

Cortes & Vapnik, Machine Learning 1995

Among all hyperplanes that separate the data, choose the one with the widest margin. That is a convex quadratic program whose dual depends on the data only through inner products and whose solution is determined by a few "support vectors". Cortes and Vapnik add slack variables so it works on data that are not separable, which turns the method into hinge-loss minimisation with a regulariser.

Cold open

Draw two clouds of points that a line can separate. Infinitely many lines do it; the perceptron (Day 4) returns whichever one it happens to reach first. Choose instead the line that stays as far as possible from the nearest point of either class. It has the most room for error: small perturbations of the data, or of new points, do not flip their side. The geometric idea fits in one sentence; the algebra below is the whole method.

The problem

Vapnik's statistical learning theory (Day 17) said generalisation depends on capacity, and that hyperplanes with a large margin have small effective capacity whatever the dimension. Boser, Guyon and Vapnik (Day 15) had built the optimal-margin classifier with kernels in 1992, but it required perfectly separable data, which real data with label noise rarely are. This paper adds the soft margin and tests the result on handwritten digits.

The method: the margin and the primal

Derivation · the margin is \(2/\|w\|\)

The hyperplane \(w \cdot x + b = 0\) has unit normal \(w/\|w\|\). The signed distance of a point \(x\) to it is \((w \cdot x + b)/\|w\|\): write \(x = x_\perp + t\,w/\|w\|\) with \(x_\perp\) on the plane, so \(w \cdot x + b = t\|w\|\). The pair \((w, b)\) can be rescaled freely without moving the plane, so fix the scale by requiring \(\min_i y_i(w \cdot x_i + b) = 1\) (the canonical hyperplane). The closest points then lie at distance \(1/\|w\|\) on each side, and the margin is \(2/\|w\|\). Maximising it is minimising \(\tfrac12\|w\|^2\):

$$\min_{w, b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(w \cdot x_i + b) \ge 1, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$$

The method: the dual, derived

Derivation · Lagrangian dual and KKT conditions

Introduce multipliers \(\alpha_i \ge 0\), one per constraint:

$$\mathcal L(w, b, \alpha) = \tfrac12\|w\|^2 - \sum_i \alpha_i\big[y_i(w \cdot x_i + b) - 1\big].$$

Stationarity. \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial w = 0 \Rightarrow w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\), and \(\partial \mathcal L/\partial b = 0 \Rightarrow \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\).

Substitute. \(\|w\|^2 = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j\, x_i \cdot x_j\), the term \(-\sum_i \alpha_i y_i\, w \cdot x_i\) equals \(-\|w\|^2\), and the \(b\) term vanishes because \(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\). What remains is the dual:

$$\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\, y_i y_j\, \langle x_i, x_j\rangle \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \alpha_i \ge 0,\ \ \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0.$$

KKT. The problem is convex with linear constraints, so strong duality holds and the optimum satisfies the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions: primal and dual feasibility, stationarity, and complementary slackness, \(\alpha_i\big[y_i(w \cdot x_i + b) - 1\big] = 0\). Either a constraint is tight, so \(x_i\) sits exactly on the margin, or \(\alpha_i = 0\). Only points on the margin get \(\alpha_i > 0\); these are the support vectors, and \(b = y_k - w \cdot x_k\) for any of them.

Complementary slackness is the source of sparsity: \(w\) is a combination of the support vectors only. Remove every other training point and the solution is unchanged. The dual also touches the data only through \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\), which is tomorrow's whole paper.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

X = np.array([[3, 4], [5, 2], [5, 4], [6, 3], [4, 5.5], [6.5, 5], [7, 1.5],      # y = +1
              [2, 3], [4, 1], [1, 1.5], [2, 0.8], [0.5, 3], [1.5, 2], [3, 0.5]])  # y = -1
y = np.array([1] * 7 + [-1] * 7, dtype=float)
K = (y[:, None] * X) @ (y[:, None] * X).T          # K_ij = y_i y_j <x_i, x_j>

dual = lambda a: 0.5 * a @ K @ a - a.sum()          # minimise the negated dual
res = minimize(dual, np.zeros(len(y)), method="SLSQP",
               bounds=[(0, None)] * len(y),         # hard margin: alpha_i >= 0
               constraints={"type": "eq", "fun": lambda a: a @ y})
alpha = res.x
w = (alpha * y) @ X                                 # stationarity: w = sum alpha_i y_i x_i
sv = alpha > 1e-6
b = np.mean(y[sv] - X[sv] @ w)                      # complementary slackness on SVs
print("support vectors:", X[sv].tolist())
print("w =", w.round(3), " b =", round(b, 3), " margin 2/|w| =", round(2 / np.linalg.norm(w), 3))

Output: four support vectors, \(w = (1, 1)\), \(b = -6\), margin \(\sqrt2 \approx 1.414\). A general-purpose solver is fine for 14 points; real SVM solvers exploit the structure.

The method: the soft margin, and the loss it was all along

With overlapping classes no \(w\) satisfies every constraint. Allow violations \(\xi_i \ge 0\) and pay for them:

$$\min_{w, b, \xi}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.}\quad y_i(w \cdot x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0.$$

The paper writes the penalty in a more general form; this linear one is the version that became standard. Repeating the Lagrangian derivation with a second set of multipliers for \(\xi_i \ge 0\) gives the same dual with a box constraint, \(0 \le \alpha_i \le C\). The KKT conditions now sort the points into three groups: \(\alpha_i = 0\) (outside the margin), \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) (exactly on it), and \(\alpha_i = C\) (inside it or misclassified). As \(C \to \infty\) the box disappears and, if the data are separable, the hard margin returns. Small \(C\) tolerates violations for a wider margin: \(C\) is the regularisation dial.

Derivation · soft margin = regularised hinge loss

For fixed \((w, b)\), the best slack is the smallest one allowed: \(\xi_i = \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big)\) with \(f(x) = w \cdot x + b\). Substituting eliminates the constraints:

$$\min_{w, b}\ \tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i \max\big(0,\ 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big),$$

equivalently \(\frac1n\sum_i \text{hinge}_i + \frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\) with \(\lambda = 1/(nC)\). The SVM is L2-regularised empirical risk minimisation with the hinge loss, a convex upper bound on the 0–1 loss (the dashed curve on Day 11).

Convexity. The objective is a convex quadratic over a convex set, so every local minimum is global, and \(w\) is unique. There is no initialisation to choose and no bad basin to fall into. Everything after Day 31 gives this up in exchange for learned features.

Evidence

The experiments map digits into polynomial feature spaces of degree 1 to 7 through the kernel and report test error on the US Postal Service digits and on a larger NIST set. The authors report error rates competitive with the best methods of the time, including convolutional networks, from a method with almost nothing to tune; the number of support vectors stays a modest fraction of the training set even as the feature-space dimension becomes astronomically large. The paper also recalls a bound linking sparsity to generalisation: the expected test error is at most the expected number of support vectors divided by the number of training examples, a leave-one-out argument, since removing a non-support vector changes nothing.

Limits & what broke
  • Quadratic programming does not scale naively. The dual has one variable per example and a dense \(n \times n\) matrix. Decomposition methods and Platt's SMO (1998), which optimises two \(\alpha\)s at a time in closed form, made SVMs practical up to hundreds of thousands of examples, but kernel SVMs never reached deep-learning data scales.
  • No probabilities. \(f(x)\) is a score, not a probability; Platt scaling (fitting a sigmoid to it) was added later.
  • Model selection by hand. \(C\) and the kernel parameters must be cross-validated, and the margin bound rarely tells you their values.
  • Binary at heart. Multi-class needs one-versus-rest or one-versus-one wrappers. Linear SVMs remain strong for sparse text; kernel SVMs lost perception to convnets.

Bridge

Look again at the dual: the data appear only as \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\). Replace that inner product by any function that behaves like an inner product in some feature space, possibly infinite-dimensional, and the same QP fits a nonlinear boundary. Day 15 is the kernel trick.

L15 · DAY 15

A Training Algorithm for Optimal Margin Classifiers (the kernel trick)

45m videoLevel 2

Boser, Guyon & Vapnik, COLT 1992

The optimal-margin classifier only ever needs inner products between training points, so replacing \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) with a kernel \(k(x_i, x_j)\) trains a maximum-margin separator in a feature space that may be enormous or infinite-dimensional, at the cost of evaluating \(k\). Boser, Guyon and Vapnik put the margin and the kernel together and the support vector machine was born.

Cold open

A 16×16 digit image has 256 pixels. All products of four pixels number about \(\binom{259}{4} \approx 1.8 \times 10^8\) features, too many to store per image. Yet \((x \cdot z)^4\) is an inner product in exactly that space, and computing it costs 256 multiplications and one power. The RBF kernel goes further: its feature space is infinite-dimensional, and it costs one exponential.

The problem

Linear classifiers are well understood and convex to train, but too weak; nonlinear features fix that and explode in number. The idea of computing inner products implicitly was not new: Aizerman, Braverman and Rozonoer's "potential functions" (1964) used kernels with a perceptron-like rule. What was missing was a training method that combined implicit features with control of capacity, so that a huge feature space would not simply overfit. The margin supplies that control; Cortes and Vapnik's paper (Day 14), three years later, added the soft margin.

The method: the dual only touches inner products

The hard-margin dual is \(\max_\alpha \sum_i \alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j \langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) subject to \(\alpha_i \ge 0\), \(\sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\), and the decision function is \(f(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \langle x_i, x\rangle + b\). If the data are first mapped by \(\varphi\), every \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\) becomes \(\langle \varphi(\cdot), \varphi(\cdot)\rangle\). Define \(k(x, z) = \langle \varphi(x), \varphi(z)\rangle\) and \(\varphi\) never has to be computed:

$$f(x) = \sum_{i \in \text{SV}} \alpha_i y_i\, k(x_i, x) + b .$$

Training needs the \(n \times n\) Gram matrix \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\); prediction needs \(k\) between the input and each support vector (the paper's "supporting patterns"). That is the whole insight.

Theorem · what makes a valid kernel (Mercer; Moore–Aronszajn)

A symmetric function \(k\) is an inner product \(\langle\varphi(x), \varphi(z)\rangle\) in some Hilbert space if and only if it is positive semi-definite: for every finite set \(x_1, \dots, x_m\), the Gram matrix \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\) satisfies \(c^\top K c \ge 0\) for all \(c\). Mercer's condition is the integral form, \(\iint k(x, z) g(x) g(z)\,dx\,dz \ge 0\) for all square-integrable \(g\).

The PSD requirement is not a technicality. It makes the dual a concave maximisation, so the QP stays convex. With an indefinite "kernel" such as a sigmoid \(\tanh(a\,x \cdot z + c)\) for some parameters, the optimisation can have no meaningful solution.

Worked example · polynomial kernels

In \(\R^2\), \((x \cdot z)^2 = x_1^2 z_1^2 + 2x_1x_2z_1z_2 + x_2^2z_2^2 = \langle \varphi(x), \varphi(z)\rangle\) with \(\varphi(x) = (x_1^2, \sqrt2\,x_1x_2, x_2^2)\). In general \((x \cdot z)^p\) is the inner product of all degree-\(p\) monomials (suitably weighted), and \((x \cdot z + 1)^p\) of all monomials up to degree \(p\). The work is \(O(d)\) instead of \(O(d^p)\).

The method: the RBF kernel is infinite-dimensional

Derivation · the Taylor feature map of \(e^{-\gamma(x - z)^2}\)

In one dimension, expand the square and the cross term:

$$e^{-\gamma(x - z)^2} = e^{-\gamma x^2}\, e^{-\gamma z^2}\, e^{2\gamma x z} = e^{-\gamma x^2} e^{-\gamma z^2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\gamma)^k x^k z^k}{k!} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varphi_k(x)\,\varphi_k(z),$$

with \(\varphi_k(x) = e^{-\gamma x^2}\sqrt{(2\gamma)^k/k!}\;x^k\). The RBF kernel is an inner product of infinitely many weighted monomials: a polynomial kernel of every degree at once, with weights decaying like \(1/k!\). In \(d\) dimensions the same argument runs over all multi-indices.

import math
import numpy as np

def rbf(x, z, gamma):
    return math.exp(-gamma * (x - z) ** 2)

def phi(x, gamma, terms=30):
    """Explicit (truncated) feature map of the 1-D RBF kernel."""
    return np.array([math.exp(-gamma * x * x) * math.sqrt((2 * gamma) ** k / math.factorial(k)) * x ** k
                     for k in range(terms)])

x, z, gamma = 0.7, -0.4, 0.5
print("kernel:", rbf(x, z, gamma))
print("feature-space inner product, 30 terms:", phi(x, gamma) @ phi(z, gamma))

pts = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=8)
G = np.array([[rbf(a, b, gamma) for b in pts] for a in pts])
print("min eigenvalue of the Gram matrix:", np.linalg.eigvalsh(G).min())   # >= 0 (PSD)

Both lines print 0.54607…; thirty terms of the infinite feature map already agree to about 15 digits. The Gram matrix's smallest eigenvalue is about \(10^{-11}\): non-negative, and nearly singular, which is typical for smooth kernels.

The bandwidth. \(\gamma\) sets the width \(1/\sqrt{2\gamma}\) of each bump \(k(x_i, \cdot)\). Small \(\gamma\): every bump covers the whole dataset, the Gram matrix is nearly all ones, and \(f\) is nearly constant or linear: underfitting. Large \(\gamma\): every bump is a spike around one training point, \(K \to I\), any labelling can be fitted, and far from the data \(f\) falls back to \(b\): overfitting. \(\gamma\) and \(C\) are tuned together.

The method: why the solution is always a kernel expansion

Theorem · representer theorem (Kimeldorf–Wahba 1971; Schölkopf, Herbrich & Smola 2001)

Let \(\mathcal H\) be the reproducing-kernel Hilbert space of \(k\). Any minimiser of \(\sum_i L\big(y_i, f(x_i)\big) + \Omega\big(\|f\|_{\mathcal H}\big)\) with \(\Omega\) strictly increasing has the form \(f = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i\, k(x_i, \cdot)\).

Proof

Write \(f = f_\parallel + f_\perp\), with \(f_\parallel\) in the span of \(\{k(x_i, \cdot)\}\) and \(f_\perp\) orthogonal to it. The reproducing property gives \(f(x_i) = \langle f, k(x_i, \cdot)\rangle = f_\parallel(x_i)\), so the loss term ignores \(f_\perp\). But \(\|f\|^2 = \|f_\parallel\|^2 + \|f_\perp\|^2\), so any \(f_\perp \ne 0\) strictly increases the penalty. The minimiser has \(f_\perp = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

An infinite-dimensional search reduces to \(n\) coefficients. This holds for SVMs, kernel ridge regression and Gaussian processes (Day 30) alike.

Evidence

The paper's experiments are on handwritten digit recognition, comparing polynomial and radial-basis kernels with other classifiers of the time. The authors report low error rates, solutions in which only a small fraction of the training patterns are supporting patterns, and a leave-one-out error estimate bounded by that fraction. The evidence shows the method is practical and that a huge implicit feature space need not overfit when the margin is controlled. It does not yet handle noisy, non-separable data; that is Cortes and Vapnik's soft margin.

Limits & what broke
  • The \(O(n^2)\) wall. The Gram matrix needs \(n^2\) memory, and exact solvers \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n^3)\) time. At a million examples that is \(10^{12}\) kernel entries. Nyström approximations and random Fourier features (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) approximate \(k\) with explicit low-dimensional features to get back to linear cost.
  • Why kernels lost to deep nets: scaling more than expressiveness. RBF kernel machines are universal approximators, so expressiveness was not the bottleneck. Cost growing with \(n^2\) was, at exactly the moment datasets grew to millions. The second reason is that the kernel is a fixed prior: it measures similarity in input space, while a deep net learns the similarity.
  • Kernels on structure. Kernels were defined for strings (subsequence and spectrum kernels), graphs (random-walk and Weisfeiler–Lehman kernels) and sets, a real advantage before learned encoders. Each still pays the \(n^2\) cost, and a bad kernel cannot be rescued by data.

Bridge

Margins were justified by an appeal to capacity: a function class that cannot fit everything generalises. To make that precise you first need a definition of "learning" itself. Day 16 is Valiant's.

L16 · DAY 16

A Theory of the Learnable (PAC learning)

40m videoLevel 2

Valiant, CACM 1984

Valiant defines learning as a computational problem: from a polynomial number of random examples, in polynomial time, produce a rule that is probably (with probability at least \(1 - \delta\)) approximately correct (error at most \(\varepsilon\)) on the same distribution the examples came from. For a finite hypothesis class, a union bound shows that \((1/\varepsilon)(\ln|H| + \ln(1/\delta))\) examples suffice.

Cold open

What would it mean to say a program has "learned" a concept? Not that it memorised the examples, and not that it found the true rule exactly: neither is checkable or achievable from finite data. Valiant's answer, in a short paper in Communications of the ACM, is that it should be right most of the time on future examples from the same source, with high confidence, using reasonable data and computation. That definition founded computational learning theory and earned a large part of his 2010 Turing Award citation.

The problem

The existing formal model, Gold's identification in the limit (1967), asked for exact identification after finitely many examples and said nothing about how many, or how much computation. Statistics had consistency theorems but no notion of computational cost. Valiant wanted a definition under which some useful classes (he studied Boolean formulas such as \(k\)-CNF) are learnable and others provably are not, and that is distribution-free: it must work whatever the distribution of examples, which the learner does not know.

The method: the definition

Definition · PAC learnability (modern statement)

A concept class \(C\) over instances of size \(n\) is PAC-learnable by hypothesis class \(H\) if there is an algorithm \(A\) and a polynomial \(p\) such that for every target \(c \in C\), every distribution \(D\) on the instance space, and every \(\varepsilon, \delta \in (0, 1)\): given \(m \ge p(1/\varepsilon, 1/\delta, n, \operatorname{size}(c))\) i.i.d. examples \((x, c(x))\) with \(x \sim D\), \(A\) runs in polynomial time and outputs \(h \in H\) with

$$\Pr_{\text{sample}}\big[\operatorname{err}_D(h) \le \varepsilon\big] \ge 1 - \delta, \qquad \operatorname{err}_D(h) = \Pr_{x \sim D}\big[h(x) \ne c(x)\big].$$

Each clause matters. Every \(D\): no assumptions about the input distribution, but training and testing use the same one. \(\delta\): an unlucky sample can always mislead; we only ask that it rarely does. \(\varepsilon\): rare regions of the space may be wrong, because they are rarely tested. Polynomial in \(1/\varepsilon\), \(1/\delta\) and the size of the problem: in both examples and time. Valiant's paper states this with a single accuracy parameter and also allows an oracle that answers membership queries; the two-parameter form is the standard restatement, and the name "PAC" came a few years later.

The method: sample complexity for a finite class

Derivation · \(m \ge \frac1\varepsilon\big(\ln|H| + \ln\frac1\delta\big)\) (realizable case)

Assume some \(h \in H\) is perfect, and let the learner output any \(h\) consistent with the sample. Call \(h\) bad if \(\operatorname{err}_D(h) > \varepsilon\). A fixed bad \(h\) agrees with one random example with probability less than \(1 - \varepsilon\), so it survives \(m\) independent examples with probability less than \((1 - \varepsilon)^m \le e^{-\varepsilon m}\).

Union bound. There are at most \(|H|\) bad hypotheses, so the probability that any of them survives is at most \(|H|\,e^{-\varepsilon m}\). Setting this at most \(\delta\) and solving for \(m\) gives the bound. Then with probability at least \(1 - \delta\), every consistent hypothesis has error at most \(\varepsilon\).

The union bound does all the work, and its price is \(\ln|H|\), not \(|H|\): doubling the class adds only \((\ln 2)/\varepsilon\) examples. Occam's razor falls out (Blumer, Ehrenfeucht, Haussler and Warmuth, 1987). If every hypothesis can be written in \(b\) bits, then \(|H| \le 2^b\) and \(m = O\big((b + \ln\frac1\delta)/\varepsilon\big)\): short consistent explanations generalise, and the shorter, the fewer examples you need.

Algorithm · learning a conjunction by elimination
  1. Start with the conjunction of all \(2n\) literals \(x_1 \wedge \bar x_1 \wedge \dots \wedge x_n \wedge \bar x_n\), which is false everywhere.
  2. For each positive example, delete every literal it makes false. Ignore negative examples.
  3. Output what remains.

The output always contains every literal of the target, so it never says "yes" wrongly; it errs only by saying "no" on positives it has not yet seen. Valiant's algorithm for \(k\)-CNF from positive examples is this idea with clauses of up to \(k\) literals in place of single literals.

Worked example · conjunctions over 20 variables

Each variable can appear positively, negatively, or not at all, so \(|H| = 3^{20} + 1\) (adding the always-false rule) and \(\ln|H| \approx 20\ln 3 \approx 21.97\). For \(\varepsilon = 0.1\), \(\delta = 0.05\): \(m \ge 10\,(21.97 + \ln 20) = 10\,(21.97 + 3.00) \approx 250\) examples, whatever the distribution.

Realizable versus agnostic

If no hypothesis is perfect (the agnostic case), the learner minimises training error instead. Hoeffding's inequality gives \(\Pr\big[|\widehat{\operatorname{err}}(h) - \operatorname{err}(h)| > \varepsilon\big] \le 2e^{-2m\varepsilon^2}\) for each \(h\), and the same union bound gives uniform convergence over \(H\) once \(m \ge \frac{1}{2\varepsilon^2}\big(\ln|H| + \ln\frac2\delta\big)\). Empirical risk minimisation is then within \(2\varepsilon\) of the best hypothesis in \(H\). The rate drops from \(1/\varepsilon\) to \(1/\varepsilon^2\): for the conjunction example, about 1,300 examples instead of 250. Estimating an error rate is harder than certifying it is zero.

Evidence

The evidence is a set of theorems. Valiant shows that \(k\)-CNF (fixed \(k\)) is learnable from positive examples alone, and that some richer classes, such as monotone DNF, are learnable if the learner may also ask an oracle whether a chosen example is positive. He also argues that some classes should be unlearnable for computational rather than statistical reasons, anticipating the later cryptographic hardness results. The definition's real test came afterwards: it proved robust (many reasonable variants turned out to be equivalent) and productive, generating boosting (Day 11) and the VC theory of Day 17.

Limits & what broke
  • Worst case over all distributions. Real data are not adversarial, so the bounds are loose by orders of magnitude. Nobody sizes a dataset with them.
  • \(\ln|H|\) is the wrong measure for continuous classes. Halfspaces form an infinite class, yet they are learnable. VC dimension replaces \(\ln|H|\) tomorrow.
  • Representation matters for time, not just samples. Learning 3-term DNF with 3-term DNF hypotheses is NP-hard (Pitt and Valiant, 1988), yet the same targets are efficiently learnable as 3-CNF. Statistical and computational learnability come apart.
  • Neural nets make finite-class bounds vacuous-looking. A net with \(P\) float32 parameters is a finite class with \(\ln|H| \le 32P\ln 2 \approx 22P\); the realizable bound then asks for hundreds of examples per parameter, while modern nets generalise with far fewer, as Month 11 discusses.

Bridge

The union bound needs a finite \(|H|\), and even a single threshold on the real line gives an infinite class. Day 17 shows what to count instead: not how many hypotheses there are, but how many ways they can label \(n\) points.

L17 · DAY 17

On the Uniform Convergence of Relative Frequencies (VC dimension)

45m videoLevel 3

Vapnik & Chervonenkis, 1971

What makes an infinite class of classifiers learnable is not how many classifiers it contains but how many different ways it can label \(n\) points. That count is either \(2^n\) for every \(n\), or it is polynomial in \(n\) of degree \(d\), the VC dimension. When it is polynomial, training error converges to test error uniformly over the class, at a rate of roughly \(\sqrt{d/n}\), for every data distribution.

Cold open

Two puzzles from Day 16. Halfspaces in \(\R^2\) are uncountably many, yet a few hundred examples pin one down. The classifiers \(x \mapsto \operatorname{sign}(\sin\theta x)\) have a single real parameter, yet no amount of data guarantees that the one with lowest training error generalises. Counting parameters gets both wrong. Vapnik and Chervonenkis found the right count.

The problem

The Glivenko–Cantelli theorem says the empirical distribution function converges to the true one uniformly over all half-lines \((-\infty, t]\). Learning needs the same for a richer class of events: for every \(h\) in a class, the error frequency on the sample should approach its probability, simultaneously. Pointwise convergence (the law of large numbers for one fixed \(h\)) is useless, because the learner chooses \(h\) after seeing the data. The paper (the English translation of their Russian work) asks exactly when convergence is uniform, and answers with a combinatorial quantity.

The method: shattering and the growth function

Definition · shattering, growth function, VC dimension

A class \(H\) of \(\{0,1\}\)-valued functions shatters points \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) if it realises all \(2^n\) labellings of them. The growth function \(\Pi_H(n)\) is the largest number of distinct labellings \(H\) induces on any \(n\) points. The VC dimension is the largest \(n\) with \(\Pi_H(n) = 2^n\), that is, the size of the largest shattered set (infinite if there is none).

Theorem · halfspaces in \(\R^d\) have VC dimension \(d + 1\)

For \(h(x) = \operatorname{sign}(w \cdot x + b)\) on \(\R^d\), the VC dimension is exactly \(d + 1\).

Proof

At least \(d+1\). Take \(0, e_1, \dots, e_d\). For any labels \(y_0, \dots, y_d \in \{\pm1\}\), set \(b = y_0\) and \(w_i = 2y_i\). Then \(\operatorname{sign}(b) = y_0\), and \(\operatorname{sign}(2y_i + y_0) = y_i\) because \(|2y_i| > |y_0|\).

At most \(d+1\): Radon's theorem. Any \(d + 2\) points \(x_1, \dots, x_{d+2}\) in \(\R^d\) can be split into two groups whose convex hulls intersect. The \(d + 2\) vectors \((x_i, 1) \in \R^{d+1}\) are linearly dependent, so \(\sum_i \lambda_i x_i = 0\) and \(\sum_i \lambda_i = 0\) with not all \(\lambda_i = 0\). Let \(I = \{i : \lambda_i > 0\}\), \(J\) the rest, and \(\Lambda = \sum_I \lambda_i = -\sum_J \lambda_j > 0\). Then \(p = \sum_I \frac{\lambda_i}{\Lambda} x_i = \sum_J \frac{-\lambda_j}{\Lambda} x_j\) lies in both hulls. Label \(I\) positive and \(J\) negative. A halfspace containing \(I\) contains its hull and hence \(p\); its complement contains \(J\)'s hull and hence \(p\) too. Contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

The method: Sauer–Shelah and the bound

Lemma · Sauer–Shelah (a version is proved in this paper)

If \(H\) has VC dimension \(d\), then \(\Pi_H(n) \le \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom{n}{i} \le \big(\tfrac{en}{d}\big)^d\) for \(n \ge d\). Either \(\Pi_H(n) = 2^n\) for all \(n\), or it grows only polynomially once \(n > d\).

Proof sketch · induction on \(n\)

Restrict \(H\) to \(n\) points and let \(\Phi_d(n) = \sum_{i \le d}\binom ni\). Remove the last point: let \(H'\) be the set of labellings of the first \(n-1\) points, and \(H''\) the subset that occur with both labels of the last point. Then \(|H| = |H'| + |H''|\). \(H'\) has VC dimension at most \(d\), and \(H''\) at most \(d - 1\), since if \(H''\) shattered a set \(S\), \(H\) would shatter \(S\) plus the last point. By induction \(|H| \le \Phi_d(n-1) + \Phi_{d-1}(n-1) = \Phi_d(n)\), by Pascal's rule.

The same lemma was published independently by Sauer and by Shelah in 1972, hence its name. Now redo Day 16's argument with the growth function in place of \(|H|\). The key step is symmetrisation: the probability that some \(h\) has a large gap between training and true error is bounded by the probability of a large gap between its errors on the training sample and on an independent "ghost" sample of the same size. That second event only depends on the labellings induced on \(2n\) points, at most \(\Pi_H(2n)\) of them, so the union bound now costs \(\ln \Pi_H(2n) \le d \ln(2en/d)\) instead of \(\ln|H|\).

Theorem · VC generalisation bound (one classical form)

With probability at least \(1 - \delta\) over an i.i.d. sample of size \(n\), every \(h \in H\) satisfies

$$\operatorname{err}(h) \le \widehat{\operatorname{err}}(h) + \sqrt{\frac{d\big(\ln\frac{2n}{d} + 1\big) + \ln\frac4\delta}{n}} .$$

Constants differ between textbooks; the shape \(\sqrt{d\log(n/d)/n}\) does not, and chaining arguments remove the logarithm to give \(O(\sqrt{d/n})\). Conversely, a class with infinite VC dimension is not learnable in this distribution-free sense.

Evidence: VC dimension is not parameter count

The paper's evidence is its theorems; the most useful sanity check is an example. Take \(h_\theta(x) = \operatorname{sign}(\sin\theta x)\) with one real parameter. The points \(x_i = 10^{-i}\), \(i = 1, \dots, n\), are shattered for every \(n\): given labels \(y_i \in \{\pm1\}\), choose \(\theta = \pi\big(1 + \sum_i \tfrac{1 - y_i}{2}\,10^{i}\big)\). Then \(\theta x_j\) is \(\pi\) times an even integer (from the terms \(i > j\)), plus \(\pi\) exactly when \(y_j = -1\), plus a remainder in \((0, 0.22\pi)\) from the smaller terms. So \(\sin\theta x_j\) has the sign of \(y_j\). The VC dimension is infinite.

import itertools
import math

n = 6
xs = [10.0 ** -(i + 1) for i in range(n)]               # x_i = 10^-i, i = 1..n
ok = 0
for labels in itertools.product([-1, 1], repeat=n):
    theta = math.pi * (1 + sum((1 - y) / 2 * 10 ** (i + 1) for i, y in enumerate(labels)))
    preds = [1 if math.sin(theta * x) > 0 else -1 for x in xs]
    ok += preds == list(labels)
print(f"{ok} of {2 ** n} labellings realised by sign(sin(theta x)) with one parameter")

Prints "64 of 64". In the other direction, threshold networks with \(W\) weights have VC dimension \(O(W\log W)\) (Baum and Haussler, 1989): parameter count can over- or under-state capacity.

The bound is distribution-free: it must hold for the worst distribution, including ones nothing like real data. That is its strength (no assumptions) and the reason it is loose by orders of magnitude on any particular problem.

Limits & what broke
  • Vacuous for neural networks. A modern net's VC dimension is at least on the order of its parameter count, far above the number of training examples, so the bound's right-hand side exceeds 1. Zhang et al. (Day 49) showed the same nets fit random labels perfectly, so no bound that depends only on the class and \(n\) can explain why they generalise on real labels.
  • The U-curve is not the whole story. Classical theory predicts that test error rises once capacity outruns data. Double descent (Day 316) shows it falling again past the interpolation threshold. This is the classical theory that deep learning broke; the repairs (margin and norm-based bounds, PAC-Bayes, algorithm-dependent stability) all bring in the data or the training algorithm.
  • Still the right vocabulary. Shattering, growth functions and uniform convergence remain the language every newer bound is written in.

Bridge

Neural Networks and the Bias/Variance Dilemma

35m videoLevel 1

Geman, Bienenstock & Doursat, Neural Computation 1992

For squared loss, the expected error of a learned predictor splits exactly into irreducible noise, squared bias and variance, where "expected" means averaged over the training sets you might have drawn. Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat use the decomposition to argue that flexible, assumption-free learners such as neural networks pay for their flexibility in variance, and that useful structure has to be built in.

Cold open

By 1990 connectionism promised learning "from examples alone": a big enough network, trained long enough, approximates any function. Geman, Bienenstock and Doursat agree that it does in the limit, and point out that the limit is the problem. A model-free estimator converges so slowly that the data it needs are prohibitive. Their remedy reads, thirty years on, like a description of convolutional networks: build the important properties into the architecture.

The problem

Everyone knew that overly complex models overfit. What was missing was a clean account of why, stated in terms that apply to any estimator, parametric or not, and that separate what a better model can fix from what it cannot. The paper's 58 pages give the decomposition, a long discussion of nonparametric estimators (nearest neighbours, Parzen windows, networks) as consistent but data-hungry, and experiments illustrating the trade-off.

The method: the decomposition, line by line

Fix an input \(x\). Let \(y = \bar f(x) + \varepsilon\) with \(\bar f(x) = \E[y \mid x]\) (the regression function) and \(\E[\varepsilon \mid x] = 0\), \(\Var[\varepsilon \mid x] = \sigma^2(x)\). A training set \(D\) of size \(n\) produces an estimate \(f(x; D)\). The test label \(y\) is independent of \(D\).

Derivation · \(\E[(y - f)^2] = \sigma^2 + \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance}\)

Step 1, separate the noise. Write \(y - f = (y - \bar f) + (\bar f - f)\) and square:

$$\E_{D, y}\big[(y - f(x;D))^2\big] = \E\big[(y - \bar f)^2\big] + 2\,\E\big[(y - \bar f)(\bar f - f)\big] + \E_D\big[(\bar f - f(x;D))^2\big].$$

The middle term is zero: \(y - \bar f = \varepsilon\) has mean zero and is independent of \(D\), hence of \(f(x; D)\). The first term is \(\sigma^2(x)\).

Step 2, split the estimation error. Let \(\E_D f = \E_D[f(x; D)]\), the prediction averaged over training sets. Write \(\bar f - f = (\bar f - \E_D f) + (\E_D f - f)\):

$$\E_D\big[(\bar f - f)^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\E_D f - \bar f\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\E_D\big[(f - \E_D f)^2\big]}_{\text{variance}},$$

because the cross term \(2(\bar f - \E_D f)\,\E_D[\E_D f - f]\) vanishes. Averaging over \(x\) gives the global version.

Two distinctions are easy to lose. Estimator versus model. Bias is a property of the whole learning procedure at sample size \(n\) (architecture, training algorithm, regularisation, early stopping), measured against the regression function \(\bar f\), not against some "true model". Variance is over training sets. It measures how much the prediction at \(x\) would change if you retrained on a fresh sample of the same size: an ensemble concept you cannot read off one trained model. Bagging (Day 9) is a direct attack on this term, and the bootstrap is one way to estimate it.

The method: the classic U-curve

The figure computes each term exactly as defined: 400 independent training sets, a k-NN fit on each, then the squared gap between the average fit and \(\sin 2\pi x\) (bias²) and the spread of the fits around their average (variance). At \(k = 1\) the bias is essentially zero and the variance equals the noise \(\sigma^2\); at \(k = 60\) the variance is small and bias dominates. GBD make the same point with nearest-neighbour, Parzen-window and network estimators, and draw the conclusion that matters: for a fixed amount of data, a model-free estimator can lower bias only by raising variance, and the escape is to choose a better bias, one that matches the problem.

Evidence

Keep two claims apart. The decomposition is an identity: true for any estimator and any distribution under squared loss, no evidence required. The trade-off (that lowering one term raises the other, giving a U-shaped curve) is an empirical regularity of classical estimators, which the paper illustrates with experiments, including handwritten-numeral recognition. Its argument about neural networks is that, as nonparametric estimators, they inherit the slow convergence of all such methods. The data-hungry part proved right. The conclusion that more parameters must mean more variance did not survive.

Limits & what broke
  • Squared loss only. For 0–1 loss there is no additive decomposition with the same meaning, and several incompatible definitions were proposed (Kong and Dietterich; Breiman; Kohavi and Wolpert; Domingos). Friedman (1997) showed bias and variance interact multiplicatively for classification, and that where the average prediction is on the wrong side of the boundary, extra variance can reduce error.
  • Modern networks sit past the right edge of the U. Heavily over-parameterised nets interpolate the training data and still generalise. Test error can peak near the interpolation threshold and fall again as models grow (Belkin et al. 2019; Nakkiran et al., Day 316), and studies of wide networks report variance that eventually decreases with width. The decomposition still holds; the trade-off does not.
  • "Variance" is rarely measured. People infer it from a train–test gap, which conflates variance with bias of the training-set fit. Measuring it needs retraining on resampled data.

Bridge

Bias and variance describe how an estimator behaves across datasets. Day 19 turns to computing one particular estimator, maximum likelihood, when part of each data point is missing or hidden. Its key step, a lower bound built with Jensen's inequality, returns on Day 241 as the variational autoencoder.

Maximum Likelihood from Incomplete Data via the EM Algorithm

50m videoLevel 2

Dempster, Laird & Rubin, JRSS-B 1977

When the likelihood involves hidden variables, maximise it by alternating two easy steps: compute the posterior over the hidden variables under the current parameters (E-step), then refit the parameters as if those posteriors were data (M-step). Each pair of steps maximises a lower bound that touches the likelihood at the current point, so the likelihood never decreases.

Cold open

Chicken and egg. If you knew which of two Gaussians produced each data point, fitting the Gaussians would be two sample means. If you knew the Gaussians, assigning each point would be Bayes' rule. EM does both, alternately, and converges. Versions of the idea existed in many places, including Baum and colleagues' HMM re-estimation (Day 25). Dempster, Laird and Rubin named it, stated it in general, proved its key property, and showed a dozen problems to be special cases.

The problem

With observed data \(x\), latent variables \(z\) and parameters \(\theta\), the quantity to maximise is the incomplete-data log-likelihood \(\log p(x \mid \theta) = \log \sum_z p(x, z \mid \theta)\). The logarithm of a sum has no closed-form maximiser even when the complete-data log-likelihood \(\log p(x, z \mid \theta)\) is simple, as it is for mixtures, missing values, censored data, factor analysis and hidden Markov models.

The method: the ELBO, via Jensen

Derivation · the lower bound and its gap

For any distribution \(q(z)\) over the latents with \(q(z) > 0\) wherever \(p(x, z \mid \theta) > 0\):

$$\log p(x \mid \theta) = \log \sum_z q(z)\,\frac{p(x, z \mid \theta)}{q(z)} \;\ge\; \sum_z q(z) \log \frac{p(x, z \mid \theta)}{q(z)} =: \mathcal F(q, \theta),$$

by Jensen's inequality, since \(\log\) is concave. This is the evidence lower bound. The gap is exact: writing \(p(x, z \mid \theta) = p(z \mid x, \theta)\,p(x \mid \theta)\),

$$\log p(x \mid \theta) - \mathcal F(q, \theta) = \sum_z q(z)\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x, \theta)} = \mathrm{KL}\big(q \,\|\, p(z \mid x, \theta)\big) \ge 0 .$$

Also \(\mathcal F(q, \theta) = \E_q[\log p(x, z \mid \theta)] + H(q)\): an expected complete-data log-likelihood plus an entropy that does not depend on \(\theta\).

Algorithm · EM as coordinate ascent on \(\mathcal F\)
  1. E-step (tighten the bound). Set \(q_t(z) = p(z \mid x, \theta_t)\). The KL gap becomes zero: \(\mathcal F(q_t, \theta_t) = \log p(x \mid \theta_t)\).
  2. M-step (maximise the bound). \(\theta_{t+1} = \arg\max_\theta \mathcal F(q_t, \theta) = \arg\max_\theta Q(\theta \mid \theta_t)\), where \(Q(\theta \mid \theta_t) = \E_{z \sim p(z \mid x, \theta_t)}[\log p(x, z \mid \theta)]\) is DLR's notation.
Theorem · monotonicity (DLR's Theorem 1, in bound form) $$\log p(x \mid \theta_{t+1}) \;\ge\; \mathcal F(q_t, \theta_{t+1}) \;\ge\; \mathcal F(q_t, \theta_t) \;=\; \log p(x \mid \theta_t).$$

The first inequality is the bound, the second is the M-step, the equality is the E-step. Any \(\theta_{t+1}\) that merely increases \(Q\) also works, which DLR call generalised EM (GEM).

The method: a two-component Gaussian mixture, by hand

The latent \(z_i \in \{1, 2\}\) says which component produced \(x_i\). The E-step computes responsibilities \(r_{ik} = \pi_k \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2) / \sum_j \pi_j \mathcal N(x_i; \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)\). The M-step is weighted maximum likelihood: \(N_k = \sum_i r_{ik}\), \(\pi_k = N_k/n\), \(\mu_k = \sum_i r_{ik}x_i / N_k\), \(\sigma_k^2 = \sum_i r_{ik}(x_i - \mu_k)^2/N_k\).

Worked example · four points, one iteration

Data \(x = \{0, 1, 4, 5\}\); start at \(\mu = (1, 3)\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\), \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). With equal weights and variances, \(r_{i1} = 1/\big(1 + e^{\,[(x_i-1)^2 - (x_i-3)^2]/2}\big) = 1/(1 + e^{2x_i - 4})\).

E-step: \(r_{\cdot 1} = (0.982,\ 0.881,\ 0.018,\ 0.0025)\). M-step: \(N_1 = 1.883\), \(\mu_1 = (0.881 + 4 \times 0.018 + 5 \times 0.0025)/1.883 \approx 0.512\); likewise \(\mu_2 \approx 4.27\). One step moved the means most of the way to the clusters' centres \(0.5\) and \(4.5\), where EM settles two iterations later.

import numpy as np

x = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 4.0, 5.0])
mu, var, pi = np.array([1.0, 3.0]), np.array([1.0, 1.0]), np.array([0.5, 0.5])

def loglik(x, mu, var, pi):
    dens = pi * np.exp(-(x[:, None] - mu) ** 2 / (2 * var)) / np.sqrt(2 * np.pi * var)
    return np.log(dens.sum(axis=1)).sum(), dens

for it in range(6):
    ll, dens = loglik(x, mu, var, pi)
    r = dens / dens.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)    # E-step: responsibilities
    Nk = r.sum(axis=0)                            # M-step: weighted counts
    mu = (r * x[:, None]).sum(axis=0) / Nk
    var = (r * (x[:, None] - mu) ** 2).sum(axis=0) / Nk
    pi = Nk / len(x)
    print(f"iter {it}: loglik {ll:8.4f}  r(x=1, comp 1) {r[1, 0]:.3f}  mu {mu.round(3)}  var {var.round(3)}")

The log-likelihood goes −9.28, −6.48, −5.68, −5.676 and stops: never decreasing, as the theorem promises. The fixed point is \(\mu = (0.5, 4.5)\), \(\sigma^2 = (0.25, 0.25)\).

k-means is hard-assignment EM. Give every component the same variance \(\sigma^2 I\) and equal weights, and let \(\sigma^2 \to 0\). The responsibilities become 0 or 1 (each point goes entirely to its nearest mean) and the M-step becomes "move each mean to the centroid of its points". That is Lloyd's algorithm, Day 20.

Evidence

The paper was read before the Royal Statistical Society and published with an extensive discussion. Its evidence is the general theorem plus a catalogue of examples: multinomial models with grouped cells, missing values in multivariate normal data, censored and truncated data, finite mixtures, variance-components models, factor analysis, and iteratively reweighted least squares. For exponential families the E-step reduces to computing expected sufficient statistics, which is why so many special cases look alike. DLR also show that convergence near the optimum is linear, with a rate set by the fraction of missing information: the more the latents hide, the slower EM goes.

Limits & what broke
  • The convergence proof had a gap. Wu (1983) showed DLR's argument for convergence to a maximum was flawed, and gave conditions under which EM converges to a stationary point of the likelihood. Monotonicity is solid; global optimality never was.
  • Local optima and initialisation. Mixture likelihoods are multimodal. Practice is several restarts, often initialised by k-means.
  • Degenerate solutions. Put one Gaussian's mean on a single data point and shrink its variance: the likelihood goes to infinity. The global "maximum" of an unconstrained Gaussian mixture is a useless spike. Variance floors or priors (MAP-EM) fix it.
  • The E-step must be tractable. For most deep latent-variable models \(p(z \mid x, \theta)\) cannot be computed. Neal and Hinton (1998) recast EM as coordinate ascent on \(\mathcal F\), which licenses partial E-steps; replacing the exact posterior with a learned approximate \(q\) and maximising the same bound gives the VAE (Day 241).

Bridge

Take EM for a Gaussian mixture, make the assignments hard and the variances equal, and you get the clustering algorithm everyone learns first. Day 20 finds it in a 1957 Bell Labs memo about quantising telephone signals.

Least Squares Quantization in PCM (k-means)

30m videoLevel 1

Lloyd, 1957/IEEE IT 1982

To quantise a signal with the least mean-squared error, alternate two conditions until neither changes anything: every value goes to its nearest quantisation level, and every level sits at the mean of the values assigned to it. Run on a dataset instead of a probability density, that alternation is the k-means algorithm. It always converges, usually to a local optimum, and it silently assumes round clusters of similar size.

Cold open

Bell Telephone Laboratories, 1957. Stuart Lloyd writes an internal memo on pulse-code modulation: a telephone signal is sampled, and each sample must be sent as one of \(K\) levels. Where should the levels go? The memo circulated for 25 years before appearing in an issue of IEEE Transactions on Information Theory devoted to quantisation in 1982. By then its algorithm had been rediscovered several times, and named "k-means" by MacQueen (1967).

The problem

A uniform quantiser wastes levels where the signal rarely goes. Given the amplitude density \(p(x)\) and \(K\) levels, choose thresholds \(t_1 < \dots < t_{K-1}\) and levels \(q_1, \dots, q_K\) to minimise the quantisation noise power \(D = \sum_j \int_{t_{j-1}}^{t_j} (x - q_j)^2 p(x)\,dx\). The joint problem is non-convex. Max (1960) studied the same question independently; optimal scalar quantisers are often called Lloyd–Max quantisers.

The method: two necessary conditions

Derivation · nearest-neighbour and centroid conditions

Levels fixed, choose thresholds. Each \(x\) should go to the level that costs least, \(\arg\min_j (x - q_j)^2\). In one dimension the boundary between \(q_j\) and \(q_{j+1}\) is their midpoint, \(t_j = (q_j + q_{j+1})/2\). In general the regions are the Voronoi cells of the levels.

Regions fixed, choose levels. \(\frac{\partial D}{\partial q_j} = -2\int_{R_j} (x - q_j)p(x)\,dx = 0\) gives \(q_j = \E[x \mid x \in R_j]\): the centroid of the region under \(p\).

An optimal quantiser must satisfy both conditions. Lloyd's first method alternates them; each half-step can only lower \(D\), because it solves its own sub-problem exactly.

Algorithm · Lloyd's iteration on data (k-means)
  1. Choose \(K\) initial centres \(\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K\).
  2. Assign: \(c_i = \arg\min_j \|x_i - \mu_j\|^2\) (each point to its nearest centre).
  3. Update: \(\mu_j = \) mean of \(\{x_i : c_i = j\}\).
  4. Repeat until the assignments stop changing. The objective is \(J = \sum_i \|x_i - \mu_{c_i}\|^2\).
Result · guaranteed convergence

\(J\) never increases: the assignment step minimises \(J\) over \(c\) with \(\mu\) fixed, and the update step minimises it over \(\mu\) with \(c\) fixed (the mean minimises the sum of squared distances). There are finitely many partitions of \(n\) points, and no partition repeats unless \(J\) is unchanged, so the algorithm stops after finitely many steps at a fixed point. That fixed point is a local minimum in the sense of the two conditions, not necessarily the global one.

The assignment step is a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier (Day 7) against the centres: k-means partitions space into the Voronoi cells of its centroids. The update step is the M-step of EM for a mixture of spherical Gaussians with equal, vanishing variance (Day 19).

The method: the objective is hard, the algorithm is local

Minimising \(J\) exactly is NP-hard: for points in general dimension even with \(k = 2\) (Dasgupta; Aloise et al.), and in the plane when \(k\) is part of the input (Mahajan, Nimbhorkar and Varadarajan). Lloyd's iteration sidesteps this by being local, and what it finds depends on where it starts. Arthur and Vassilvitskii's k-means++ (2007) seeds the centres randomly but spread out: the first uniformly, each next one with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\), the squared distance to the nearest centre chosen so far. They prove the seeding alone gives \(\E[J] \le 8(\ln k + 2)\,J_{\text{opt}}\), an \(O(\log k)\) guarantee before Lloyd's iterations even start.

import numpy as np

def kmeans_pp_init(X, k, rng):
    """k-means++: each new centre is sampled with probability proportional to D(x)^2."""
    C = [X[rng.integers(len(X))]]
    for _ in range(k - 1):
        d2 = np.min(((X[:, None, :] - np.array(C)[None]) ** 2).sum(-1), axis=1)
        C.append(X[rng.choice(len(X), p=d2 / d2.sum())])
    return np.array(C)

def lloyd(X, C, iters=100):
    for _ in range(iters):
        labels = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(1)   # assignment: Voronoi cells
        newC = np.array([X[labels == j].mean(0) if np.any(labels == j) else C[j]
                         for j in range(len(C))])                       # update: centroids
        if np.allclose(newC, C):
            break
        C = newC
    J = ((X - C[labels]) ** 2).sum()
    return C, labels, J

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
centres = rng.uniform(-10, 10, size=(8, 2))
X = np.concatenate([c + rng.normal(0, 0.6, size=(50, 2)) for c in centres])
for name, init in [("random rows", lambda: X[rng.choice(len(X), 8, replace=False)]),
                   ("k-means++  ", lambda: kmeans_pp_init(X, 8, rng))]:
    Js = [lloyd(X, init())[2] for _ in range(20)]
    print(f"{name}: best J = {min(Js):7.1f}  median J = {np.median(Js):7.1f}  worst J = {max(Js):7.1f}")

Eight well-separated blobs, 20 restarts each. Random initial centres: best \(J \approx 265\), median about 596, worst 1809, so most runs merge some blobs and split others. k-means++: same best, median about 293. Local minima are real, and seeding matters.

Evidence: what the objective assumes

Lloyd's memo argues analytically: the two conditions, the monotone iteration, and its use for designing quantisers for given signal densities. As a clustering method, the evidence about k-means is its objective. \(J\) is the log-likelihood of a mixture of spherical Gaussians with one shared variance and equal weights (in the hard-assignment limit), so it prefers clusters that are round and of similar spread. When clusters are elongated, the objective itself, not a bad initialisation, picks the wrong answer:

Choosing \(k\). \(J\) always decreases as \(k\) grows (\(k = n\) gives \(J = 0\)), so it cannot choose \(k\) on its own. The elbow heuristic looks for a bend in \(J(k)\); the silhouette compares each point's mean distance to its own cluster, \(a\), with that to the nearest other cluster, \(b\), via \((b - a)/\max(a, b)\); the gap statistic (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001) compares \(J(k)\) with its value on reference data with no clusters. The honest answer is that clustering without a downstream purpose is ill-posed. Kleinberg (2002) proved that no clustering function can satisfy three natural axioms (scale invariance, richness, consistency) at once.

Limits & what broke
  • Isotropic, equal-variance clusters assumed. Elongated, differently sized or non-convex clusters (rings, moons) are cut wrongly, as the figure shows. Use Gaussian mixtures, density-based methods or spectral clustering.
  • Scale-dependent. Rescaling one feature changes every distance and therefore the answer. Standardise deliberately, not by habit.
  • Worst-case iteration counts are exponential (Vattani, 2011), even in the plane, although practical runs converge in a handful of passes.
  • Hard assignments discard uncertainty. A point midway between two centres counts fully for one; soft EM keeps the ambiguity.

Lloyd's real subject, vector quantisation (representing a vector by the index of its nearest codebook entry), returns in Month 9: VQ-VAE (Day 243) learns a codebook whose entries are updated towards the mean of the encoder outputs assigned to them, which is Lloyd's update step inside a neural network.

Bridge

k-means compresses data to \(K\) points. Day 21 compresses it to a \(K\)-dimensional linear subspace instead, keeping the directions of greatest variance, and shows them as faces.

Eigenfaces for Recognition

40m videoLevel 1

Turk & Pentland, J. Cognitive Neuro. 1991

Treat each face image as one long vector, find the few directions along which a set of faces varies most (the principal components, which look like ghostly faces), and describe any face by its coordinates along them. Computing those directions is cheap because of a trick: diagonalise the small \(M \times M\) matrix of inner products between images rather than the enormous pixel covariance.

Cold open

PCA is usually a column of numbers. Here the eigenvectors can be displayed as images, and they look like faces: blurry, lit from odd angles, somewhere between everyone in the training set. Turk and Pentland built a near-real-time recogniser on them, detecting and identifying people from a video camera with early-1990s hardware.

The problem

Earlier face recognition measured geometric features (distances between eyes, nose and mouth), which were hand-designed and hard to extract reliably. Sirovich and Kirby (1987) had shown that faces can be compressed well by principal component analysis (the Karhunen–Loève expansion). Turk and Pentland's question was whether that compact code is good enough to recognise people, fast, and whether it could also tell a face from a non-face.

The method: PCA on images, and the snapshot trick

Take \(M\) training images \(\Gamma_1, \dots, \Gamma_M\), each flattened to \(d\) pixels (for \(256 \times 256\) images, \(d = 65{,}536\)). Subtract the mean face \(\Psi = \frac1M\sum_i \Gamma_i\) to get \(\Phi_i = \Gamma_i - \Psi\), and stack them as the columns of \(A \in \R^{d \times M}\). The covariance is \(C = \frac1M A A^\top\), a \(d \times d\) matrix with over four billion entries. But it has rank at most \(M - 1\), so at most \(M - 1\) eigenvectors are worth finding.

Derivation · the snapshot trick, and PCA = SVD

If \(A^\top A\, v = \lambda v\), multiply by \(A\): \(A A^\top (A v) = \lambda (A v)\), so \(u = Av\) is an eigenvector of \(AA^\top\) with the same eigenvalue. Its length is \(\|Av\|^2 = v^\top A^\top A v = \lambda\), hence \(u = Av/\sqrt\lambda\) is a unit eigenvector.

The same fact in SVD form: write \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\). Then \(AA^\top = U\Sigma^2U^\top\) and \(A^\top A = V\Sigma^2V^\top\), so the principal directions are the left singular vectors \(U\), the eigenvalues are \(\sigma_i^2\), and \(u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i\). PCA of centred data is the SVD of the centred data matrix, and the snapshot trick is computing it through the smaller Gram matrix. Projecting onto the top \(K\) directions is also the best rank-\(K\) approximation of \(A\) in squared error (Eckart–Young), and the error is the sum of the discarded \(\sigma_i^2\).

Mean subtraction is not optional. Without it, the first "component" points roughly at the mean face, because the largest second moment of uncentred data is its offset from the origin. It encodes average brightness, not variation between people, and every later component is distorted by having to be orthogonal to it.

The method: face space, recognition and detection

Keep the top \(K\) eigenfaces (the variance-explained curve \(\sum_{k \le K}\lambda_k / \sum_k \lambda_k\) guides the choice). A new image \(\Gamma\) gets weights \(\omega_k = u_k^\top(\Gamma - \Psi)\), its coordinates in face space, and a reconstruction \(\Phi_f = \sum_k \omega_k u_k\).

Algorithm · recognise, reject or detect
  1. For each known person \(k\), average the weight vectors of their training images into a class vector \(\Omega_k\).
  2. For a new image compute \(\omega\), its distance to each class \(\varepsilon_k = \|\omega - \Omega_k\|\), and the distance from face space \(\varepsilon = \|(\Gamma - \Psi) - \Phi_f\|\).
  3. If \(\varepsilon\) is large, it is not a face. If \(\varepsilon\) is small and some \(\varepsilon_k\) is below a threshold, it is person \(k\). If \(\varepsilon\) is small but every \(\varepsilon_k\) is large, it is an unknown face, which can be added as a new person.
  4. For detection, compute \(\varepsilon\) at every location of a larger image; faces are the minima of this "face map".
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
M, d = 40, 64 * 64                                 # 40 "images" of 64x64 pixels
faces = rng.normal(size=(M, 5)) @ rng.normal(size=(5, d)) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=(M, d))
mean_face = faces.mean(axis=0)
A = (faces - mean_face).T                          # d x M, columns are centred images

L = A.T @ A                                        # M x M instead of d x d (4096 x 4096)
lam, V = np.linalg.eigh(L)
lam, V = lam[::-1], V[:, ::-1]                     # descending order
U = A @ V[:, :5] / np.sqrt(lam[:5])                # eigenfaces: unit-norm eigenvectors of A A^T

U_svd = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)[0][:, :5]
print("agreement with SVD (|cos|):", np.abs(np.sum(U * U_svd, axis=0)).round(6))
print("variance explained by 5 components:", (lam[:5].sum() / lam.sum()).round(4))

x = faces[0] - mean_face
w = U.T @ x                                        # face-space coordinates
print("distance from face space:", np.linalg.norm(x - U @ w).round(3), "of", np.linalg.norm(x).round(3))

Synthetic "faces" built from 5 hidden factors plus noise: the snapshot eigenvectors match the SVD's exactly (\(|\cos| = 1\)), 5 components explain 99.8% of the variance, and a training image lies close to face space (residual 6.2 against a norm of 58.9).

Evidence

The authors collected a database of over 2,500 images of 16 people, varying lighting, head orientation and apparent size in a controlled way, and tested recognition with each factor varied between training and test images. Accuracy was high when only the lighting changed, lower across head orientation, and much lower across changes of scale, which the system had to correct for separately. They also demonstrated a working pipeline that located heads by motion, used distance from face space to confirm a face, and identified it. The evidence shows that a few dozen numbers suffice for recognition under controlled conditions, and exactly which nuisance factors break it.

Limits & what broke
  • The top components are mostly lighting. PCA keeps the directions of largest variance, and across images of a few people the largest variation is often illumination, not identity. Belhumeur, Hespanha and Kriegman (1997) found that discarding the first three principal components made recognition more robust to lighting, and proposed Fisherfaces, which choose directions that separate people (Day 22).
  • Pose and alignment. A pixel-wise linear model needs faces centred, scaled and frontal; a turned head moves every pixel and leaves face space.
  • Unsupervised directions. PCA never sees identity labels, so maximum variance is only luckily discriminative.
  • Superseded by local features and, from the mid-2010s, by deep networks trained to produce face embeddings. The same work started the long-running debate about automated face recognition and consent.

Bridge

Eigenfaces fail when the biggest variation is not the variation you care about. Use the labels: Day 22, Fisher's 1936 discriminant, finds the directions that maximise separation between classes relative to the spread within them.

L22 · DAY 22

The Use of Multiple Measurements in Taxonomic Problems (LDA)

35m videoLevel 1

Fisher, Annals of Eugenics 1936

Fisher asks which weighted combination of several measurements best tells two species apart, and answers: the one that maximises the squared difference of class means relative to the spread within classes, \(w \propto S_W^{-1}(m_1 - m_2)\). With \(C\) classes the answer is the top \(C - 1\) generalised eigenvectors of \(S_W^{-1}S_B\), and the same direction is the Bayes-optimal boundary for Gaussian classes sharing one covariance.

Cold open

The iris dataset begins here: 50 flowers from each of three species, Iris setosa, versicolor and virginica, with four measurements each (sepal and petal, length and width), collected by the botanist Edgar Anderson. It is in nearly every machine-learning library. The journal it appeared in deserves an honest word. The Annals of Eugenics was founded by Karl Pearson at University College London to publish eugenics research; it was renamed the Annals of Human Genetics in 1954. Fisher held UCL's Galton chair of eugenics and supported eugenic policies throughout his career, and institutions have since revisited how they commemorate him (his Cambridge college removed a memorial window in 2020). The mathematics stands on its own; the context is part of the history, and many teachers now prefer other demonstration datasets.

The problem

A taxonomist has several measurements per specimen. Each separates the species a little, and they are correlated. How should they be combined into a single score that discriminates best? Comparing one measurement at a time ignores the correlations; a distance-based approach (Mahalanobis published his generalised distance the same year) answers a related question. Fisher wants an explicit linear compound \(y = w^\top x\) and a criterion for choosing \(w\).

The method: Fisher's criterion

Definition · scatter matrices and the criterion

For two classes with means \(m_1, m_2\), the within-class scatter is \(S_W = \sum_{c}\sum_{x \in c}(x - m_c)(x - m_c)^\top\) and the between-class scatter is \(S_B = (m_1 - m_2)(m_1 - m_2)^\top\). Projecting onto \(w\), the squared distance between projected means is \(w^\top S_B w\) and the projected within-class spread is \(w^\top S_W w\). Fisher maximises

$$J(w) = \frac{w^\top S_B\, w}{w^\top S_W\, w}.$$
Derivation · \(w^* \propto S_W^{-1}(m_1 - m_2)\)

\(J\) is unchanged by rescaling \(w\). Setting its gradient to zero,

$$\nabla J = \frac{2\,S_B w\,(w^\top S_W w) - 2\,S_W w\,(w^\top S_B w)}{(w^\top S_W w)^2} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; S_B w = J(w)\, S_W w .$$

But \(S_B w = (m_1 - m_2)\big[(m_1 - m_2)^\top w\big]\) always points along \(m_1 - m_2\). So \(S_W w \propto m_1 - m_2\), giving \(w \propto S_W^{-1}(m_1 - m_2)\) when \(S_W\) is invertible. The within-class covariance "whitens" the problem: directions in which the classes are naturally spread out are down-weighted.

The method: many classes, and why at most \(C - 1\) directions

With \(C\) classes, overall mean \(m\) and class sizes \(n_c\), set \(S_B = \sum_c n_c (m_c - m)(m_c - m)^\top\). Maximising the same ratio for each successive direction, subject to being uncorrelated with the earlier ones, gives the generalised eigenproblem \(S_B w = \lambda S_W w\), equivalently the eigenvectors of \(S_W^{-1}S_B\) with the largest eigenvalues.

Derivation · \(\operatorname{rank} S_B \le C - 1\)

\(S_B\) is a sum of \(C\) rank-one matrices built from the vectors \(m_c - m\). These satisfy one linear constraint, \(\sum_c n_c (m_c - m) = \sum_c n_c m_c - n\,m = 0\), so they span at most \(C - 1\) dimensions. Hence \(S_W^{-1}S_B\) has at most \(C - 1\) non-zero eigenvalues: for iris, at most two useful discriminant directions however many measurements are taken.

The method: LDA as a generative classifier

Derivation · shared-covariance Gaussians give a linear boundary

Model each class as \(x \mid c \sim \mathcal N(\mu_c, \Sigma)\) with one shared \(\Sigma\) and priors \(\pi_c\). The log posterior odds are

$$\log\frac{p(1 \mid x)}{p(2 \mid x)} = \log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_2} - \tfrac12(x - \mu_1)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu_1) + \tfrac12(x - \mu_2)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu_2) = (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^\top\Sigma^{-1}x + \text{const},$$

because the quadratic terms \(x^\top\Sigma^{-1}x\) cancel. The boundary is linear with normal \(\Sigma^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_2)\): Fisher's direction, with the pooled \(S_W/(n - C)\) estimating \(\Sigma\). Fisher's criterion needs no Gaussian assumption; the Gaussian model says when the resulting rule is Bayes-optimal.

Give each class its own \(\Sigma_c\) and the quadratic terms no longer cancel: quadratic discriminant analysis (QDA), with curved boundaries and \(C\) covariance matrices to estimate instead of one. QDA is the relaxed-covariance sibling: less bias, much more variance (Day 18) when \(d\) is large.

Evidence

The paper is short and applied. Fisher computes the discriminant between I. setosa and I. versicolor from the four measurements, shows that the compound separates the two species with no overlap, and works through the arithmetic in tables. He then applies the method to I. virginica as well and uses it to examine a genetic hypothesis about whether I. versicolor could have arisen as a hybrid of the other two. The evidence shows a practical, closed-form discriminant; it does not show optimality on non-Gaussian data, and setosa is so distinct that almost any method separates it.

Limits & what broke
  • Singular \(S_W\) when \(d > n\). \(S_W\) has rank at most \(n - C\), so with more measurements than samples (pixels of face images, genes) it cannot be inverted. Fixes: shrink it, \(S_W + \lambda I\) (regularised discriminant analysis, Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage), or reduce dimension first with PCA, which is exactly Fisherfaces (Belhumeur et al., 1997), built on Day 21.
  • At most \(C - 1\) directions. Two classes give one projection, however rich the data.
  • Linear, and sensitive to outliers. Means and covariances are not robust; heavy tails or multimodal classes break the shared-Gaussian picture.
  • Iris is too easy. It is useful for checking code, useless for comparing methods.

L23 · DAY 23

A Comparison of Event Models for Naive Bayes Text Classification

30m videoLevel 1

McCallum & Nigam, 1998

"Naive Bayes" for text is two different models. The multivariate Bernoulli model treats a document as the set of vocabulary words present or absent; the multinomial model treats it as a bag of word tokens with counts. McCallum and Nigam show that the Bernoulli model does well with small vocabularies, while the multinomial usually wins once the vocabulary is large.

Cold open

Naive Bayes is wrong on purpose: it assumes that, given the class, every word is independent of every other. "San" and "Francisco" are not independent. Yet for decades it has been the first spam filter, the first sentiment classifier, and the baseline that newer models must beat. This short workshop paper makes the point that the word "naive Bayes" hides a modelling choice, and that the choice matters.

The problem

Bayes' rule classifies a document \(d\) by \(\arg\max_c P(c)\,P(d \mid c)\). Everything depends on the event model for \(P(d \mid c)\): what random process is imagined to generate the document. Papers of the 1990s used two models interchangeably under one name, and nobody had compared them carefully across datasets and vocabulary sizes.

The method: two event models

Definition · the two likelihoods, with Laplace smoothing

Multivariate Bernoulli. \(B_w \in \{0, 1\}\) indicates whether word \(w\) occurs in \(d\): \(P(d \mid c) = \prod_{w \in V}\big[B_w\, p_{wc} + (1 - B_w)(1 - p_{wc})\big]\), estimated by \(p_{wc} = \frac{1 + \#\{\text{docs in } c \text{ containing } w\}}{2 + \#\{\text{docs in } c\}}\).

Multinomial. With counts \(n_w\), \(P(d \mid c) \propto \prod_{w} \theta_{wc}^{\,n_w}\) (the length distribution and multinomial coefficient do not depend on \(c\) and drop out), estimated by \(\theta_{wc} = \frac{1 + \text{count}(w, c)}{|V| + \sum_{w'}\text{count}(w', c)}\).

The added 1s are Laplace smoothing (Lidstone: add \(\alpha\) instead). Without them, one word never seen in class \(c\) makes \(P(d \mid c) = 0\) whatever else the document says.

Work in log space. A long document multiplies thousands of probabilities below 1; the product underflows double precision (smallest positive value around \(10^{-308}\)) after a few hundred factors. Sum logarithms instead, and normalise posteriors with the log-sum-exp trick.

The method: naive Bayes is a linear classifier

Derivation · linear in the features

Multinomial: \(\log P(c \mid d) = \log P(c) + \sum_w n_w \log\theta_{wc} + \text{const}\), a linear function of the count vector with weights \(\log\theta_{wc}\). For two classes the decision is \(\sum_w n_w \log\frac{\theta_{w1}}{\theta_{w2}} + \log\frac{\pi_1}{\pi_2} \gtrless 0\): a hyperplane.

Bernoulli: \(\log P(d \mid c) = \sum_w B_w \log\frac{p_{wc}}{1 - p_{wc}} + \sum_w \log(1 - p_{wc})\), linear in the binary vector \(B\) plus a class-dependent bias. Both are linear models whose weights are set by counting rather than by optimisation.

import math
from collections import Counter

train = [("free money now free offer", "spam"), ("click now for free money", "spam"),
         ("meeting moved to friday", "ham"), ("lunch on friday with the team", "ham"),
         ("project report for the meeting", "ham")]
V = sorted({w for d, _ in train for w in d.split()})
classes = sorted({c for _, c in train})

def fit(alpha=1.0):
    prior, multi, bern = {}, {}, {}
    for c in classes:
        docs = [d.split() for d, k in train if k == c]
        prior[c] = math.log(len(docs) / len(train))
        counts = Counter(w for d in docs for w in d)                   # token counts
        total = sum(counts.values())
        multi[c] = {w: math.log((counts[w] + alpha) / (total + alpha * len(V))) for w in V}
        df = Counter(w for d in docs for w in set(d))                  # document frequencies
        bern[c] = {w: (df[w] + alpha) / (len(docs) + 2 * alpha) for w in V}
    return prior, multi, bern

def score(doc, prior, multi, bern):
    words = [w for w in doc.split() if w in V]
    present = set(words)
    m = {c: prior[c] + sum(multi[c][w] for w in words) for c in classes}   # repeats count
    b = {c: prior[c] + sum(math.log(bern[c][w]) if w in present else math.log(1 - bern[c][w])
                           for w in V) for c in classes}                    # absences count
    return m, b

prior, multi, bern = fit()
for doc in ["free free free money", "friday meeting report"]:
    m, b = score(doc, prior, multi, bern)
    pm = 1 / (1 + math.exp(m["ham"] - m["spam"]))                      # P(spam | doc), multinomial
    pb = 1 / (1 + math.exp(b["ham"] - b["spam"]))
    print(f"{doc!r}: P(spam) multinomial = {pm:.4f}, Bernoulli = {pb:.4f}")

Both models agree on the labels; they disagree on the confidence. The multinomial gives "free free free money" 0.996 because each repeat adds evidence; the Bernoulli model counts "free" once but lets every absent vocabulary word vote.

Evidence: the crossover

The experiments use five text collections (including 20 Newsgroups, WebKB and Reuters) and vary the vocabulary size by keeping the words with the highest mutual information with the class. The paper's key figures plot accuracy against vocabulary size for both models. The Bernoulli model often does best at small vocabularies, of a few hundred to a few thousand words, and degrades as the vocabulary grows; the multinomial keeps improving and is usually better at large vocabularies. The authors summarise the multinomial as reducing error by about a quarter on average (27%) relative to the Bernoulli model. The explanation they offer fits the figure above: with a large vocabulary, the Bernoulli likelihood is dominated by thousands of "absent" factors carrying little information, and it ignores how often a word occurs. The evidence is for topic classification of moderately long documents; for very short texts the two models converge.

Why a wrong model still classifies well

Classification needs only the arg max to be right, not the probabilities. Domingos and Pazzani (1997) showed that naive Bayes can be optimal under 0–1 loss even when its independence assumption is badly violated, as long as the dependencies do not change which class scores highest. And as a high-bias, low-variance learner (Day 18) it approaches its asymptotic error with far fewer examples than logistic regression, although logistic regression eventually wins with enough data (Ng and Jordan, 2001).

Limits & what broke
  • Badly calibrated probabilities. Correlated words are counted as independent evidence, so posteriors are pushed towards 0 and 1: a document containing "San Francisco" gets the evidence twice. The arg max can be right while "99.9% sure" is meaningless. Calibration methods such as binning and isotonic regression were applied to naive Bayes early on, and the problem returns for neural networks on Day 326.
  • Systematic biases in the multinomial. Rennie et al. (2003) showed that skewed class sizes and word burstiness distort the weights, and proposed fixes (complement naive Bayes, log and TF-IDF transforms of counts).
  • Independence blocks feature engineering. Adding bigrams double-counts evidence further; discriminative models (logistic regression, SVMs) absorb correlated features gracefully. Still, naive-Bayes features inside a linear SVM (Wang and Manning's NBSVM, 2012) remained a strong sentiment baseline well into the neural era.

Bridge

Naive Bayes sets the weights of a linear classifier by counting. Day 24 sets them by maximising likelihood directly, with arbitrary overlapping features and no independence assumption, and shows that the resulting model, the softmax, is also the maximum-entropy distribution consistent with the features.

L24 · DAY 24

A Maximum Entropy Approach to Natural Language Processing

45m videoLevel 2

Berger, Della Pietra & Della Pietra, Comp. Ling. 1996

Among all distributions that reproduce the observed averages of some chosen features, pick the one with the most entropy: assume nothing else. Lagrange multipliers show that this distribution is always exponential in the features, \(p(y \mid x) \propto \exp\big(\sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x, y)\big)\), and a duality theorem shows that finding it is the same as fitting that exponential model by maximum likelihood. With indicator features of the input and class, it is the softmax.

Cold open

Every classifier head in this series ends with a softmax, usually justified as "a smooth arg max that sums to one". This paper gives a better reason: the softmax is the least committal distribution consistent with what the features say. Read every softmax layer from now on as a maximum-entropy model whose features were learned.

The problem

IBM's statistical translation system needed context-dependent probabilities such as "how is English in translated into French, given the surrounding words?" There are many overlapping, correlated cues (the next word, the previous word, the sentence's topic). N-gram counts cannot combine them, and naive Bayes (Day 23) double-counts them. The authors wanted a principled way to combine arbitrary evidence, and the principle they chose (Jaynes, 1957) is maximum entropy.

Worked example · translating "in" (the paper's running example)

Suppose the expert translator always picks one of dans, en, à, au cours de, pendant. With only that constraint, the least-biased model is uniform: 1/5 each. Now add the observed fact \(p(\textit{dans}) + p(\textit{en}) = 3/10\). The maximum-entropy answer spreads mass evenly within each constrained group: \(p(\textit{dans}) = p(\textit{en}) = 3/20\), and the other three get \(7/30\) each. Add a third fact, \(p(\textit{dans}) + p(\textit{à}) = 1/2\), and the groups overlap; there is no longer an obvious answer, which is why the paper needs a general theory and an algorithm.

import numpy as np

words = ["dans", "en", "à", "au cours de", "pendant"]
F = np.array([[1, 1, 0, 0, 0],        # f1 = [y in {dans, en}]
              [1, 0, 1, 0, 0]], float)  # f2 = [y in {dans, à}]
target = np.array([0.3, 0.5])         # the constraints E_p[f] = empirical expectations

lam = np.zeros(2)
for step in range(2000):
    p = np.exp(lam @ F); p /= p.sum()          # exponential-family form
    grad = target - F @ p                      # dual gradient = moment mismatch
    lam += 1.0 * grad
p = np.exp(lam @ F); p /= p.sum()
print({w: round(float(v), 4) for w, v in zip(words, p)})
print("constraints:", (F @ p).round(4), " entropy (bits):", round(float(-(p * np.log2(p)).sum()), 4))

With all three facts: dans 0.186, en 0.114, à 0.314, au cours de and pendant 0.193 each. Both constraints hold exactly, and the two unconstrained options stay equal, as symmetry demands.

The method: from constraints to the exponential form

Definition · the conditional maxent problem

Features \(f_i(x, y)\) are (usually binary) functions of context and outcome. With empirical distribution \(\tilde p(x, y)\), the model's expectation is \(\E_p[f_i] = \sum_{x,y}\tilde p(x)\,p(y \mid x)\,f_i(x, y)\). Maximise the conditional entropy \(H(p) = -\sum_{x}\tilde p(x)\sum_y p(y \mid x)\log p(y \mid x)\) subject to \(\E_p[f_i] = \E_{\tilde p}[f_i]\) for every \(i\), and \(\sum_y p(y \mid x) = 1\) for every \(x\).

Derivation · Lagrange multipliers give \(p_\lambda(y \mid x) = e^{\sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x,y)}/Z_\lambda(x)\)

Form \(\Lambda(p, \lambda, \gamma) = H(p) + \sum_i \lambda_i\big(\E_p[f_i] - \E_{\tilde p}[f_i]\big) + \sum_x \gamma_x\big(\sum_y p(y \mid x) - 1\big)\). Differentiate with respect to one \(p(y \mid x)\):

$$-\tilde p(x)\big(\log p(y \mid x) + 1\big) + \tilde p(x)\sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x, y) + \gamma_x = 0 .$$

So \(\log p(y \mid x) = \sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x, y) + \text{(terms depending only on } x)\). The normalisation constraint fixes those terms: \(p_\lambda(y \mid x) = \exp\big(\sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x, y)\big)/Z_\lambda(x)\) with \(Z_\lambda(x) = \sum_{y'}\exp\big(\sum_i \lambda_i f_i(x, y')\big)\). The maxent solution must be a member of this exponential (log-linear) family; only the \(\lambda\)s remain to be found.

The method: duality, maxent = maximum likelihood

Proof · the dual function is the log-likelihood

Substitute \(p_\lambda\) back into the Lagrangian. Since \(\log p_\lambda = \sum_i\lambda_i f_i - \log Z_\lambda\), the entropy term equals \(-\sum_i \lambda_i \E_{p_\lambda}[f_i] + \sum_x \tilde p(x)\log Z_\lambda(x)\), which cancels the \(\E_{p_\lambda}\) part of the constraint terms, leaving the dual

$$\Psi(\lambda) = \sum_i \lambda_i \E_{\tilde p}[f_i] - \sum_x \tilde p(x)\log Z_\lambda(x).$$

Now compute the conditional log-likelihood of the data under \(p_\lambda\): \(L(\lambda) = \sum_{x,y}\tilde p(x, y)\log p_\lambda(y \mid x) = \sum_i \lambda_i \E_{\tilde p}[f_i] - \sum_x \tilde p(x)\log Z_\lambda(x)\). The two expressions are identical: \(\Psi = L\). Because \(\log Z_\lambda\) is convex in \(\lambda\), \(L\) is concave and its maximiser is unique (up to redundant features). Its gradient is \(\partial L/\partial\lambda_i = \E_{\tilde p}[f_i] - \E_{p_\lambda}[f_i]\), so the maximum-likelihood point is exactly where the constraints hold.

Softmax is the maxent distribution. Take a class label \(y \in \{1, \dots, K\}\), input features \(x_j\), and maxent features \(f_{kj}(x, y) = x_j\,[y = k]\). Then \(\sum_{k,j}\lambda_{kj} f_{kj}(x, y) = \lambda_y^\top x\) and \(p_\lambda(y \mid x) = e^{\lambda_y^\top x}/\sum_k e^{\lambda_k^\top x}\): multinomial logistic regression. Every softmax output layer is a maxent model over the features the network below it computes. In this paper the features are designed by hand, thousands of indicator functions written by linguists; deep learning's contribution is to learn them.

Training, and what the paper adds

The paper trains with improved iterative scaling (IIS), a refinement of Darroch and Ratcliff's generalised iterative scaling (GIS, 1972). GIS requires the features to sum to a constant \(C\) on every example and updates \(\lambda_i \leftarrow \lambda_i + \frac1C\log\frac{\E_{\tilde p}[f_i]}{\E_{p_\lambda}[f_i]}\); IIS drops that requirement by solving a one-dimensional equation per feature. Later work found general-purpose quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) much faster, and today the same model is trained by gradient descent. The paper's other contribution is feature induction: grow the model greedily, at each step adding the candidate feature with the largest estimated gain in log-likelihood.

Evidence

The applications are three components of IBM's Candide French–English translation system: context-sensitive translation of individual words, segmenting sentences into translatable chunks, and word reordering. The authors report that the maxent models improved these components over simpler baselines, and list the features the induction algorithm chose. The evidence is persuasive about practicality; it is not a controlled comparison against other ways of combining features, and NLP took several more years to make maxent the standard tagger and parser model.

Limits & what broke
  • Exact constraints overfit. Matching empirical expectations exactly for a rare feature drives its \(\lambda\) to \(\pm\infty\). The cure is to relax the constraints. Requiring \(|\E_p[f_i] - \E_{\tilde p}[f_i]| \le \beta_i\) adds the penalty \(-\sum_i \beta_i|\lambda_i|\) to the dual (L1 regularisation; Kazama and Tsujii 2003, Dudík, Phillips and Schapire 2004), and a quadratic penalty on the slack gives a Gaussian prior, L2 (Chen and Rosenfeld). Regularisation is constraint relaxation.
  • Hand-designed features. Performance depends on features written by experts, and their number explodes with conjunctions. This is precisely the bottleneck deep learning removed.
  • Local normalisation. Chained maxent taggers normalise per position, which causes the "label bias" problem; conditional random fields (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001) normalise over whole sequences.
  • Slow training. IIS and GIS converge slowly; quasi-Newton methods superseded them.

Bridge

Maxent models predict one label at a time. Speech is a sequence whose hidden states (phonemes) must be inferred jointly. Day 25 is Rabiner's tutorial on hidden Markov models: three problems, three dynamic programs, and EM again for training.

L25 · DAY 25

A Tutorial on Hidden Markov Models and Selected Applications in Speech Recognition

55m videoLevel 2

Rabiner, Proc. IEEE 1989

A hidden Markov model explains a sequence of observations by an unseen chain of discrete states. Rabiner organises everything around three problems (score a sequence, find its best state path, learn the parameters) and solves each with dynamic programming over a lattice: the forward algorithm, Viterbi decoding, and Baum–Welch, which is EM.

Cold open

For roughly a quarter of a century, from the late 1970s until deep networks took over acoustic modelling in the early 2010s, essentially every serious speech recogniser was an HMM. Even the first neural systems kept the HMM and replaced only its emission model. This tutorial is how a generation of engineers learned the machinery, and it is still the clearest account.

The problem

Speech is a sequence of acoustic frames generated by an unobserved sequence of sounds, each lasting a variable number of frames. A model must assign probabilities to whole sequences, recover the hidden sounds, and be trainable from recordings without frame-by-frame labels. Enumerating the \(N^T\) possible state sequences of length \(T\) is hopeless; the Markov structure is what makes all three tasks tractable.

The method: the model and its three problems

Definition · HMM \(\lambda = (A, B, \pi)\)

Hidden states \(q_t \in \{1, \dots, N\}\) with initial distribution \(\pi_i = P(q_1 = i)\) and transitions \(a_{ij} = P(q_{t+1} = j \mid q_t = i)\); observations \(o_t\) with emission probabilities \(b_j(o) = P(o_t = o \mid q_t = j)\). Two assumptions: the state sequence is first-order Markov, and each observation depends only on the current state.

ProblemQuestionAlgorithmSemiring
1. Evaluation\(P(O \mid \lambda)\)?forward (or backward)sum–product
2. Decodingmost likely state sequence?Viterbimax–product
3. Learning\(\arg\max_\lambda P(O \mid \lambda)\)?Baum–Welch (EM)sum–product, twice
Derivation · the forward algorithm

Define \(\alpha_t(i) = P(o_1, \dots, o_t,\ q_t = i \mid \lambda)\). Split on the previous state and use the two assumptions:

$$\alpha_1(i) = \pi_i\, b_i(o_1), \qquad \alpha_{t+1}(j) = \Big[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha_t(i)\, a_{ij}\Big] b_j(o_{t+1}), \qquad P(O \mid \lambda) = \sum_i \alpha_T(i).$$

Cost \(O(N^2 T)\), against about \(2T N^T\) for direct enumeration. The backward variable \(\beta_t(i) = P(o_{t+1}, \dots, o_T \mid q_t = i)\) satisfies the mirror recursion \(\beta_t(i) = \sum_j a_{ij} b_j(o_{t+1})\beta_{t+1}(j)\), with \(\beta_T = 1\).

Algorithm · Viterbi decoding
  1. \(\delta_1(i) = \pi_i b_i(o_1)\).
  2. \(\delta_{t}(j) = \max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\big]\, b_j(o_t)\), storing the arg max as a backpointer \(\psi_t(j)\).
  3. End at \(q_T^* = \arg\max_i \delta_T(i)\) and follow the backpointers to recover \(q_{T-1}^*, \dots, q_1^*\). In practice, work with log probabilities.

It is the forward recursion with the sum replaced by a max: forward–backward is sum–product, Viterbi is max–product, the same dynamic program over two different semirings.

The method: a lattice by hand

Worked example · weather from ice-cream sales

States hot (H) and cold (C); \(\pi = (0.6, 0.4)\); \(a_{HH} = 0.7\), \(a_{HC} = 0.3\), \(a_{CH} = 0.4\), \(a_{CC} = 0.6\). Ice creams eaten per day: if hot, \(P(1, 2, 3) = (0.1, 0.4, 0.5)\); if cold, \((0.7, 0.2, 0.1)\). Observed: 3, 1, 3.

Forward. \(\alpha_1 = (0.6 \cdot 0.5,\ 0.4 \cdot 0.1) = (0.30, 0.04)\). \(\alpha_2(H) = (0.30 \cdot 0.7 + 0.04 \cdot 0.4)\cdot 0.1 = 0.0226\), \(\alpha_2(C) = (0.30 \cdot 0.3 + 0.04 \cdot 0.6)\cdot 0.7 = 0.0798\). Then \(\alpha_3 = (0.02387, 0.00547)\) and \(P(O) \approx 0.0293\).

Viterbi. Replace each sum by a max: \(\delta_2(H) = \max(0.21, 0.016)\cdot 0.1 = 0.021\), \(\delta_2(C) = \max(0.09, 0.024)\cdot 0.7 = 0.063\); \(\delta_3(H) = \max(0.0147, 0.0252)\cdot 0.5 = 0.0126\), from C. The best path is H → C → H, with joint probability 0.0126, about 43% of all the probability mass of the observations.

import numpy as np

states = ["H", "C"]                          # hot, cold
pi = np.array([0.6, 0.4])
A = np.array([[0.7, 0.3],                    # A[i, j] = P(next = j | current = i)
              [0.4, 0.6]])
B = np.array([[0.1, 0.4, 0.5],               # B[i, k] = P(observe k + 1 ice creams | state i)
              [0.7, 0.2, 0.1]])
obs = [2, 0, 2]                              # observed 3, 1, 3 ice creams

def forward(obs):
    """Scaled forward pass: returns log P(obs) without underflow."""
    alpha, logp = pi * B[:, obs[0]], 0.0
    for t in range(len(obs)):
        if t > 0:
            alpha = (alpha @ A) * B[:, obs[t]]     # sum over predecessors
        c = alpha.sum()
        alpha, logp = alpha / c, logp + np.log(c)   # rescale, keep the log of the scale
    return logp

def viterbi(obs):
    delta, back = np.log(pi) + np.log(B[:, obs[0]]), []
    for o in obs[1:]:
        scores = delta[:, None] + np.log(A)          # max over predecessors
        back.append(scores.argmax(0))
        delta = scores.max(0) + np.log(B[:, o])
    path = [int(delta.argmax())]
    for bp in reversed(back):
        path.insert(0, int(bp[path[0]]))
    return [states[s] for s in path], delta.max()

print("P(obs) =", np.exp(forward(obs)).round(6))
path, logp = viterbi(obs)
print("Viterbi path:", path, " P(path, obs) =", np.exp(logp).round(6))

Prints \(P(O) = 0.029336\) and the path H, C, H with probability 0.0126, matching the hand computation.

The scaling problem nobody mentions. \(\alpha_t\) is a product of \(t\) numbers below 1, so for a few hundred frames it underflows double precision. Rabiner's fix is to normalise \(\alpha_t\) at every step by \(c_t = 1/\sum_i \alpha_t(i)\) and recover \(\log P(O \mid \lambda) = -\sum_t \log c_t\); the backward pass uses the same coefficients. (The snippet stores the reciprocal sums instead.) Readers have circulated errata for some of the tutorial's scaling equations, so derive them yourself rather than copying them. Viterbi avoids the issue by working in logs.

The method: Baum–Welch is EM

Derivation · re-estimation as expected counts

E-step (forward–backward). State posteriors \(\gamma_t(i) = \alpha_t(i)\beta_t(i)/P(O \mid \lambda)\), and transition posteriors \(\xi_t(i, j) = \alpha_t(i)\, a_{ij}\, b_j(o_{t+1})\,\beta_{t+1}(j)/P(O \mid \lambda)\).

M-step (normalised expected counts).

$$\bar\pi_i = \gamma_1(i), \qquad \bar a_{ij} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\xi_t(i, j)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\gamma_t(i)}, \qquad \bar b_j(k) = \frac{\sum_{t:\, o_t = k}\gamma_t(j)}{\sum_t \gamma_t(j)} .$$

These are the maximum-likelihood estimates you would compute if the state sequence were observed, with counts replaced by posterior expectations. That is exactly EM's E-step and M-step (Day 19), so the likelihood never decreases. Baum–Welch is EM, not a separate algorithm; it simply predates the general name by several years.

Speech-specific choices. Rabiner contrasts ergodic models (any state reaches any other) with left–right (Bakis) models, where \(a_{ij} = 0\) for \(j < i\), so time only moves forward through a word's sub-sounds. Real acoustic frames are continuous vectors, so emissions become Gaussian mixtures, \(b_j(o) = \sum_m c_{jm}\,\mathcal N(o; \mu_{jm}, \Sigma_{jm})\), re-estimated by the same expected-count logic.

Evidence

As a tutorial, its evidence is pedagogical and practical. It works through the theory with toy examples (coins, urns and balls), then describes isolated-word and connected-digit recognisers built on HMMs at Bell Labs, with qualitative discussion of what helped: left–right topologies, mixture emissions, multiple training sequences, good initialisation. It does not compare HMMs against alternative sequence models; in 1989 there were no serious ones at scale.

Limits & what broke
  • The Markov assumption is the failure mode. All memory must live in one discrete state, so long-range structure needs an exponentially large state space. Recurrent networks (Day 58) carry a continuous, high-dimensional state instead.
  • Frames are not conditionally independent. Consecutive acoustic frames are strongly correlated given the state; HMMs paper over this with delta features.
  • Geometric durations. Staying in a state for \(d\) steps has probability \(a_{ii}^{d-1}(1 - a_{ii})\), always decreasing in \(d\), which suits no real sound; the tutorial discusses explicit-duration variants that cost much more.
  • Generative, maximum-likelihood training does not directly target recognition errors. Discriminative training patched this; neural acoustic models, then CTC (Day 277), which keeps a forward–backward recursion over label alignments but drops the HMM, replaced it.

Bridge

Keep the same chain of hidden state and noisy observation, but make the state continuous and Gaussian, and the forward recursion becomes a pair of matrix equations. That is the Kalman filter, Day 26.

L26 · DAY 26

A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems

50m videoLevel 2

Kalman, J. Basic Eng. 1960

Model a system as a hidden state that evolves linearly with Gaussian noise and is observed linearly with Gaussian noise. Then the best estimate of the state can be updated recursively, one measurement at a time, by two short matrix steps: predict, then correct by a gain that weighs the new measurement against the prediction according to their relative precision.

Cold open

Within a few years of this paper, a version of the filter was navigating spacecraft. Stanley Schmidt's group at NASA Ames extended it to the nonlinear dynamics of a trip to the Moon, and the resulting extended Kalman filter became part of Apollo's navigation. Today the same equations run in every phone's GPS, every drone's attitude estimator and most robots. The paper itself barely mentions applications: it is about the structure of the estimation problem.

The problem

Wiener's filtering theory of the 1940s produced the optimal linear filter for stationary signals, but by solving an integral equation in the frequency domain, for processes that never change their statistics. It did not handle time-varying systems, finite observation windows or multiple outputs gracefully, and it was not recursive. Kalman reformulates the problem in the time domain with a state-space model, which makes the solution a recursion that a computer can run as data arrive.

The method: the model and the two steps

Definition · linear-Gaussian state-space model $$x_k = F x_{k-1} + w_k,\quad w_k \sim \mathcal N(0, Q); \qquad z_k = H x_k + v_k,\quad v_k \sim \mathcal N(0, R).$$

\(x_k\) is the hidden state, \(z_k\) the measurement; \(F\) and \(H\) are the transition and observation matrices; \(Q\) and \(R\) are the process and measurement noise covariances. The filter maintains the posterior mean \(\hat x_k\) and covariance \(P_k\) of \(x_k\) given \(z_1, \dots, z_k\).

Derivation · predict and update as recursive Bayesian estimation

Predict. If \(x_{k-1} \mid z_{1:k-1} \sim \mathcal N(\hat x_{k-1}, P_{k-1})\), then the linear map plus independent noise gives \(x_k \mid z_{1:k-1} \sim \mathcal N(\hat x_k^-, P_k^-)\) with

$$\hat x_k^- = F\hat x_{k-1}, \qquad P_k^- = F P_{k-1} F^\top + Q .$$

Update. Before seeing \(z_k\), the pair \((x_k, z_k)\) is jointly Gaussian with \(\Cov(x_k, z_k) = P_k^- H^\top\) and \(\Var(z_k) = HP_k^-H^\top + R =: S_k\). Condition a joint Gaussian on one block (the formula derived in full on Day 30):

$$\hat x_k = \hat x_k^- + K_k\big(z_k - H\hat x_k^-\big), \qquad K_k = P_k^- H^\top \big(HP_k^-H^\top + R\big)^{-1}, \qquad P_k = (I - K_kH)\,P_k^- .$$

The term \(z_k - H\hat x_k^-\) is the innovation, the part of the measurement the prediction did not anticipate. Because each posterior is again Gaussian, the recursion closes: two moments carry everything the past data say about the state.

Kalman's own derivation does not use Bayes' rule. He treats random variables as vectors in a Hilbert space and the optimal estimate as the orthogonal projection of the state onto the space spanned by the observations; the innovations form an orthogonal sequence, which is what makes the projection recursive. The two derivations agree, and the difference matters for the limits below.

The method: the gain is a precision-weighted average

In one dimension with \(H = 1\), \(K = P^-/(P^- + R)\) and

$$\hat x = \frac{R\,\hat x^- + P^-\,z}{P^- + R} = \frac{\hat x^-/P^- + z/R}{1/P^- + 1/R}, \qquad \frac1P = \frac1{P^-} + \frac1R .$$

The estimate is a weighted average of prediction and measurement, each weighted by its precision (inverse variance), and precisions add. Read \(K = PH^\top(HPH^\top + R)^{-1}\) the same way: trust the measurement in proportion to its precision relative to the prediction's. In the scalar case a perfect sensor (\(R \to 0\)) gives \(K \to 1\) and the estimate jumps to the measurement; a useless one (\(R \to \infty\)) gives \(K \to 0\) and the prediction stands.

import numpy as np

F, H = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0]]), np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])   # position-velocity model
Q, R = 0.01 * np.eye(2), np.array([[4.0]])                          # process and sensor noise

def kalman(zs, x=np.zeros(2), P=10.0 * np.eye(2)):
    gains, xs = [], []
    for z in zs:
        x, P = F @ x, F @ P @ F.T + Q                       # predict
        S = H @ P @ H.T + R                                 # innovation covariance
        K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)                      # Kalman gain
        x = x + K @ (np.atleast_1d(z) - H @ x)              # update the mean
        P = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P                         # update the covariance
        gains.append(K[0, 0]); xs.append(x[0])
    return np.array(gains), np.array(xs), P

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
truth = 0.5 * np.arange(50)                                 # constant velocity 0.5
z1 = truth + rng.normal(0, 2.0, 50)
z2 = 100 + rng.normal(0, 30.0, 50)                          # completely different data
g1, x1, P1 = kalman(z1)
g2, x2, P2 = kalman(z2)
print("gain on position, steps 1, 5, 50:", g1[[0, 4, 49]].round(3))
print("identical gains for unrelated data:", np.allclose(g1, g2), np.allclose(P1, P2))
rms = lambda e: np.sqrt(np.mean(e[10:] ** 2)).round(2)
print("RMS position error after step 10: filter", rms(x1 - truth), " raw sensor", rms(z1 - truth))

The position gain falls from 0.83 to 0.27 as the filter grows confident, and it is identical for a second, unrelated measurement stream. After a short burn-in the filtered position error (RMS 1.29) is well below the raw sensor's (1.92).

The covariance never looks at the data. The recursions for \(P_k^-\), \(K_k\) and \(P_k\) involve only \(F, H, Q, R\) and the initial \(P_0\), never the measurements \(z_k\). The whole gain sequence can be computed offline, before the system is switched on, and for time-invariant, observable systems it converges to a steady state given by an algebraic Riccati equation. This surprised practitioners and made the filter cheap enough to fly. Kalman also notes the duality between this Riccati recursion and that of optimal linear control.

Evidence

The paper is theoretical: a precise problem statement, the derivation by orthogonal projection, the recursive solution, its relation to Wiener's theory, and its duality with control. Its evidence came within the decade, from aerospace navigation and guidance, where the filter worked on real, time-varying systems that Wiener's frequency-domain methods could not handle.

Limits & what broke
  • Optimality is linear-Gaussian only. Under linear dynamics and Gaussian noise, the filter computes the exact posterior and hence the minimum-mean-square-error estimate. Without Gaussianity it is still the best linear estimator, if \(Q\) and \(R\) are the true second moments (that is what the projection derivation proves), but a nonlinear estimator can do better, and multimodal posteriors are lost entirely.
  • Nonlinearity costs. The extended Kalman filter linearises \(f\) and \(h\) with Jacobians at the current estimate and can diverge when that is poor. The unscented Kalman filter (Julier and Uhlmann) propagates a few deterministic sigma points instead; particle filters drop the Gaussian assumption altogether.
  • The model must be known. Wrong \(Q\) or \(R\) gives overconfident or sluggish filters; tuning them is an art, or an EM problem (the model is learnable by EM, with the smoother as E-step).
  • Numerics. Rounding can make \(P_k\) lose symmetry or positive definiteness; the Joseph form and square-root filters exist for this reason.

The same algorithm, three ways. A Kalman filter is an HMM (Day 25) whose hidden state is continuous and Gaussian: the forward algorithm with integrals in place of sums is the filter, and forward–backward is the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother. And if the state is a constant weight vector (\(F = I\), \(Q = 0\)) observed through \(z_k = x_k^\top w + v_k\), the filter is recursive least squares, the second-order upgrade of the LMS rule from Day 6, which replaces \(P_k\) with \(\mu I\).

Bridge

HMMs and Kalman filters are inference on a chain. Day 27 generalises the graph: arbitrary directed acyclic graphs of variables, a rule (d-separation) for reading independence off the graph, and belief propagation, which reduces to forward–backward on a chain.

L27 · DAY 27

Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems (Ch. 3–4, belief propagation)

50m videoLevel 2

Pearl, 1988

A directed acyclic graph over random variables does two jobs at once: it factorises the joint distribution into small local tables, and it encodes which variables are independent given which others, readable directly off the graph by d-separation. On tree-shaped graphs, local message passing (belief propagation) computes every posterior marginal exactly, in time linear in the size of the network.

Cold open

Expert systems of the early 1980s reasoned under uncertainty with ad hoc "certainty factors", because full probability seemed hopeless: a joint distribution over 30 binary variables needs about a billion numbers. Pearl's book argued that the hopelessness was an illusion created by ignoring structure. Write down which variables directly influence which, and the numbers needed shrink to a handful per variable, while inference becomes a matter of passing messages along the graph's edges. The idea earned Pearl the 2011 Turing Award.

The problem

Probability is the right calculus for uncertain reasoning, but naive use of it is exponential in both storage and computation. And human experts do not think in joint tables; they think in terms of dependence ("the alarm depends on burglary and earthquake"). Chapter 3 asks how to represent such qualitative dependence knowledge exactly; Chapter 4 asks how to update beliefs efficiently when evidence arrives.

The method: factorisation

Definition · Bayesian network

A DAG \(G\) on variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) and, for each node, a conditional table \(p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i)\) given its parents. The joint distribution is

$$p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i).$$

This is the chain rule \(p = \prod_i p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1})\) in a topological order, with each conditioning set shrunk to the parents, which is valid exactly when each variable is independent of its other predecessors given its parents. For binary variables the cost drops from \(2^n - 1\) numbers to \(\sum_i 2^{|\mathrm{pa}_i|}\).

The method: d-separation, all three cases

Definition · d-separation

A path between \(X\) and \(Y\) is blocked by a set \(\mathbf Z\) if it contains a node \(M\) such that either (a) \(M\) is the middle of a chain \(\to M \to\) or a fork \(\leftarrow M \to\), and \(M \in \mathbf Z\); or (b) \(M\) is a collider \(\to M \leftarrow\), and neither \(M\) nor any descendant of \(M\) is in \(\mathbf Z\). \(X\) and \(Y\) are d-separated by \(\mathbf Z\) if every path between them is blocked. Then \(X \perp Y \mid \mathbf Z\) in every distribution that factorises over the graph.

The collider, slowly. In the fork and chain, \(Z\) is a channel through which information about \(X\) reaches \(Y\); conditioning on it cuts the channel. In the collider, \(X\) and \(Y\) are two independent causes of a common effect \(Z\). Knowing the effect happened says "at least one cause was active", so learning that one cause was active makes the other less necessary. Conditioning on a common effect induces dependence between its causes. This is explaining away, and it is the same mechanism as selection bias ("collider bias") in observational studies.

Worked example · explaining away, with numbers

Burglary \(B\) and earthquake \(E\) are independent, each with prior 0.01. The alarm \(A\) sounds with probability 0.99 if both occur, 0.9 if exactly one does, and 0.001 otherwise. Hearing the alarm raises belief in a burglary from 0.01 to \(P(B \mid A) \approx 0.48\). Now the radio reports an earthquake: \(P(B \mid A, E) \approx 0.011\), almost back to the prior, because the earthquake explains the alarm. Had the radio reported no earthquake, \(P(B \mid A, \neg E) \approx 0.90\). \(B\) and \(E\) were independent; given \(A\) they are strongly dependent.

Definition · Markov blanket

The Markov blanket of \(X_i\) is its parents, its children, and its children's other parents. Given its blanket, \(X_i\) is independent of every other variable, because the only factors of the joint that mention \(x_i\) are \(p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i)\) and \(p(x_c \mid \mathrm{pa}_c)\) for its children \(c\): \(\;p(x_i \mid \text{rest}) \propto p(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i)\prod_{c} p(x_c \mid \mathrm{pa}_c)\). This is exactly what a Gibbs sampler conditions on (Day 29).

The method: belief propagation on trees

Algorithm · sum-product message passing (tree-structured graph)
  1. Write the joint as a product of node potentials \(\psi_i(x_i)\) (including evidence) and edge potentials \(\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\) (the conditional tables).
  2. Node \(i\) sends neighbour \(j\) the message \(m_{i \to j}(x_j) = \sum_{x_i}\psi_i(x_i)\,\psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)\prod_{k \in N(i)\setminus j} m_{k \to i}(x_i)\), once it has heard from all its other neighbours.
  3. After messages have flowed inwards to a root and back out, every belief \(b_i(x_i) \propto \psi_i(x_i)\prod_{k \in N(i)} m_{k \to i}(x_i)\) is the exact posterior marginal.

Pearl's version on singly connected networks (polytrees) splits messages into \(\pi\) messages (causal support, from parents) and \(\lambda\) messages (diagnostic support, from children). Two passes compute all marginals, at a cost linear in the number of nodes for bounded in-degree. On a chain it is forward–backward (Day 25); with Gaussians it is the Kalman filter and smoother (Day 26). Replacing sums by maxes gives Viterbi.

Loops. With cycles in the underlying graph, messages can circulate and double-count evidence. Pearl noted that the scheme is then not exact. Running it anyway, loopy belief propagation, is a heuristic that may oscillate, yet often gives good approximations; turbo decoding, a major advance in error-correcting codes, was later recognised as loopy BP (McEliece, MacKay and Cheng, 1998).

Evidence

The book's evidence is mathematical: the axioms of conditional independence (graphoids), the soundness of d-separation, and correctness proofs for propagation on trees, illustrated with small diagnostic examples. It also gives the honest bad news. Exact inference in general networks is NP-hard (Cooper, 1990), and computing probabilities is #P-hard; even approximating posteriors to fixed accuracy is NP-hard (Dagum and Luby, 1993). The practical answer was to exploit structure (the junction-tree algorithm, exponential only in treewidth) or to approximate, by sampling or by variational methods.

Limits & what broke
  • Someone has to draw the graph. Learning structure from data is itself hard, and many different DAGs imply the same independencies (Markov equivalence), so data alone often cannot orient edges.
  • Exact inference scales with treewidth. Densely connected networks, like most real-world ones, are out of reach exactly.
  • Arrows are not yet causes. A Bayesian network encodes independence, not intervention. Turning arrows into causal claims required Pearl's later work (the do-calculus, Causality, 2000), with additional assumptions.
  • Loopy BP has few guarantees. It can fail to converge or converge to poor beliefs on strongly coupled loops.

Bridge

When exact inference is intractable, draw samples instead. Day 28 is the 1953 algorithm that samples from a distribution known only up to its normalising constant, which is exactly the situation a large graphical model leaves you in.

L28 · DAY 28

Equation of State Calculations by Fast Computing Machines (Metropolis)

45m videoLevel 2

Metropolis et al., J. Chem. Phys. 1953

To sample from a distribution known only up to its normalising constant, run a Markov chain: propose a small random move and accept it with probability \(\min\big(1, \tilde p(\text{new})/\tilde p(\text{old})\big)\), otherwise stay put. Detailed balance makes the target the chain's stationary distribution, and the unknown normaliser cancels from the ratio. That cancellation is the whole practical value.

Cold open

Los Alamos, early 1950s, on the MANIAC computer. Five authors (Nicholas Metropolis, Arianna and Marshall Rosenbluth, Augusta and Edward Teller) want the equation of state of a system of interacting particles. Statistical mechanics says every average is an integral against the Boltzmann distribution \(e^{-E/kT}/Z\), over hundreds of dimensions, with \(Z\) itself an intractable integral. Later recollections credit the Rosenbluths with much of the actual development; the algorithm carries the first author's name. It became one of the most used algorithms in science.

The problem

Naive Monte Carlo would draw configurations uniformly and weight each by \(e^{-E/kT}\). In a dense system nearly every random configuration has overlapping particles and a negligible weight, so almost all the work is wasted. The paper's reversal: "instead of choosing configurations randomly, then weighting them with \(\exp(-E/kT)\), we choose configurations with a probability \(\exp(-E/kT)\) and weight them evenly." The question is how to draw from a distribution whose normaliser you cannot compute.

The method: Markov chains with the right stationary distribution

Definition · stationarity and detailed balance

A Markov chain with transition kernel \(T(x \to y)\) has stationary distribution \(\pi\) if \(\sum_x \pi(x)T(x \to y) = \pi(y)\) for all \(y\). It satisfies detailed balance with respect to \(\pi\) if \(\pi(x)T(x \to y) = \pi(y)T(y \to x)\) for all \(x, y\): in equilibrium, the probability flow from \(x\) to \(y\) equals the flow back.

Proof · detailed balance implies stationarity

Sum the detailed-balance equation over \(x\): \(\sum_x \pi(x)T(x \to y) = \sum_x \pi(y)T(y \to x) = \pi(y)\sum_x T(y \to x) = \pi(y)\), since the kernel's rows sum to one. The converse fails: detailed balance is sufficient, not necessary. A chain that cycles \(1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1\) deterministically has the uniform distribution as its stationary distribution but no balanced reverse flow; non-reversible samplers exploit this.

Algorithm · Metropolis (symmetric proposal)
  1. From the current state \(x\), propose \(y \sim q(y \mid x)\) with \(q\) symmetric. The paper moves one particle by a uniform random displacement within a small square.
  2. Compute \(r = \tilde p(y)/\tilde p(x)\); for a Boltzmann target \(r = e^{-(E(y) - E(x))/kT}\).
  3. Accept \(y\) with probability \(\min(1, r)\): downhill moves in energy always, uphill moves sometimes.
  4. If rejected, count \(x\) again as the next sample. Dropping repeated states biases every average.
Proof · the Metropolis kernel satisfies detailed balance, and \(Z\) cancels

For \(x \ne y\), \(T(x \to y) = q(y \mid x)\min\big(1, p(y)/p(x)\big)\). Then \(p(x)T(x \to y) = q(y \mid x)\min\big(p(x), p(y)\big)\), which is symmetric in \(x\) and \(y\) because \(q\) is. Only the ratio \(p(y)/p(x) = \tilde p(y)/\tilde p(x)\) is ever used, so the normaliser \(Z = \int \tilde p\) never needs to be known.

Hastings (1970) allowed asymmetric proposals by correcting the ratio: accept with probability \(\min\Big(1, \dfrac{\tilde p(y)\,q(x \mid y)}{\tilde p(x)\,q(y \mid x)}\Big)\). The same symmetry argument goes through. Stationarity alone is not enough for convergence; the chain must also be irreducible and aperiodic, which a proposal with full support and the possibility of rejection usually guarantee.

import numpy as np

def log_target(x):
    """Unnormalised log density: a two-bump mixture. Its normaliser is never needed."""
    return np.logaddexp(-0.5 * (x + 2) ** 2, -0.5 * (x - 2) ** 2)

def metropolis(n, step, x0=0.0, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    x, lp, out, acc = x0, log_target(x0), np.empty(n), 0
    for t in range(n):
        y = x + step * rng.normal()                  # symmetric proposal
        lq = log_target(y)
        if np.log(rng.random()) < lq - lp:          # accept with prob min(1, p(y)/p(x))
            x, lp, acc = y, lq, acc + 1
        out[t] = x                                   # on rejection, repeat the old state
    return out, acc / n

for step in [0.1, 2.5, 50.0]:
    xs, rate = metropolis(20000, step)
    print(f"step {step:5.1f}: acceptance {rate:.2f}, estimate of E[x^2] = {np.mean(xs[2000:] ** 2):.2f} (exact 5)")

Target: equal mixture of \(\mathcal N(\pm2, 1)\), so \(\E[x^2] = 5\). Step 0.1 accepts 97% of moves yet gets 4.18, because it rarely crosses between the modes. Step 2.5 accepts 58% and gets 5.04. Step 50 accepts only 5% but still gets 5.02 here, wastefully.

The method: tuning and diagnosing

The 23.4% folklore. Roberts, Gelman and Gilks (1997) proved that for random-walk Metropolis on targets that are products of many identical independent components, as the dimension grows the optimal proposal scale gives an acceptance rate of about 0.234; for one-dimensional Gaussian targets the optimum is nearer 0.44. Outside those assumptions it is a rule of thumb, not a law. The deeper point is that the step must shrink like \(d^{-1/2}\) in \(d\) dimensions, so random-walk exploration slows with dimension.

Diagnostics. Discard an initial burn-in while the chain forgets its start. Successive samples are correlated, so \(n\) samples carry the information of only about \(n_{\text{eff}} = n/\big(1 + 2\sum_{k \ge 1}\rho_k\big)\) independent ones, where \(\rho_k\) is the lag-\(k\) autocorrelation. Run several chains from dispersed starting points and compare within-chain and between-chain variance: the Gelman–Rubin \(\hat R\) (1992) should be close to 1, and current practice (rank-normalised split-\(\hat R\), Vehtari et al. 2021) asks for below about 1.01. Diagnostics can reveal non-convergence; they can never prove convergence.

HMC in one minute. Hamiltonian (originally "hybrid") Monte Carlo (Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton and Roweth, 1987) adds a momentum variable, simulates Hamiltonian dynamics using the gradient of \(\log\tilde p\) with a leapfrog integrator, and applies a Metropolis accept/reject step to correct the integration error. Proposals travel far along the target's contours yet are accepted with high probability. Its adaptive descendant NUTS is the default sampler in Stan.

Evidence

The paper applies the method to a two-dimensional system of rigid spheres (hard disks), computes its equation of state, and compares the results with approximate theories of the time, a virial expansion and a free-volume theory, finding where each holds. For the method, the evidence is that it produced sensible equilibrium averages in a regime where no other technique could, on a machine of the early 1950s. The paper gives an argument for convergence to the canonical distribution, in the language of ergodic chains, rather than modern mixing-time bounds.

Limits & what broke
  • No finite-time guarantee. Convergence is asymptotic; how many steps are enough depends on the target's geometry and is usually unknown.
  • Multimodality. Well-separated modes may never be crossed (the small-step chain above). Tempering, multiple chains and smarter proposals help; nothing solves it in general.
  • Random walks scale badly with dimension, which is why gradient-based samplers (HMC) took over for continuous models.
  • Correlated samples mean a long chain can be worth very few independent draws; report effective sample sizes, not raw counts.

Bridge

Metropolis proposes a move and sometimes refuses it. Day 29 proposes a new value for one variable drawn from its exact conditional distribution given its Markov blanket, a proposal that is never refused, and uses it to restore noisy images.

L29 · DAY 29

Stochastic Relaxation, Gibbs Distributions, and the Bayesian Restoration of Images

40m videoLevel 2

Geman & Geman, IEEE PAMI 1984

An image prior that is a Markov random field gives a posterior that is again a Gibbs distribution, with local conditionals. Sample it by redrawing one pixel at a time from its exact conditional. This Gibbs sampler is a Metropolis–Hastings move that is never rejected. Then cool the temperature to zero to approach the most probable restoration. A logarithmic schedule provably gets there, but far too slowly to use.

Cold open

The brothers Stuart and Donald Geman named the Gibbs sampler, after the physicist J. Willard Gibbs, and brought MCMC from physics into computer vision. Gelfand and Smith (1990) later took it into mainstream statistics.

The problem

The goal is to recover a clean image \(x\) from a blurred, noisy \(y\). Pixel-wise filters smooth away edges along with the noise. A Bayesian answer needs a prior that knows images are smooth with sharp boundaries. It also needs a way to compute with \(p(x \mid y)\), which ranges over \(2^{4096}\) configurations for a 64×64 binary image. Physics had both: the Ising model and Metropolis sampling (L28). Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi (1983) had just used a cooling Metropolis chain to optimise (simulated annealing), but with no proof that it finds the global optimum.

The method: MRFs and Hammersley–Clifford

Definition · MRF and Gibbs distribution

Each site \(s\) has neighbours \(\partial s\), and the relation is symmetric. A clique is a set of mutual neighbours; on the 4-neighbour grid that means single pixels and adjacent pairs. \(X\) is a Markov random field if \(p > 0\) and \(p(x_s \mid x_{-s}) = p(x_s \mid x_{\partial s})\). A Gibbs distribution is \(p(x) = \frac1Z e^{-U(x)/T}\) with \(U = \sum_C V_C(x_C)\), a sum of clique potentials.

Theorem · Hammersley–Clifford

A strictly positive distribution is an MRF if and only if it is a Gibbs distribution with potentials on the cliques. Hammersley and Clifford proved it in 1971 (unpublished); Besag (1974) published it. Positivity is necessary.

Proof · Gibbs implies Markov, and the conditionals are cheap $$p(x_s \mid x_{-s}) = \frac{p(x)}{\sum_{x_s'} p(x_s', x_{-s})} = \frac{\exp\big(-\sum_{C \ni s} V_C(x_C)/T\big)}{\sum_{x_s'} \exp\big(-\sum_{C \ni s} V_C(x_s', x_{C\setminus s})/T\big)}.$$

Cliques without \(s\) cancel, and so does \(Z\). What remains involves only the neighbours of \(s\). The converse builds the potentials by Möbius inversion of \(\log p\), which needs positivity.

The set \(\partial s\) is the Markov blanket from L27. In a Bayesian network it is the parents, the children and the children's other parents, which are the neighbours in the moral graph.

Worked example · binary denoising

Pixels \(x_s \in \{-1, +1\}\) have the Ising prior \(p(x) \propto \exp\big(\beta \sum_{s \sim t} x_s x_t\big)\). Each pixel is flipped independently with probability \(\varepsilon\), which gives \(p(y_s \mid x_s) \propto e^{\eta x_s y_s}\) with \(\eta = \tfrac12 \log\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\), about 0.87 at \(\varepsilon = 0.15\). The posterior \(\propto \exp\big(\beta \sum x_s x_t + \eta \sum x_s y_s\big)\) is a Gibbs distribution on the same graph. Its conditional is proportional to \(e^{x_s f_s}\), so

$$P(x_s = +1 \mid x_{\partial s}, y_s) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2 f_s}}, \qquad f_s = \beta \sum_{t \in \partial s} x_t + \eta\, y_s.$$

With blur, the posterior is still an MRF, with wider neighbourhoods (practice 2).

The line process. Smoothness priors also blur real edges. The full model adds hidden binary edge elements between adjacent pixels. Switching one on cuts the coupling across it, at a cost, so boundaries come out of the posterior.

The method: the Gibbs sampler

Proof · Gibbs is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance 1

Propose a change to site \(s\) only, drawn from its conditional: \(q(y \mid x) = p(y_s \mid x_{-s})\) with \(y_{-s} = x_{-s}\). The Hastings ratio from L28 is

$$\frac{p(y)\,q(x \mid y)}{p(x)\,q(y \mid x)} = \frac{p(y_s \mid x_{-s})\,p(x_{-s})\;p(x_s \mid x_{-s})}{p(x_s \mid x_{-s})\,p(x_{-s})\;p(y_s \mid x_{-s})} = 1.$$

Each update leaves \(p\) invariant, and so does a sweep. Because \(p > 0\), one sweep can reach any configuration, so the chain converges to \(p\) from any start; this is the paper's relaxation theorem. After burn-in, average samples for marginals, or cool \(T\) to anneal.

The whole catch

Gibbs sampling needs conditionals you can sample exactly. Discrete pixels can be enumerated, and conjugate models give closed forms, which is why Gibbs took over Bayesian statistics. Otherwise you need a Metropolis or slice step inside the sweep. A second catch: under strong coupling, axis-aligned moves crawl, with steps of scale \(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\) for a Gaussian pair with correlation \(\rho\). Update such variables together as a block.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 64
yy, xx = np.mgrid[:n, :n]
disc = (xx - 22) ** 2 + (yy - 30) ** 2 < 15 ** 2
rect = (abs(xx - 46) < 10) & (abs(yy - 32) < 18)
clean = np.where(disc | rect, 1, -1)
noisy = np.where(rng.random((n, n)) < 0.15, -clean, clean)    # flip 15%

beta, eta = 0.9, 1.0       # prior coupling; trust in the data
x = noisy.copy()
avg = np.zeros((n, n))
checker = (xx + yy) % 2
for sweep in range(60):
    for colour in (0, 1):                       # one colour class at a time
        nb = np.zeros((n, n))
        nb[1:] += x[:-1]; nb[:-1] += x[1:]; nb[:, 1:] += x[:, :-1]; nb[:, :-1] += x[:, 1:]
        field = beta * nb + eta * noisy
        p_plus = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-2 * field))   # P(x = +1 | blanket)
        draw = np.where(rng.random((n, n)) < p_plus, 1, -1)
        x = np.where(checker == colour, draw, x)
    if sweep >= 10:
        avg += x                    # after burn-in
print("noisy error:", np.mean(noisy != clean).round(3))
print("posterior-marginal estimate error:", np.mean(np.sign(avg) != clean).round(3))

Prints 0.154, then 0.009. Updating a colour class at once is exact Gibbs sampling. The output, the sign of each pixel's posterior mean, maximises the posterior marginals and so minimises the expected pixel error; it is not the MAP.

The method: annealing

Sample \(p_T \propto e^{-U/T}\), where \(U\) is the negative log posterior. As \(T \to 0\), \(p_T\) concentrates on the global minima of \(U\), the MAP restorations. Cool too fast and the chain freezes in a local minimum.

Theorem · annealing

Suppose every site is visited within each block of some fixed length, \(T(k) \to 0\), and \(T(k) \ge N\Delta/\log k\) for large \(k\), where \(N\) is the number of sites and \(\Delta = \max U - \min U\). Then, from any start, the distribution of \(X(k)\) converges to the uniform distribution on the global minima of \(U\).

Proof idea · why logarithmic, and why useless

At temperature \(T\), a chain escapes a minimum of depth \(d\) at a rate of about \(e^{-d/T}\). With \(T(k) = c/\log k\) that rate is \(k^{-d/c}\), and \(\sum_k k^{-d/c}\) diverges exactly when \(c \ge d\). Hajek (1988) made this sharp: convergence holds if and only if \(c\) is at least the depth of the deepest non-global minimum.

The cost follows from the same formula. Reaching \(T\) takes \(e^{c/T}\) steps, so even with \(c = 1\), \(T = 0.05\) takes about \(5 \times 10^8\) steps, and the theorem's \(N\Delta\) is of order \(10^8\) for a 64×64 image. Practice, including the paper's own experiments, uses much faster schedules that carry no guarantee.

Evidence

The experiments degrade simple, mostly synthetic, piecewise-constant scenes with blur, noise and sometimes a nonlinearity, then restore them by annealing, with and without the line process. The evidence is visual: hidden shapes are recovered, with sharp boundaries when the line process is used. There is no quantitative comparison with other methods, nothing shows that annealing reached the MAP, and the scenes suit the model's assumptions.

Limits & what broke
  • The exact MAP is computable, and it is not always good. Greig, Porteous and Seheult (1989) computed the binary-Ising MAP as a minimum graph cut. They reported that practical annealing stopped short of it, and that the exact MAP over-smooths at strong coupling.
  • Greedy often suffices. Besag's iterated conditional modes (1986) converges in a few sweeps.
  • Slow mixing above the 2D Ising critical coupling, \(\beta_c = \tfrac12\log(1+\sqrt2) \approx 0.44\). Cluster moves (Swendsen and Wang, 1987) help.
  • Synchronous updates are not Gibbs sampling. Updating every pixel at once runs two independent, interleaved chains, because even sites see only odd ones.

Bridge

Hinton (2002) estimates the model expectation in an energy-based model's likelihood gradient with \(k\) steps (often one) of block Gibbs sampling started at the data. Block updates work because an RBM is bipartite: the checkerboard trick again. L30 moves to Gaussian processes, where conditioning is exact and no sampler is needed. ⟵ L27, L28.

L30 · DAY 30

Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning (Ch. 2)

50m videoLevel 3

Rasmussen & Williams, MIT Press 2006

A Gaussian process is a prior over functions under which any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian, with covariances given by a kernel. Regression is then just conditioning a joint Gaussian, which gives the posterior mean and covariance in closed form. The same algebra gives the marginal likelihood, which sets the kernel's hyperparameters without cross-validation.

Cold open

The mathematics is old: geostatisticians have used it as kriging since the 1960s. Williams and Rasmussen (1996) brought it into machine learning, and their 2006 book is now the standard reference.

The problem

Bayesian linear regression gives honest uncertainty, but only once you have chosen the features. With \(f(x) = \phi(x)^\top w\) and \(w \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma_p)\), every prediction depends on the features only through \(k(x, x') = \phi(x)^\top \Sigma_p\, \phi(x')\). That is the kernel trick of L15, made probabilistic. The book therefore drops the weights and puts the prior directly on functions. This function-space view allows infinitely many features, at the price of an \(n \times n\) matrix.

The method: one formula

Definition · Gaussian process

\(f \sim \mathcal{GP}(m, k)\) means that for any inputs \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), the vector \((f(x_1), \dots, f(x_n)) \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf m, K)\) with \(K_{ij} = k(x_i, x_j)\). The kernel \(k\) must be positive semidefinite. Here \(m = 0\).

Theorem · conditioning a joint Gaussian $$\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\left(\begin{bmatrix} \mu_a \\ \mu_b \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} A & C \\ C^\top & B \end{bmatrix}\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; a \mid b \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_a + C B^{-1}(b - \mu_b),\; A - C B^{-1} C^\top\big).$$
Proof · decorrelate, then shift

Let \(z = a - C B^{-1} b\). The pair \((z, b)\) is jointly Gaussian, and \(\Cov(z, b) = C - C B^{-1} B = 0\), so \(z\) is independent of \(b\) and conditioning on \(b\) does not change it. Since \(a = z + C B^{-1} b\), given \(b\) the mean is \(\E z + C B^{-1} b = \mu_a + C B^{-1}(b - \mu_b)\), and the covariance is

$$\Cov z = A - C B^{-1} C^\top - C B^{-1} C^\top + C B^{-1} B B^{-1} C^\top = A - C B^{-1} C^\top.$$
Proof · the GP posterior

Observe \(y = f(X) + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_n^2 I)\), and predict \(f_* = f(X_*)\). Write \(K = k(X, X)\), \(K_* = k(X, X_*)\) and \(K_{**} = k(X_*, X_*)\). Under the prior,

$$\begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal N\left(0, \begin{bmatrix} K + \sigma_n^2 I & K_* \\ K_*^\top & K_{**} \end{bmatrix}\right).$$

The theorem with \(a = f_*\) and \(b = y\) gives

$$\bar f_* = K_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} y, \qquad \Cov(f_*) = K_{**} - K_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} K_*.$$

The mean is \(\sum_i \alpha_i k(x_i, x_*)\) with \(\alpha = (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} y\), which is linear in \(y\). The variance is the prior variance minus a non-negative term.

The surprising part

The posterior covariance never involves \(y\). It depends only on where you measured, the kernel and the noise level. So you can plan measurements before making them. It also means that with fixed hyperparameters a GP does not become less certain when the data surprise it.

The method: kernels and hyperparameters

The general Matérn kernel is \(\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\big(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\big)^{\nu} K_\nu\big(\frac{\sqrt{2\nu}\,r}{\ell}\big)\), where \(K_\nu\) is a modified Bessel function. The smoothness parameter \(\nu\) controls differentiability: the process is \(k\) times mean-square differentiable if and only if \(\nu > k\). As \(\nu \to \infty\) the kernel tends to RBF, which Stein (1999) argued is unrealistically smooth for physical processes. That is why \(\nu = 3/2\) and \(5/2\) are common defaults. The lengthscale \(\ell\) sets how far apart two inputs can be before their values decorrelate, \(\sigma_f\) sets the vertical scale, and \(\sigma_n\) the noise.

Proof · marginal likelihood and its gradient

Integrating out \(f\) adds the two covariances, so \(y \sim \mathcal N(0, K_y)\) with \(K_y = K + \sigma_n^2 I\):

$$\log p(y \mid X, \theta) = \underbrace{-\tfrac12\, y^\top K_y^{-1} y}_{\text{data fit}} \;\underbrace{-\;\tfrac12 \log\lvert K_y\rvert}_{\text{complexity}} \;-\; \tfrac n2 \log 2\pi.$$

With \(d(K^{-1}) = -K^{-1}\,dK\,K^{-1}\), \(d\log\lvert K\rvert = \tr(K^{-1}dK)\) and \(\alpha = K_y^{-1}y\):

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_j}\log p(y \mid X, \theta) = \tfrac12 \tr\Big(\big(\alpha\alpha^\top - K_y^{-1}\big)\frac{\partial K_y}{\partial\theta_j}\Big).$$

This works as an automatic Occam's razor. A tiny lengthscale can fit any dataset, so it spreads its probability thinly over all of them, and the determinant term charges for that. A huge lengthscale cannot bend to fit the data, and the data-fit term charges for that. No held-out set is needed. The surface is non-convex, though: short lengthscale with low noise and long lengthscale with high noise can both explain the same data.

import numpy as np

def rbf(a, b, ell=1.0, sf=1.0):
    return sf**2 * np.exp(-0.5 * (a[:, None] - b[None, :]) ** 2 / ell**2)

def gp(X, y, Xs, ell=1.0, sn=0.1):
    Ky = rbf(X, X, ell) + sn**2 * np.eye(len(X))
    L = np.linalg.cholesky(Ky)                            # the O(n^3) step, done once
    alpha = np.linalg.solve(L.T, np.linalg.solve(L, y))  # Ky^-1 y
    Ks = rbf(X, Xs, ell)
    mean = Ks.T @ alpha
    v = np.linalg.solve(L, Ks)
    var = np.diag(rbf(Xs, Xs, ell)) - np.sum(v**2, axis=0)   # y appears nowhere
    lml = -0.5 * y @ alpha - np.log(np.diag(L)).sum() - 0.5 * len(X) * np.log(2 * np.pi)
    return mean, var, lml

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
X = rng.uniform(-5, 2, 20)                    # no data to the right of x = 2
y = np.sin(X) + 0.1 * rng.normal(size=20)
Xs = np.array([0.0, 3.0, 6.0])
for ell in [0.1, 0.3, 1.0, 3.0, 10.0]:
    m, v, lml = gp(X, y, Xs, ell)
    print(f"ell={ell:4}: log p(y|X)={lml:7.1f}  mean={m.round(2)}  sd={np.sqrt(v).round(2)}")
_, v1, _ = gp(X, y, Xs)
_, v2, _ = gp(X, 5 - 3 * rng.permutation(y), Xs)
print("same variance for different y:", np.allclose(v1, v2))

The log evidence peaks at \(\ell = 1\), the right scale for \(\sin x\). There the sd is 0.06 inside the data and returns to the prior's 1.0 at \(x = 6\). With \(\ell = 10\), the model predicts 4.46 at \(x = 6\) with sd 0.24: it is confidently wrong, and its evidence of −231 says so.

The method: cost, and the seed for later

The Cholesky factorisation costs \(O(n^3)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory, so exact GPs stop being practical somewhere in the tens of thousands of points. Inducing points (\(m \ll n\), \(O(nm^2)\)) include FITC (Snelson and Ghahramani, 2006), Titsias's variational bound (2009) and minibatch SVGP (Hensman et al., 2013). Random Fourier features (Rahimi and Recht, 2007) turn the GP into a finite linear model. For Matérn kernels on time-series inputs, Kalman smoothing (L26) gives the exact posterior in \(O(n)\).

Infinite-width networks are GPs. Neal (1996) showed that a one-hidden-layer network with i.i.d. random weights becomes a GP as its width \(H \to \infty\), provided the output-weight variance is scaled as \(1/H\). Each output is a sum of \(H\) i.i.d. terms, so by the central limit theorem any finite set of outputs becomes jointly Gaussian. Lee et al. and Matthews et al. (2018) extended it to deep nets. What training does in that limit is the neural tangent kernel (L318).

Evidence

Chapter 2 is a textbook argument, so its evidence is illustrative: prior and posterior samples, the effect of each hyperparameter, and a robot-arm inverse-dynamics regression. The wider record is strong where data are scarce and uncertainty matters, as in geostatistics, emulators for expensive simulations, and Bayesian optimisation (Snoek, Larochelle and Adams, 2012). It does not show calibration under a wrong kernel.

Limits & what broke
  • Closed form needs a Gaussian likelihood. Classification needs Laplace or EP approximations (Ch. 3).
  • The error bars are only as good as the kernel. They assume stationarity and constant noise, and they can be confidently wrong, as with \(\ell = 10\) above.
  • High dimensions. Distance-based kernels weaken, and one lengthscale per input (ARD) adds hyperparameters that the evidence can overfit.
  • No feature learning. The kernel is fixed in advance, which is why learned representations took over in perception.

Bridge
