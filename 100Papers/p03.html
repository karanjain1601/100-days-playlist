Computer Vision II: Dense Prediction, 3D, and the Transformer Takeover

Fully Convolutional Networks for Semantic Segmentation

40m videoLevel 2

Long, Shelhamer & Darrell, CVPR 2015 (arXiv:1411.4038)

A classification network is already a pixel labeller in disguise: rewrite its fully connected layers as convolutions, add learned upsampling and skip connections, and train end to end with a per-pixel loss.

Cold open

Feed a 500×500 photo of a cat into an ImageNet classifier whose fully connected layers have been reshaped into convolutions. Instead of one vector of 1000 scores you get a grid of them, and the “tabby cat” channel lights up exactly where the cat is. Nothing was retrained. That heatmap, the paper's Figure 2, is the whole idea in one picture.

The problem

Before FCN, semantic segmentation with CNNs meant classifying patches or region proposals one at a time (sliding windows, superpixels, the R-CNN-style SDS pipeline of L82), then stitching and post-processing. That was slow, recomputed shared features for overlapping patches, and was rarely trained end to end. The ImageNet backbones (AlexNet, VGG, GoogLeNet) were sitting there with excellent features, but their final layers demanded a fixed input size and threw away all spatial layout.

The method

Convolutionalization. In VGG-16, fc6 reads the 7×7×512 output of pool5, flattened to 25,088 numbers, and produces 4096. Its weight matrix has shape 4096 × 25,088. Reshape it to 4096 filters of size 7×7×512 and you have a convolution that gives the same answer on a 7×7 input and slides over anything larger. fc7 (4096 → 4096) and the classifier become 1×1 convolutions; FCN swaps the 1000-way classifier for a 21-way scoring layer (20 PASCAL classes plus background).

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C, k, n_out = 8, 3, 5                        # channels, spatial size fc6 expects, outputs
W_fc = rng.normal(size=(n_out, C * k * k))   # an FC layer on a flattened C x k x k map
x = rng.normal(size=(C, k, k))
y_fc = W_fc @ x.reshape(-1)

W_conv = W_fc.reshape(n_out, C, k, k)        # same numbers, viewed as n_out filters of C x k x k

def conv_valid(x, W):                        # stride-1 'valid' convolution (correlation)
    n, _, k, _ = W.shape
    H, Wd = x.shape[1] - k + 1, x.shape[2] - k + 1
    y = np.empty((n, H, Wd))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(Wd):
            y[:, i, j] = np.tensordot(W, x[:, i:i + k, j:j + k], axes=3)
    return y

assert np.allclose(conv_valid(x, W_conv)[:, 0, 0], y_fc)   # identical on the original size
big = rng.normal(size=(C, 7, 9))                            # a larger input
print(conv_valid(big, W_conv).shape)          # (5, 5, 7): one FC output per window = a heatmap

The reshape is the key move: no new parameters, any input size, and the net computes all overlapping windows at once with shared work.

Upsampling. Five stride-2 poolings leave VGG's score map at 1/32 of the input. FCN brings it back with transposed convolutions (the paper calls them deconvolution, or backwards-strided convolution), initialised to bilinear interpolation; the paper fixes the final one to bilinear and lets the intermediate ones learn.

Definition · transposed convolution

Write a strided convolution as a sparse matrix, \(y = Wx\) with \(W \in \R^{m \times n}\), \(m < n\). A transposed convolution with the same kernel computes \(\hat x = W^\top y\): it maps the small grid back to the large one and is exactly the backward pass of the convolution with respect to its input. It is not an inverse: \(W^\top W \ne I\) in general, so \(W^\top W x\) is a blurred, reweighted version of \(x\).

Each input cell “stamps” a scaled copy of the kernel onto the output, at stride \(s\). With kernel size 3 and stride 2, input \(i\) covers outputs \(2i, 2i{+}1, 2i{+}2\), so even positions receive two stamps and odd positions one. That uneven overlap is the checkerboard artifact analysed by Odena, Dumoulin & Olah (2016). Kernel 4, stride 2 gives every interior output exactly two stamps; resize-then-convolve avoids the issue altogether.

Skips: combining what and where. Deep layers know what is in the image but only at 1/32 resolution; shallow layers know where edges are but not what they belong to. FCN adds 1×1 scoring layers on pool4 and pool3 and fuses by summation:

Loss and metric. Training minimises per-pixel softmax cross-entropy over the valid pixels \(\Omega\) (VOC's “void” border pixels are ignored), \(\mathcal L = -\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{p \in \Omega} \log \operatorname{softmax}(s_p)_{y_p}\). Evaluation uses the paper's definitions: let \(n_{ij}\) count pixels of true class \(i\) predicted as \(j\), accumulated over the whole test set, and \(t_i = \sum_j n_{ij}\). Then

$$\text{pixel acc} = \frac{\sum_i n_{ii}}{\sum_i t_i}, \qquad \text{mean IU} = \frac{1}{n_{cl}} \sum_i \frac{n_{ii}}{t_i + \sum_j n_{ji} - n_{ii}}.$$

The denominator of each IU term is \(\text{TP} + \text{FN} + \text{FP}\) for class \(i\): every pixel that is class \(i\) in either the truth or the prediction.

Worked example · why pixel accuracy lies

A dataset is 90% background, 8% person, 2% bicycle. A model that predicts “background” everywhere has pixel accuracy 0.90. Its IUs are background \(0.90/1.00 = 0.90\), person 0, bicycle 0, so mean IU \(= 0.30\). Pixel accuracy rewards the majority class; mean IU weights every class equally and punishes both missed and hallucinated pixels.

Evidence

The abstract reports a 20% relative improvement over the previous best on PASCAL VOC 2012, to 62.2% mean IU, with inference under a fifth of a second per typical image, plus state-of-the-art results on NYUDv2 and SIFT Flow. The skip ablation on their VOC 2011 validation subset is the argument for the architecture: mean IU rises by roughly three points from FCN-32s to FCN-16s, and by only a fraction of a point more for FCN-8s, while the outputs get visibly sharper. Pixel accuracy changes much less than mean IU across the three, a direct illustration of the worked example. They also compare backbones (AlexNet, GoogLeNet, VGG-16), with VGG-16 clearly best, and argue that whole-image training is as effective as, and faster than, sampling patches.

Limits & what broke
  • Still coarse. Stride 8 is the finest prediction; thin structures and boundaries blur. DeepLab (L93) attacks this with atrous convolution and a CRF; U-Net (L92) with a full decoder.
  • No global context. The effective receptive field is much smaller than the theoretical one, so FCN mislabels objects whose identity depends on the scene. PSPNet (L95) builds its paper around these failures.
  • Staged training. The paper trains 32s, then 16s, then 8s, each initialised from the previous one. The extended journal version (Shelhamer, Long & Darrell, TPAMI 2017) reports that training the skips all at once works as well.
  • Checkerboards. Learned transposed convolutions can produce periodic artifacts; bilinear initialisation hides much of it here, but the problem resurfaced in generative models.

Bridge

FCN's decoder is a few upsampling steps and two skip sums. The mask branch of Mask R-CNN (⟵ L89) is a small FCN of exactly this kind, and the backbone is the VGG of L64. Tomorrow, U-Net asks what happens if the decoder is as deep as the encoder and the skips concatenate instead of add.

U-Net: Convolutional Networks for Biomedical Image Segmentation

40m videoLevel 2

Ronneberger, Fischer & Brox, MICCAI 2015 (arXiv:1505.04597)

Make the decoder as deep as the encoder and concatenate encoder features into it at every scale, and a segmentation network can be trained to precise boundaries from a few dozen annotated images.

Cold open

The U-shaped diagram in this paper's Figure 1 may be the most reproduced architecture drawing in machine learning. It was designed for microscopy, trained on 30 images, and it now sits, lightly modified, inside the first generation of diffusion image generators.

The problem

Biomedical segmentation has two features that broke the methods of 2014. Labels are scarce (an expert annotates each cell boundary by hand), and the output must be precise at the pixel level, including the one-pixel gaps between touching cells. The best electron-microscopy method of the time, Ciresan et al. (2012), classified each pixel from a sliding patch around it: slow, redundant, and forced to trade context (large patches, more pooling) against localisation (small patches). FCN (L91) had shown that whole-image convolutional prediction works, but its decoder was a couple of upsampling steps.

The method

Contracting path. Repeated blocks of two unpadded 3×3 convolutions with ReLU, then 2×2 max pooling with stride 2; channels double at every downsampling (64, 128, 256, 512, 1024). Expanding path. A 2×2 “up-convolution” that halves the channels, concatenation with the correspondingly cropped encoder map, then two 3×3 convolutions. A final 1×1 convolution maps 64 channels to the classes. There are 23 convolutional layers and no fully connected ones.

Concatenation, not addition (the DenseNet choice, ⟵ L70). A convolution applied to \([a; b]\) with weights \([W_1\ W_2]\) computes \(W_1 a + W_2 b\); FCN's sum is the special case \(W_1 = W_2\). Concatenation lets the decoder learn how much of the encoder's high-frequency detail to use and in which channels, at the price of more channels. That detail is exactly what the 1/16-resolution bottleneck has destroyed: the skips carry edges and textures; the bottleneck carries context.

Valid padding and overlap-tile. Because nothing is padded, each output pixel has its full context inside the input, and the output is smaller than the input. Large images are processed as overlapping tiles: to predict the 388² centre, feed the surrounding 572² region; at the image border, extrapolate the missing context by mirroring. Tile sizes are chosen so every 2×2 pooling sees an even-sized map. The result is seamless predictions for arbitrarily large images with bounded GPU memory.

Derivation · the border weight map

The loss is pixel-wise softmax cross-entropy, weighted per pixel: \(\mathcal L = -\sum_x w(x) \log p_{\ell(x)}(x)\), with

$$w(x) = w_c(x) + w_0 \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\big(d_1(x) + d_2(x)\big)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big),$$

where \(w_c\) balances class frequencies, \(d_1\) is the distance to the border of the nearest cell and \(d_2\) to the second nearest. The paper uses \(w_0 = 10\), \(\sigma \approx 5\) pixels. A background pixel in a two-pixel gap between touching cells has \(d_1 = d_2 = 1\): the exponent is \(-4/50\), so \(w \approx w_c + 9.2\). A pixel 10 and 15 pixels from two cells has exponent \(-625/50\), so \(w \approx w_c\). The thin separating lines, which are a tiny fraction of pixels but decide whether two cells merge, get roughly ten times the weight. The weights are precomputed from the ground truth.

Thirty images. The ISBI 2012 electron-microscopy set has 30 training images of 512×512. The enabler is augmentation by random elastic deformations: displacement vectors on a coarse 3×3 grid, drawn from a Gaussian with 10-pixel standard deviation, interpolated bicubically to every pixel. Tissue deforms like this, so the augmentation teaches a real invariance. Training uses a batch of one large tile, momentum 0.99, dropout at the end of the contracting path, and Gaussian initialisation with standard deviation \(\sqrt{2/N}\) for \(N\) incoming connections (He-style).

Evidence

On the ISBI 2012 EM segmentation challenge the authors report the best warping error on the leaderboard at the time, beating the sliding-window network of Ciresan et al. On the ISBI 2015 cell-tracking challenge they report IoU of about 92% on PhC-U373 against about 83% for the runner-up, and about 77% on DIC-HeLa against about 46%. A 512×512 image is segmented in under a second on a 2015 GPU. What the evidence does not include is an ablation of each ingredient (weight map, elastic augmentation, depth); the paper shows that the package wins, not which part matters most.

Limits & what broke
  • Valid padding is mostly abandoned. Nearly every reimplementation uses “same” padding so output size equals input size, accepting slightly worse border context for simpler code.
  • Memory. Full-resolution encoder maps must be kept for the skips; 3D U-Nets on volumes hit GPU limits quickly.
  • Variants rarely beat the original. nnU-Net (Isensee et al., Nature Methods 2021) showed that a carefully self-configured plain U-Net beats many architectural modifications across medical benchmarks: the baseline was under-tuned, not under-designed.
  • The diffusion backbone, for a while. DDPM (L260) and latent diffusion (L264) use U-Nets with residual blocks, attention and timestep embeddings. Transformer backbones (DiT, Peebles & Xie 2023) have since displaced it in many large systems.

Bridge

Say the diffusion point loudly: a denoiser must output an image the same size as its input, know what the whole image depicts, and get every pixel's noise right. That is the U-Net's job description, with skips for detail and a bottleneck for context (⟶ L260). Tomorrow takes the opposite route to resolution: DeepLab refuses to downsample in the first place.

DeepLab: Atrous Convolution and Fully Connected CRFs

40m videoLevel 2

Chen, Papandreou, Kokkinos, Murphy & Yuille, TPAMI 2018 (arXiv:1606.00915)

Instead of downsampling and then learning to upsample, stop downsampling: atrous (dilated) convolution keeps feature maps dense while reusing ImageNet filters unchanged, ASPP adds context at several scales, and a fully connected CRF snaps the result to image edges.

Cold open

FCN outputs look like they were painted with a thick brush. DeepLab's outputs, after the CRF, follow the outline of a horse's legs. The interesting part is how little of that came from new learning: most of it came from not throwing resolution away and from a 2011 graphical-model trick bolted on at the end.

The problem

The paper names three problems with using classification networks for segmentation, and gives each a fix:

ProblemCauseDeepLab's fix
Reduced feature resolutionrepeated pooling and striding (stride 32)atrous convolution, output stride 8
Objects at multiple scalesone fixed receptive field per layeratrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP)
Poor localisationinvariance built in by poolingfully connected CRF post-processing

The method: atrous convolution

Definition · atrous convolution

In 1D, with filter \(w\) of length \(K\) and rate \(r\): \(y[i] = \sum_{k=1}^{K} x[i + r \cdot k]\, w[k]\). Rate 1 is ordinary convolution. Rate \(r\) inserts \(r-1\) “holes” between taps, so a \(k \times k\) filter covers \(k + (k-1)(r-1)\) pixels per side with the same \(k^2\) parameters and the same cost. The name is from the algorithme à trous of wavelet analysis; Yu & Koltun's “dilated convolution” (⟵ L80) is the same operation.

Derivation · why pretrained weights still fit

Take a pretrained network with a stride-2 layer followed by a filter \(w\). Remove the stride. The next layer now sees a map twice as dense, and the old filter would cover half the original image extent, computing a different function. Dilate it by 2 instead: at even positions, \(y_{\text{dense}}[2i] = \sum_k w[k]\, x[2i + 2k] = \sum_k w[k]\, x_{\text{sub}}[i + k] = y_{\text{coarse}}[i]\). The dense network reproduces every output of the original and fills in the positions between them. Repeat for each removed stride, doubling the rate each time, and an ImageNet network computes its original features at 8× (or 4×) the density, with no retraining.

import numpy as np

def conv1d(x, w, rate=1):                    # 'valid' correlation with dilation
    k, span = len(w), (len(w) - 1) * rate + 1
    return np.array([sum(w[j] * x[i + j * rate] for j in range(k))
                     for i in range(len(x) - span + 1)])

x = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=40)
w = np.array([1.0, -2.0, 0.5])               # a "pretrained" filter

coarse = conv1d(x[::2], w)                   # original net: stride-2 layer, then the filter
dense = conv1d(x, w, rate=2)                 # DeepLab: drop the stride, dilate the filter
assert np.allclose(dense[::2], coarse)       # same outputs at the old positions ...
print(len(coarse), len(dense))               # ... plus the ones in between: 18 vs 36

That reuse is the practical argument. VOC has about ten thousand training images; you cannot learn a dense segmentation backbone from scratch on that, but you can fine-tune an ImageNet one whose features you have not disturbed. In VGG-16, DeepLab skips subsampling in the last two pooling layers and dilates the following filters by 2 and 4; the converted fc6 becomes a 3×3 atrous convolution. The final score map, at stride 8, is upsampled by plain bilinear interpolation.

The method: ASPP and the CRF

The fully connected CRF (Krähenbühl & Koltun 2011) treats each pixel's label \(x_i\) as a variable with energy

$$E(x) = \sum_i -\log P(x_i) + \sum_{i<j} \mu(x_i, x_j)\Big[w_1 e^{-\frac{\|p_i - p_j\|^2}{2\sigma_\alpha^2} - \frac{\|I_i - I_j\|^2}{2\sigma_\beta^2}} + w_2 e^{-\frac{\|p_i - p_j\|^2}{2\sigma_\gamma^2}}\Big],$$

where \(P\) is the network's softmax, \(\mu\) is the Potts penalty (1 if the labels differ), \(p\) are positions and \(I\) colours. The first kernel says nearby pixels of similar colour should share a label; the second is a plain smoothness term. Every pair of pixels is connected, which sounds quadratic, but mean-field inference only needs \(\sum_j k(f_i, f_j) Q_j\): a Gaussian filter in (position, colour) space, which high-dimensional filtering computes in linear time. About ten mean-field iterations suffice. The CRF's parameters are set by cross-validation, not learned with the network.

Evidence

The full system (ResNet-101, ASPP, multi-scale inputs, COCO pretraining, CRF) reaches 79.7% mean IoU on the PASCAL VOC 2012 test set, by the authors' report, with strong results on PASCAL-Context, PASCAL-Person-Part and Cityscapes. The ablations go the way the table above predicts: atrous convolution plus a large field of view improves over the FCN-style baseline, ASPP improves over a single rate, and the CRF adds a consistent gain concentrated near object boundaries. They also show that a 3×3 filter with rate 12 matches a much larger, slower converted fc6.

Output stride is the deployment knob

Going from output stride 16 to 8 quadruples the spatial area of the last stages, so their memory and FLOPs grow about 4×. DeepLabv3 trains at stride 16 and evaluates at stride 8 to get most of the accuracy for less training cost. If you deploy any segmentation network, this is the first number to check.

Limits & what broke
  • The CRF was dropped. DeepLabv3 (Chen et al. 2017) removes it. With batch-normalised ASPP, image-level features and better backbones, the network's own boundaries improved enough that a non-learned post-process was no longer worth its cost. CRF-as-RNN (Zheng et al. 2015) had already made it trainable end to end.
  • Large rates degenerate. As the rate approaches the feature-map size, most taps of a 3×3 filter land in padding and it acts like a 1×1 filter. DeepLabv3 adds a global image-pooling branch to ASPP for this reason.
  • Gridding. Stacking layers with the same rate samples a sparse lattice of inputs and can leave grid artifacts; hybrid rates such as 1, 2, 5 (Wang et al. 2018) avoid it.
  • Memory. Dense features at stride 8 in the deepest, widest layers are expensive, which is exactly what tomorrow's decoder trades away.

Bridge

There are now two segmentation families: encoder–decoders that recover resolution (FCN, U-Net, L92) and dilation-based networks that never lose it. DeepLabv3+ (L94) merges them: an atrous encoder at stride 16, plus a deliberately small decoder.

Encoder-Decoder with Atrous Separable Convolution (DeepLabv3+)

30m videoLevel 2

Chen, Zhu, Papandreou, Schroff & Adam, ECCV 2018 (arXiv:1802.02611)

An atrous-pyramid encoder supplies context and a deliberately tiny decoder supplies boundaries; making every convolution depthwise-separable pays for both.

Cold open

The decoder in this paper is one 1×1 convolution, one concatenation and two 3×3 convolutions. The authors tried bigger ones. That they did not need them is a result, and the reason the model became a default baseline in open-source segmentation toolkits for several years.

The problem

By 2017 segmentation had split into two families. Spatial pyramid pooling networks (DeepLab, L93; PSPNet, L95) encode rich multi-scale context, but keeping features at stride 8 through a deep backbone is expensive, and at stride 16 the boundaries are blurry. Encoder–decoders (U-Net, L92; SegNet) recover sharp boundaries from shallow features but have weaker context. DeepLabv3 had just improved the first family: ASPP with batch normalisation, rates {6, 12, 18} at output stride 16, plus a 1×1 branch and a global image-pooling branch, and no CRF. Its output still had to be upsampled 16× in one step.

The method

The 48-channel detail. The shallow stride-4 features (e.g. ResNet's Conv2 block) have 256 or more channels; the encoder output has 256. Concatenate them unprojected and the decoder sees as many low-level as high-level channels, and the low-level ones, being numerous and noisy, swamp the semantic signal. A 1×1 convolution reduces them first. The ablation tries 8, 16, 32, 48 and 64 channels and 48 works best; it also finds two 3×3 convolutions with 256 filters to be the best refinement, and adding a second, even shallower skip gave no further gain.

Derivation · cost of depthwise-separable atrous convolution

A standard \(k \times k\) convolution from \(C_{\text{in}}\) to \(C_{\text{out}}\) channels on an \(H \times W\) map costs \(k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} HW\) multiply–adds. Factor it (⟵ L73) into a depthwise \(k \times k\) filter per input channel, \(k^2 C_{\text{in}} HW\), and a pointwise 1×1 mix, \(C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}} HW\). The ratio is

$$\frac{k^2 C_{\text{in}} + C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}}{k^2 C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}} = \frac{1}{C_{\text{out}}} + \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

For \(k = 3\) and 256 channels in and out: \(589{,}824\) versus \(67{,}840\) per position, about 8.7× cheaper. Dilation applies to the depthwise filter only, so the saving is the same at any rate. Applied to ASPP and the decoder, this is what lets a heavy multi-branch head run at stride 16 without the cost of the original DeepLab.

Modified (aligned) Xception backbone. Starting from the version of Dai et al. (2017), the authors make it deeper, replace every max-pooling with a strided depthwise-separable convolution (so any stage can be switched to atrous convolution and features extracted at any output stride), and add batch normalisation and ReLU after each 3×3 depthwise convolution, as in MobileNet.

Evidence

The abstract reports 89.0% mean IoU on the PASCAL VOC 2012 test set and 82.1% on Cityscapes test, without any post-processing. The VOC figure uses an Xception backbone pretrained on COCO and Google's internal JFT-300M, so it is not reproducible from public data; the ablations on VOC val are the more useful evidence. They show that the decoder improves mean IoU and, more clearly, accuracy in a narrow band around object boundaries; that an encoder at output stride 16 with the decoder is a better accuracy-per-FLOP point than stride 8 without it; and that separable convolutions in ASPP and the decoder cut computation substantially with little or no loss.

Limits & what broke
  • Headline needs private data. 89.0% depends on JFT pretraining. Compare methods against the paper's public-data rows and the val-set ablations, not the leaderboard headline.
  • Still stride 4. The last step is bilinear 4× upsampling, so hair, wires and thin poles remain soft. Minimal decoders are a choice with a ceiling.
  • Separable convolutions are cheap in FLOPs, not always in time. Depthwise kernels have low arithmetic intensity and often run far below peak on GPUs; measured latency improves less than the 8.7× above.
  • Superseded by mask classification and transformers. SegFormer (2021) and Mask2Former (2022) overtook it on ADE20K and Cityscapes; DeepLabv3+ remains the honest baseline new methods should beat.

Bridge

DeepLabv3+ settles the architecture question for a while: context module plus light decoder. Tomorrow goes back a year to the context module DeepLab was competing with, PSPNet, which pools instead of dilating, and to its method of designing a module from a list of failures.

Pyramid Scene Parsing Network (PSPNet)

25m videoLevel 2

Zhao, Shi, Qi, Wang & Jia, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1612.01105)

Look at how a strong baseline fails, name the failure types, and design the module that fixes them: here, a pyramid of pooled global context concatenated onto every pixel's features.

Cold open

An FCN looks at a boat on a river and labels it “car”. Locally, the shape is plausible. Globally it is absurd: cars do not float. PSPNet's argument is that the network never saw the river while deciding about the boat, and its fix is to hand every pixel a summary of the whole scene.

The problem: an error taxonomy

The paper opens not with an architecture but with an analysis of FCN's mistakes on ADE20K, a scene-parsing dataset with 150 classes. It sorts them into three patterns:

  • Mismatched relationship. The prediction contradicts co-occurrence: a car on water, where context says “boat”.
  • Confusion categories. Labels that are hard to tell apart locally (field vs earth, mountain vs hill, building vs skyscraper) get mixed within one object, so half a building is “skyscraper”. A model that saw the whole object would pick one.
  • Inconspicuous classes. Small things (streetlights, signboards) vanish, and objects larger than the receptive field are broken up; the paper's example is a pillow whose appearance matches the bed sheet around it.

All three point to the same missing ingredient: a global, scene-level prior. Why don't deep networks already have one? Their theoretical receptive field covers the image, but the paper cites evidence (Zhou et al. 2015) that the empirical receptive field is much smaller, especially at high layers. Global average pooling, as in ParseNet, is the obvious fix, but squashing the scene into one vector discards where things are.

The methodology is the lesson

This is how to write a paper, and how to debug any model: inspect failures of a strong baseline, cluster them into named types, propose a mechanism aimed at those types, then show the same examples fixed. It turns “we added a module and the number went up” into a falsifiable claim about what the module does.

The method: pyramid pooling

Every pixel's final feature now holds its local feature plus four summaries of the scene at different granularities: the whole image, quadrants, ninths, and a 6×6 layout. The boat pixels can see that the bottom of the image is water. Conceptually this is ASPP's job (L93) done by pooling rather than dilation: ASPP samples context sparsely at fixed offsets from each pixel; PPM averages it densely over fixed image regions.

Definition · the auxiliary loss

For a ResNet backbone, a second classifier is attached after res4b22 with its own per-pixel cross-entropy, and the total loss is \(\mathcal L = \mathcal L_{\text{main}} + \alpha\, \mathcal L_{\text{aux}}\) with \(\alpha = 0.4\). The auxiliary branch is discarded at test time. It gives the lower half of a very deep network a direct gradient signal, so both halves learn useful features.

Evidence

PSPNet won the ImageNet Scene Parsing Challenge 2016 and the abstract reports 85.4% mean IoU on PASCAL VOC 2012 (with COCO pretraining) and 80.2% on Cityscapes. The ablations on ADE20K are the evidence for the design: average pooling beats max pooling, the four-level pyramid beats the single global bin, and reducing channels before concatenation helps. The auxiliary loss improves the ResNet baseline clearly, with 0.4 the best of the weights they tried. Unlike GoogLeNet's auxiliary classifiers (⟵ L65), whose benefit was small and later reinterpreted as mild regularisation, this deep supervision is load-bearing. The qualitative figure revisits the three failure types and shows them corrected, which closes the loop the introduction opened.

Limits & what broke
  • Fixed bins. The pooled regions are a rigid grid, the same for every image. The 2×2 quadrant containing a boat mixes water with sky. Later context modules make the aggregation content-dependent: non-local blocks, DANet, OCRNet, and eventually transformer segmenters.
  • Expensive. Dilated ResNet-101 at output stride 8 is heavy; DeepLabv3+ (L94) gets similar context at stride 16 with a light decoder.
  • Taxonomy without counts. The failure types are presented largely through examples. A stronger version of the method would measure how often each type occurs before and after the fix.

Bridge

Semantic segmentation labels pixels but cannot count: two touching people are one “person” region. Instance segmentation (Mask R-CNN, ⟵ L89) counts objects but ignores sky and road. Tomorrow's paper defines a task, and a metric, that demands both at once.

Panoptic Segmentation

30m videoLevel 2

Kirillov, He, Girshick, Rother & Dollár, CVPR 2019 (arXiv:1801.00868)

Unify semantic and instance segmentation into one task where every pixel gets a class and an instance id with no overlaps, and measure it with a single metric, Panoptic Quality, that factors cleanly into segmentation quality times recognition quality.

Cold open

This paper contains no new network. Its contributions are a task definition, a metric with a small proof attached, and a human-consistency study. It reshaped the field anyway: within two years panoptic segmentation was a standard COCO, Cityscapes and Mapillary benchmark. Task and metric design is rarer content than architecture, and arguably harder.

The problem: things and stuff

The distinction is old (Adelson 2001): things are countable objects with instances (people, cars); stuff is amorphous, uncountable material (sky, grass, road). The field had split along this line. Semantic segmentation (L91–L95) labels every pixel but cannot separate two touching people; it is scored with mean IoU. Instance segmentation (Mask R-CNN, ⟵ L89) detects things as possibly overlapping, scored masks and ignores stuff; it is scored with AP, which needs confidence rankings. No metric scored a full scene description.

Definition · the panoptic task

Given a label set \(\mathcal C = \mathcal C^{\text{St}} \cup \mathcal C^{\text{Th}}\), map every pixel \(i\) to a pair \((l_i, z_i)\): a semantic class and an instance id. Pixels with the same pair form one segment. For stuff classes the id is ignored, so all sky is one segment. Ambiguous or out-of-vocabulary pixels may get a void label. Crucially, each pixel gets exactly one label: segments do not overlap, and there are no confidence scores.

The non-overlap constraint is what makes this hard for instance-segmentation methods. Mask R-CNN emits a ranked list of overlapping masks and lets AP sort it out; a panoptic output must commit, resolving every overlap between two detections and between a detection and the stuff underneath it.

The method: Panoptic Quality

Theorem · unique matching

If predicted segments are mutually non-overlapping and so are ground-truth segments, then each ground-truth segment \(g\) has at most one predicted segment \(p\) with \(\operatorname{IoU}(p, g) > 0.5\), and vice versa.

Proof

\(\operatorname{IoU}(p, g) > \tfrac12\) means \(|p \cap g| > \tfrac12 |p \cup g| \ge \tfrac12 |g|\). Suppose two predictions \(p_1, p_2\) both match \(g\). Then \(|p_1 \cap g| + |p_2 \cap g| > |g|\). But \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are disjoint, so \(p_1 \cap g\) and \(p_2 \cap g\) are disjoint subsets of \(g\), and their sizes sum to at most \(|g|\). Contradiction. The same argument with roles swapped gives uniqueness in the other direction. ∎

So matching needs no Hungarian algorithm, no tie-breaking and no scores: threshold at 0.5 and the matching is determined. Matched pairs are true positives (TP), unmatched predictions false positives (FP), unmatched ground truth false negatives (FN). For each class,

$$\text{PQ} = \frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \text{TP}} \operatorname{IoU}(p, g)}{|\text{TP}| + \tfrac12 |\text{FP}| + \tfrac12 |\text{FN}|} = \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \text{TP}} \operatorname{IoU}(p, g)}{|\text{TP}|}}_{\text{SQ: segmentation quality}} \times \underbrace{\frac{|\text{TP}|}{|\text{TP}| + \tfrac12 |\text{FP}| + \tfrac12 |\text{FN}|}}_{\text{RQ: recognition quality}}$$

The factorisation is just multiplying and dividing by \(|\text{TP}|\). SQ is the mean IoU of matched segments; RQ is exactly the F1 score of detection, since \(\text{F1} = 2\text{TP}/(2\text{TP} + \text{FP} + \text{FN})\). PQ is computed per class and averaged over classes, and reported separately for things and stuff. Two refinements: pixels labelled void in the ground truth are removed from predictions before computing IoU, and predicted segments that mostly cover void or a same-class “crowd” region are not counted as false positives.

Evidence

The paper's evidence is about the task, not a model. On Cityscapes, ADE20K and Mapillary Vistas, where some images were annotated twice, the authors compute PQ of one human annotation against the other. Humans are far from perfect; the paper breaks their disagreement down by stuff versus things and by object size, and small objects are where annotators agree least. The machine baselines fall well short of human consistency. Those baselines are a deliberately simple heuristic: take the best available semantic and instance segmentation outputs; resolve overlaps between instance masks with an NMS-like procedure in confidence order; then fill the remaining pixels with the semantic model's stuff predictions. The point is that the task is well posed, measurable, and far from solved.

Two follow-ups in one minute. Panoptic FPN (Kirillov, Girshick, He & Dollár, CVPR 2019) adds a semantic branch to Mask R-CNN's FPN (⟵ L88) so one network produces both outputs. Mask2Former (Cheng et al., CVPR 2022) predicts a set of masks with class labels via masked attention and DETR-style bipartite matching (⟵ L90), and one architecture handles semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation.

Limits & what broke
  • All segments weigh the same. Within a class, a 20-pixel person counts as much as a 20,000-pixel one; DeeperLab (Yang et al. 2019) proposed a size-weighted alternative, Parsing Covering, for this reason.
  • Stuff is awkward. Thresholding at 0.5 suits objects better than sprawling stuff regions, where a decent but fragmented prediction scores zero; variants such as PQ† treat stuff with plain IoU.
  • A hard threshold. PQ jumps when an IoU crosses 0.5, so small changes in a mask can change the score discontinuously.
  • No confidence. Dropping scores makes the output a commitment, but it also removes any notion of calibrated uncertainty from the benchmark.

Bridge

Panoptic segmentation asks for a complete, closed-vocabulary parse of the scene. Tomorrow's paper drops the vocabulary entirely: Segment Anything predicts masks for whatever you point at and never says what they are.

Segment Anything

45m videoLevel 3

Kirillov et al., ICCV 2023 (arXiv:2304.02643)

Define a task general enough to pretrain on (promptable segmentation), build a model cheap enough to run interactively, and use that model to annotate its own training set at a scale no one could label by hand: 1.1 billion masks.

Cold open

Click once on any object in any photo, a microscope slide, a satellite tile, a painting, and a mask appears in a fraction of a second. No class list, no fine-tuning. The model was not the hard part. The hard part was that no dataset of “every object in 11 million images” existed, so the authors built a pipeline in which the model helped create it.

The problem

Segmentation models were specialists: trained on a fixed label set (COCO's 80 things, ADE20K's 150 classes, L96), they segmented those classes and nothing else, and adapting them meant new labels. Interactive segmentation tools existed (RITM and others) but were trained on small data. NLP had shown a different route: a foundation model pretrained on a broad task that transfers zero-shot through prompting. Vision lacked both the task and the data for segmentation.

Definition · promptable segmentation

Given an image and a prompt (foreground/background points, a box, a rough mask, or text), return a valid mask. Valid means: even if the prompt is ambiguous, the output is a reasonable mask for at least one of the things it could refer to. A point on a shirt could mean the shirt or the person wearing it; either answer is acceptable, an incoherent blend of both is not.

The method: architecture

  • Image encoder: an MAE-pretrained ViT-H/16 (⟵ L118), minimally adapted to 1024×1024 inputs with windowed attention plus a few global blocks, producing a 16×-downscaled 64×64 embedding.
  • Prompt encoder: points and boxes become positional encodings plus learned embeddings for each prompt type; free text goes through CLIP's text encoder; a mask prompt is embedded by convolutions and added to the image embedding.
  • Mask decoder: output tokens and prompt tokens attend to each other and, in both directions, to the image embedding (token-to-image and image-to-token cross-attention), for two blocks. The embedding is upsampled, and an MLP turns each output token into a dynamic linear classifier applied at every pixel.

The decoder is tiny on purpose. Interactive annotation, and the data engine below, need a response per click. The expensive computation is amortised over every prompt on the same image.

Derivation · making an ill-posed task trainable

With one output, a point on a shirt has two correct targets, and squared or cross-entropy loss is minimised by their average: a blurry mask that is neither. SAM predicts \(K = 3\) masks \(\hat m_1, \hat m_2, \hat m_3\) and backpropagates only through the best one:

$$\mathcal L = \min_{k \in \{1,2,3\}} \ell(\hat m_k, m), \qquad \ell = 20\, \ell_{\text{focal}} + \ell_{\text{dice}}.$$

Each output slot is free to specialise (empirically: whole, part, subpart), because it is never pulled towards a target another slot already explains. A separate head predicts each mask's IoU with the target (trained with MSE), so at inference the masks can be ranked. When several prompts are given, ambiguity largely disappears, and a fourth output token produces a single mask.

The method: the data engine

Algorithm · data engine
  1. Assisted-manual. Annotators click foreground and background points in a browser tool powered by SAM and refine masks with brush tools. The model is retrained repeatedly on the growing data. The authors report about 4.3M masks from 120k images.
  2. Semi-automatic. SAM proposes confident masks automatically; annotators label what it missed, which pushes diversity towards less prominent objects. About 5.9M further masks from 180k images.
  3. Fully automatic. Prompt SAM with a regular 32×32 grid of points; keep masks with high predicted IoU that are also stable (nearly unchanged when the probability threshold is moved), and remove duplicates with NMS. Run on 11M images: 1.1B masks.

The released SA-1B dataset has 11M licensed, privacy-protected images and 1.1B masks, of which 99.1% were generated fully automatically. The data engine is the paper's real contribution, and it is a process contribution: a loop in which the model makes annotation cheaper and the annotation makes the model better.

Evidence

The authors evaluate zero-shot transfer: single-point segmentation on 23 diverse datasets, where SAM beats the strong interactive baseline RITM on most datasets and, more consistently, in human ratings of mask quality; and, through prompt engineering alone, edge detection on BSDS500, object proposals on LVIS, instance segmentation by prompting with a detector's boxes, and a proof-of-concept text-to-mask. On these tasks SAM is reasonable but generally below a supervised specialist on that benchmark's own metric, which is the right expectation for a zero-shot model. A quality audit compared a sample of automatic masks with professionally corrected versions and found close agreement for the large majority.

Limits & what broke
  • No semantics. SAM segments; it does not name. Its masks carry no class, and the text prompt is exploratory and not robust. Practical systems pair it with a detector or vision-language model (Grounding DINO, CLIP) to say what each mask is. That gap is instructive: the training data never contained names.
  • Boundaries and fine structure. The paper notes SAM can miss fine structures, sometimes hallucinates small disconnected components, and produces less crisp boundaries than heavier “zoom-in” methods.
  • The encoder is not real-time. ViT-H at 1024² is expensive; distilled variants (EfficientSAM, MobileSAM and others) replaced it for edge use.
  • Images only. SAM 2 (Ravi et al. 2024) extended promptable segmentation to video with a streaming memory.

Bridge

SAM closes the dense-prediction arc: from FCN's 21 classes to class-agnostic masks for anything. The rest of the month leaves single images. Tomorrow, video, starting with the observation that motion is information a single frame does not contain.

Two-Stream Convolutional Networks for Action Recognition

30m videoLevel 2

Simonyan & Zisserman, NIPS 2014 (arXiv:1406.2199)

Give the network motion explicitly: one ConvNet sees a single RGB frame for appearance, a second sees a stack of precomputed optical-flow fields for motion, and their predictions are fused late.

Cold open

In 2014, a deep network shown many frames of a video recognised actions barely better than one shown a single frame. Karpathy et al.'s large Sports-1M study found exactly that: their multi-frame fusion models gained little over the single-frame model. Networks were not learning motion from raw pixels. This paper stopped asking them to and computed the motion for them.

The problem

Action recognition was dominated by hand-crafted features: improved dense trajectories (iDT, Wang & Schmid 2013) encoded with Fisher vectors. ConvNets had just overtaken hand-crafted features on images (AlexNet, ⟵ L61), but video datasets were small (UCF-101 has about 13k clips, HMDB-51 about 7k), and a network fed stacked frames had to discover, from little data, that the useful signal is how pixels move, not what they look like.

The method

The spatial stream is an ordinary ImageNet-pretrained image classifier applied to individual frames: many actions (playing a cello, horse riding) are recognisable from one frame. The temporal stream takes a stack of dense optical flow. Each flow field \(d_t\) gives, for every pixel \((u, v)\), its horizontal and vertical displacement \(d^x_t, d^y_t\) between frames \(t\) and \(t+1\).

Definition · two ways to stack flow

Optical flow stacking. For \(L\) consecutive frames starting at \(\tau\), stack the displacement channels at fixed pixel locations:

$$I_\tau(u, v, 2k-1) = d^x_{\tau+k-1}(u, v), \qquad I_\tau(u, v, 2k) = d^y_{\tau+k-1}(u, v), \qquad k = 1, \dots, L.$$

Trajectory stacking. Sample the flow along motion trajectories instead: \(p_1 = (u, v)\), \(p_k = p_{k-1} + d_{\tau+k-2}(p_{k-1})\), and stack \(d_{\tau+k-1}(p_k)\). Either way the input has \(2L\) channels; the paper uses \(L = 10\). Two refinements: bidirectional flow (half the stack forward, half backward) and mean flow subtraction, which removes the average displacement as a crude camera-motion compensation.

The temporal ConvNet is trained from scratch, because ImageNet weights expect colour images, not displacement fields. With small video datasets that invites overfitting, so the paper uses multi-task learning: one network with two softmax heads, one for UCF-101 and one for HMDB-51, each loss computed only on its own dataset's clips, summed. The shared trunk sees both datasets; the heads keep the label spaces separate. Flow is computed with the method of Brox et al. (2004) on a GPU, then compressed to JPEG so the whole dataset's flow fits on disk.

The motivation is biological: the two-streams hypothesis of Goodale & Milner, with a ventral pathway for recognising objects and a dorsal pathway for motion and action. Treat it as an analogy that suggested a design, not as evidence for it.

Evidence

The ablations make three points. The temporal stream alone clearly beats the spatial stream alone on UCF-101, so explicit motion is the stronger cue. Optical flow stacking works at least as well as trajectory stacking, and mean subtraction helps. Multi-task training improves the small HMDB-51 more than simply adding UCF-101 clips to its training set. Fusing the streams with a linear SVM on their softmax scores beats averaging, and the final two-stream model reaches about 88% on UCF-101 and about 59% on HMDB-51 by the authors' report, on par with the best trajectory-based hand-crafted pipelines. Parity, not a decisive win, was the headline: deep networks had caught up on video.

Limits & what broke
  • Flow is the bottleneck. It is expensive to compute, must be precomputed and stored, and is not differentiable with respect to the network, so the representation of motion is fixed by a 2004 algorithm. That is exactly why the field moved to 3D convolutions that learn motion (L99), then to flow-free designs (L100), and why better learned flow (RAFT, ⟶ L107) mattered.
  • Late fusion only. The streams cannot relate where something is to how it moves. Feichtenhofer et al. (2016) showed fusing at a convolutional layer works better.
  • Short temporal extent. Ten frames is a fraction of a second; long actions need sampling across the whole video (Temporal Segment Networks, Wang et al. 2016).
  • Why flow helps is subtle. Later analysis (Sevilla-Lara et al. 2018) suggested much of its value is invariance to appearance, not motion accuracy as such.

Bridge

Two-stream networks proved that motion matters and that hand-computed flow was a strong but costly crutch. Tomorrow's paper keeps both streams, makes each one a 3D ConvNet, and finds a clean trick for starting those 3D networks from ImageNet weights. It also brings the dataset the field had been missing.

Quo Vadis, Action Recognition? (I3D)

30m videoLevel 2

Carreira & Zisserman, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1705.07750)

With a large video dataset (Kinetics) and a way to initialise 3D ConvNets from ImageNet weights (inflate each 2D filter along time and rescale it), 3D convolutions finally beat 2D ones for video, and pretraining on Kinetics transfers like ImageNet pretraining does for images.

Cold open

Take a 2D filter trained on ImageNet. Copy it three times along a new time axis and divide by three. Show the resulting 3D filter a “video” in which nothing moves, the same frame repeated, and it responds exactly as the 2D filter did to the image. That one-line identity let a whole generation of video networks start from ImageNet instead of from scratch.

The problem

Video architectures in 2017 were a zoo: ConvNet+LSTM, 3D ConvNets such as C3D trained from scratch, and two-stream networks (⟵ L98). Comparisons were inconclusive because the benchmarks were tiny: UCF-101 and HMDB-51 have thousands of clips, not millions. 3D ConvNets have more parameters than 2D ones and no ImageNet weights to start from, so on small data they lost. The question “which architecture is best?” could not be answered until there was enough data to train the big ones.

Kinetics, introduced alongside this work (Kay et al. 2017), supplied it: 400 human action classes, at least 400 clips per class, each about 10 seconds and from a distinct YouTube video. The paper's title asks where action recognition is going; its answer is largely “to Kinetics pretraining”.

The method: inflation

Take an image architecture (BN-Inception) and give every filter and pooling kernel a temporal dimension: an \(N \times N\) kernel becomes \(N \times N \times N\) (the temporal size can be chosen per layer). The architecture is now a 3D ConvNet, the Inflated 3D ConvNet (I3D). The question is how to initialise it.

Derivation · the boring-video fixed point

Let a 3D filter \(W\) have temporal slices \(W_1, \dots, W_N\) and apply it to a video whose frames are all the same image \(x\). Its response at any interior position is \(\sum_{t=1}^{N} W_t * x\). Setting \(W_t = w/N\) for every \(t\) gives

$$\sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{w}{N} * x = w * x,$$

the 2D filter's response to the image. Since this holds for every layer, and pooling and batch norm of identical frames are unchanged too, the whole inflated network's output on a static video equals the original network's output on the image. Without the \(1/N\), activations would be \(N\) times too large at the first layer and compound through depth. At the temporal borders, zero padding means fewer than \(N\) slices see real frames, so the identity holds exactly only in the interior.

Inflation is a starting point: from there, fine-tuning on Kinetics learns genuinely spatio-temporal filters. A few design choices control temporal receptive field: the early pooling layers do not pool in time, so the network does not collapse time too soon; training uses 64-frame clips, and test-time predictions are averaged over the whole video. Two-stream I3D trains one I3D on RGB and another on TV-L1 optical flow and averages their predictions, because, empirically, flow still helped.

Evidence

The paper re-implements five architectures and trains all of them on Kinetics, with and without ImageNet initialisation. Two-stream I3D is best, and ImageNet-inflated I3D beats I3D from scratch. The larger result is about data: every architecture improves substantially when pretrained on Kinetics before fine-tuning on the small benchmarks. The abstract reports 80.9% on HMDB-51 and 98.0% on UCF-101 after Kinetics pretraining. In this series' running theme, data scale beat architecture: the architecture comparison that was inconclusive on small data became clear on a large one.

Derivation · R(2+1)D parameter matching

A full \(t \times d \times d\) convolution from \(N_{i-1}\) to \(N_i\) channels has \(t d^2 N_{i-1} N_i\) weights. The factorised version uses \(d^2 N_{i-1} M_i\) (spatial, to \(M_i\) channels) plus \(t M_i N_i\) (temporal). Equating, Tran et al. (2018) choose

$$M_i = \left\lfloor \frac{t d^2 N_{i-1} N_i}{d^2 N_{i-1} + t N_i} \right\rfloor .$$

Same capacity, twice the nonlinearities, and the authors report easier optimisation. C3D (Tran et al. 2015), the 3×3×3-everywhere network trained from scratch on Sports-1M, is the other neighbour worth knowing.

Limits & what broke
  • Kinetics rewards appearance. Many classes can be recognised from a single frame or the scene; datasets such as Something-Something (Goyal et al. 2017) were built to require temporal reasoning, and models strong on Kinetics do much less well there.
  • Flow still needed. The best result keeps the expensive optical-flow stream that 3D convolution was supposed to make unnecessary.
  • Compute. Dense 3D convolution over 64 frames is costly. SlowFast (L100) attacks this by spending capacity unevenly over time.
  • A decaying dataset. Kinetics is distributed as YouTube links; videos disappear over time, so exact reproductions get harder every year.

Bridge

I3D treats time like a third spatial axis. Tomorrow's paper argues that time is different: semantics change slowly and motion changes fast, so a network should look at video at two frame rates with very different capacities, and needs no optical flow at all.

SlowFast Networks for Video Recognition

30m videoLevel 2

Feichtenhofer, Fan, Malik & He, ICCV 2019 (arXiv:1812.03982)

Space and time are not symmetric in video: what objects are changes slowly, how they move changes fast. So process video with two pathways, a high-capacity one at a low frame rate and a very thin one at a high frame rate, and let motion flow from the thin one into the thick one.

Cold open

SlowFast's Fast pathway sees eight times as many frames as its Slow pathway and costs only about a fifth of the network's computation. There is no optical flow anywhere, no ImageNet pretraining, and it beat the flow-based two-stream models of the day. The asymmetry is the design.

The problem

I3D (L99) treats time as one more axis and convolves over it everywhere, which is expensive, and still needs a separate optical-flow stream for its best results. But video is not isotropic. A person waving stays a person for the whole clip: the categorical content barely changes from frame to frame, so it can be sampled sparsely. The waving itself is fast and needs dense temporal sampling, but recognising motion does not need rich colour or fine spatial detail.

The method

  • Slow pathway. A large temporal stride \(\tau\) (16 raw frames, so about 2 frames per second at 30 fps), \(T\) frames, full channel width \(C\). It uses temporal convolutions only in res4 and res5; earlier stages use \(1 \times k \times k\) kernels. The authors found temporal kernels in early Slow layers hurt: with frames that far apart, a small spatial receptive field sees an object in one frame and background in the next.
  • Fast pathway. Stride \(\tau/\alpha\), so \(\alpha T\) frames, with \(\alpha = 8\) by default; channel width \(\beta C\), \(\beta = 1/8\); temporal convolutions in every stage and no temporal downsampling, so it keeps fine temporal resolution to the end.
  • Lateral connections after pool1, res2, res3 and res4. The shapes differ (Fast is \(\{\alpha T, S^2, \beta C\}\), Slow is \(\{T, S^2, C\}\)), so Fast features are transformed first. Three options are compared: time-to-channel (reshape \(\alpha\) frames into channels, \(\{T, S^2, \alpha\beta C\}\)), time-strided sampling (keep one frame in \(\alpha\)), and a time-strided convolution (a 5×1² kernel with stride \(\alpha\) and \(2\beta C\) outputs). The result is summed or concatenated into Slow. The strided convolution works best. Both pathways' outputs are globally pooled and concatenated before the classifier.
Derivation · why eight times the frames costs so little

A convolution's cost is proportional to (number of frames) × (spatial positions) × \(k_t k^2\) × \(C_{\text{in}} C_{\text{out}}\). Compare a Fast layer with the corresponding Slow layer at the same spatial size and kernel:

$$\frac{\text{Fast}}{\text{Slow}} = \frac{\alpha T \cdot (\beta C)^2}{T \cdot C^2} = \alpha \beta^2 = 8 \cdot \tfrac{1}{64} = \tfrac18 .$$

Channels enter the cost squared and frames only linearly, so cutting channels by 8 more than pays for 8× the frames. In the early stages the Fast pathway also uses temporal kernels (\(k_t = 3\)) in layers where the Slow one uses \(k_t = 1\), which raises its relative cost there. Summed over the network, the paper reports the Fast pathway at roughly 20% of total computation.

Evidence

Trained from scratch on Kinetics-400 and -600, SlowFast reached state-of-the-art accuracy at the time, by the authors' report beating prior results that relied on ImageNet pretraining and optical flow, and it also set strong results on Charades and AVA action detection. The ablations are the persuasive part. Adding the Fast pathway to a Slow-only network gives a clear accuracy gain for a modest increase in FLOPs, a better trade than making the Slow pathway itself denser in time. Across \(\beta\) from 1/4 to 1/32, every setting improves on Slow-only. And the Fast pathway still helps when its input is deliberately weakened: half spatial resolution, grayscale, or frame differences. Motion does not need colour.

How much weight to put on the retina

The paper draws an analogy with primate retinal ganglion cells: roughly 80% are P-cells (fine spatial detail and colour, slow temporal response) and roughly 15–20% M-cells (fast temporal response, coarse spatial detail, little colour sensitivity), and the Fast pathway's 20% share of compute echoes that ratio. It is a pleasing post-hoc story. It did not determine \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\), which were set by ablation, and the network's “cells” are not neurons. Mention it, then move on.

Limits & what broke
  • Two backbones to tune. \(\tau\), \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), fusion type and placement are all hyperparameters. X3D (Feichtenhofer 2020) found that progressively expanding a single small network along time, space, width and depth reaches similar accuracy with far less computation.
  • Local in time. Convolutions see a few frames at a time; long-range reasoning comes only from depth and the final pooling.
  • Superseded by attention. Video transformers (TimeSformer, ViViT, MViT, Video Swin) took over the benchmarks from 2021, usually with image pretraining, which SlowFast had shown was not strictly necessary.

Bridge

SlowFast hand-designs which frames talk to which. Tomorrow's paper lets attention decide, and immediately hits a wall: joint attention over every patch of every frame is quadratic in the number of space-time tokens.

Is Space-Time Attention All You Need for Video Understanding? (TimeSformer)

30m videoLevel 3

Bertasius, Wang & Torresani, ICML 2021 (arXiv:2102.05095)

A convolution-free video classifier is a ViT over space-time patches; the only real design question is which patches attend to which, and the paper answers it empirically: attend over time, then over space, in separate steps.

Cold open

Cut an 8-frame 224×224 clip into 16×16 patches and you have 1,568 tokens. Let every token attend to every other and one attention map has about 2.5 million entries, per head, per layer. At 96 frames it is about 354 million. Joint space-time attention is the obvious design and it does not fit. This paper is a careful study of what to do instead.

The problem

ViT (L115) had just shown that a plain transformer on image patches can match CNNs given enough pretraining. Video models were still 3D CNNs (L99, L100): local in space and time, with long-range reasoning only through depth. Self-attention offers global receptive fields from the first layer, but video multiplies the token count by the number of frames, and attention cost is quadratic in tokens.

The method

Each frame of a clip with \(F\) frames is split into \(N = HW/P^2\) patches, linearly embedded, given a space-time position embedding, and prefixed with a class token, exactly as in ViT. A ViT-Base encoder initialised from ImageNet-pretrained weights processes the \(NF\) tokens. Five ways to restrict attention are compared:

Definition · divided space-time attention

Within each block, for patch \((p, t)\) with spatial index \(p\) and frame \(t\):

  1. Temporal attention: the query attends to keys \(\{(p, t') : t' = 1, \dots, F\}\), the same location in every frame (plus the class token). Residual add.
  2. Spatial attention: the result attends to \(\{(p', t) : p' = 1, \dots, N\}\), every patch in its own frame. Residual add.
  3. MLP, residual add.

The two attention steps have separate query/key/value projections. Each token makes \(F + N + 2\) comparisons instead of \(NF + 1\).

Derivation · the cost, for a real clip

Per layer and head, joint attention compares all pairs: \((NF)^2\). Divided attention costs \(NF \cdot F\) for the temporal step plus \(NF \cdot N\) for the spatial step:

$$\underbrace{(NF)^2}_{\text{joint}} \quad\text{vs}\quad \underbrace{N F^2 + N^2 F}_{\text{divided}} = NF(N + F).$$

That is \(N\) temporal problems of size \(F^2\) plus \(F\) spatial problems of size \(N^2\). With \(N = 196\) and \(F = 8\): 2,458,624 versus 319,872 comparisons, about 8× fewer. With \(F = 96\): about \(3.5 \times 10^8\) versus \(5.5 \times 10^6\), about 64× fewer. Since \(F \ll N\) for typical clips, the \(N^2 F\) term dominates and divided attention grows roughly linearly in the number of frames, while joint attention grows quadratically. A single fp32 joint attention map at 96 frames would take about 1.4 GB.

def attention_pairs(frames, side=224, patch=16):
    n = (side // patch) ** 2               # patches per frame
    tokens = n * frames
    joint = tokens ** 2                    # every token attends to every token
    divided = tokens * (frames + n)        # temporal (frames) + spatial (n) per token
    return tokens, joint, divided

for f in (8, 96):
    t, j, d = attention_pairs(f)
    print(f"{f:3d} frames: {t:6,} tokens  joint {j:,}  divided {d:,}  ratio {j / d:.0f}x")

Evidence

The five-scheme ablation is the paper. On Kinetics-400 and Something-Something-v2, divided attention gives the best accuracy. Space-only attention is competitive on Kinetics, where single frames carry most of the signal (L99), but falls well behind on Something-Something, which requires temporal reasoning: the pair of benchmarks separates “recognises scenes” from “models motion”. Joint attention is no more accurate than divided at the settings where it fits and becomes infeasible as resolution or clip length grows. The authors then scale the divided model to higher resolution (TimeSformer-HR) and to 96-frame inputs (TimeSformer-L) for long-form video, and report lower inference cost than strong 3D CNNs at comparable accuracy.

Pretraining is again the hidden variable: the model starts from ImageNet-21K (or 1K) ViT weights, the authors report that training from scratch on Kinetics works much worse, and the larger pretraining set helps on Kinetics. A transformer without convolution's inductive biases needs the data to come from somewhere.

Limits & what broke
  • Factorisation is an assumption. Divided attention reaches a patch in another frame and location only in two hops, through the same-location token. Motion along a diagonal trajectory is modelled indirectly.
  • Image pretraining dependence. Without ImageNet-21K-scale initialisation the model underperforms 3D CNNs trained from scratch; later video self-supervision (VideoMAE, 2022) addressed this.
  • Global attention is wasteful. Video Swin (Liu et al., CVPR 2022) used 3D shifted windows (the idea of L117) and outperformed global factorised attention at lower cost; ViViT (Arnab et al., ICCV 2021) explored several factorisations concurrently; MViT built multiscale pyramids.
  • Short clips still. 8 frames sampled from a 10-second clip is sparse; “long-range” here means a few seconds, not minutes.

Bridge

Video added a time axis to a grid. The next three lessons drop the grid: a 3D point cloud from a lidar or depth sensor is an unordered set of coordinates, and neither convolution nor positional embeddings apply directly.

PointNet: Deep Learning on Point Sets

40m videoLevel 3

Qi, Su, Mo & Guibas, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1612.00593)

To learn on an unordered set of points, apply the same MLP to every point and combine the results with a symmetric function, max pooling; a theorem says this architecture can approximate any continuous set function, and the max tells you which points matter.

Cold open

Feed PointNet a chair as 1,024 points. Only a small subset of them, a sparse skeleton of the chair, actually determines the global feature; delete every other point and the output does not change at all. That is not an empirical surprise but a consequence of the architecture, and it makes PointNet unusually robust to missing data.

The problem

A lidar scan or depth sensor gives a set of \(n\) points \(\{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subset \R^3\), possibly with colour or normals. It has no grid, so convolution does not apply, and no order: the same object can arrive in any of \(n!\) orderings. The workarounds of 2016 converted the data into something CNN-shaped. Voxelisation (VoxNet, 3D ShapeNets) costs memory cubic in resolution and quantises away detail; multi-view rendering (MVCNN) works well for classification but discards 3D structure and is awkward for per-point tasks.

The paper lists three properties a network on points must respect: permutation invariance (the output cannot depend on the order of points); interaction among points (neighbouring points form local structure); invariance under transformations (a rotated chair is still a chair).

The method

Definition · symmetric set function

PointNet computes \(f(\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}) = \gamma\big(\max_{i} h(x_i)\big)\), where \(h: \R^3 \to \R^K\) is a shared MLP, the max is taken coordinate-wise, and \(\gamma\) is another MLP. Because the max of a set does not depend on the order of its elements, \(f\) is permutation-invariant by construction. Max pooling is the entire invariance mechanism; nothing else in the network knows the points form a set.

Theorem 1 · universal approximation

Let \(f\) be a continuous set function on subsets of \([0, 1]^3\) with respect to the Hausdorff distance. For every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there are a continuous \(h\) and \(\gamma\) such that, for every set \(S\) of \(n\) points, \(\big|f(S) - \gamma\big(\max_{x \in S} h(x)\big)\big| < \varepsilon\).

Proof sketch

Continuity gives a \(\delta\) such that moving every point by less than \(\delta\) changes \(f\) by less than \(\varepsilon\). Partition the cube into voxels of side about \(\delta\). Let \(h\) map a point to a (continuous approximation of a) one-hot vector marking its voxel. Then \(\max_{x \in S} h(x)\) is the voxel occupancy vector of \(S\): coordinate \(j\) is 1 exactly when some point lies in voxel \(j\). Let \(\gamma\) map an occupancy vector to the value of \(f\) on the set of occupied voxel centres. That set is within Hausdorff distance \(\delta\) of \(S\), so the error is below \(\varepsilon\). The construction needs \(K\) as large as the number of voxels; the theorem says max pooling is enough, not that 1024 dimensions are. ∎

Critical points. A second theorem makes the cold open precise. Each of the \(K\) coordinates of the max is achieved by some point, so the global feature is determined by a critical point set \(C_S\) of at most \(K\) points: any \(T\) with \(C_S \subseteq T \subseteq N_S\) gives the same output, where the upper-bound shape \(N_S\) is every point of space whose feature vector is coordinate-wise below the current max. Points can be deleted down to \(C_S\), and points added anywhere inside \(N_S\), without changing the result.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
W1, W2 = rng.normal(size=(3, 64)), rng.normal(size=(64, 1024))

def point_features(P):                        # shared MLP: the same weights for every point
    return np.maximum(np.maximum(P @ W1, 0) @ W2, 0)

def global_feature(P):                        # P: n x 3, rows in any order
    return point_features(P).max(axis=0)      # max pool = symmetric function over the set

P = rng.normal(size=(1024, 3))
g = global_feature(P)
assert np.allclose(g, global_feature(P[rng.permutation(len(P))]))  # order does not matter

critical = np.unique(point_features(P).argmax(axis=0))   # points that win at least one max
assert np.allclose(g, global_feature(P[critical]))       # they alone give the same feature
print(len(critical), "of", len(P), "points determine g")

Even with random weights the critical set is small (about a hundred points here): many coordinates are won by the same extreme points of the cloud. In the paper's visualisations of trained networks it is likewise a sparse skeleton of the shape.

T-Nets. To approach transformation invariance, a mini-PointNet predicts a 3×3 matrix applied to the input points, and a second predicts a 64×64 matrix applied to the features. The 64×64 matrix has 4,096 free parameters and can collapse or blow up features, so it is regularised towards an orthogonal transform, which preserves information: \(\mathcal L_{\text{reg}} = \|I - AA^\top\|_F^2\), added to the loss with a small weight.

Evidence

On ModelNet40 classification the authors report about 89% overall accuracy, on par with or better than volumetric CNNs and competitive with multi-view methods at a fraction of the computation, plus strong results for ShapeNet part segmentation and S3DIS indoor scene segmentation with the same backbone. The robustness experiments follow from the critical-set result: accuracy drops by only a few points with half the input points deleted, and degrades gracefully with outliers. The transform ablation is small but telling: the input and feature transforms together give a gain of about two points, and the feature transform helps only when regularised; without the orthogonality term it does slightly worse than no feature transform.

Limits & what broke
  • No local structure. Points interact only through one global max, so PointNet cannot build the hierarchy of local patterns that makes CNNs work. This is the whole motivation of PointNet++ (L103).
  • Rotation. T-Nets learn an alignment; they are not equivariant, and accuracy falls on arbitrarily rotated inputs. Rotation-equivariant point networks came later.
  • Max discards counts. The global feature is identical for any subset containing the critical points, so density and multiplicity information is lost.
  • The general theorem was Deep Sets'. Zaheer et al. (NeurIPS 2017) proved that permutation-invariant functions (on countable domains, with extensions under conditions) decompose as \(\rho\big(\sum_i \phi(x_i)\big)\). PointNet is the max-pooling instance of the same idea, which reappears in graph networks and in attention-based set models.

Bridge

PointNet is to point clouds what a single fully connected layer over a bag of pixels would be to images: invariant, universal, and blind to locality. Tomorrow's paper rebuilds the CNN hierarchy (sample centres, group neighbours, summarise each group with a small PointNet) for data with no grid.

PointNet++: Deep Hierarchical Feature Learning on Point Sets

30m videoLevel 3

Qi, Yi, Su & Guibas, NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1706.02413)

Rebuild the CNN hierarchy for irregular data: sample centres with farthest-point sampling, group their neighbours with a fixed-radius ball, summarise each group with a small PointNet, and repeat, with multi-scale grouping and point dropout to survive uneven density.

Cold open

PointNet (L102) lets points meet in exactly one place, a single global max. Imagine an image classifier whose only interaction between pixels was a global max pool after per-pixel MLPs: no edges, no textures, no parts. That is the gap this paper closes, and the way to understand it is as a CNN rebuilt from parts that do not need a grid.

The problem

CNNs work because they build features hierarchically: small receptive fields detect local patterns, and deeper layers combine them over larger regions. PointNet captures neither local structure nor its composition, which limits fine-grained recognition and generalisation to complex scenes. Point clouds add a second problem grids do not have: non-uniform density. A lidar scan is dense near the sensor and sparse far away, so a neighbourhood of fixed size may contain hundreds of points in one place and three in another.

The method: set abstraction

Algorithm · one set-abstraction level
  1. Sample. Choose \(N'\) centroids from the \(N\) input points with farthest point sampling (FPS).
  2. Group. For each centroid, collect up to \(K\) points within radius \(r\) (ball query), giving \(N' \times K\) groups.
  3. Summarise. Express each group's coordinates relative to its centroid and pass it through a shared mini-PointNet (MLP + max). Output: \(N'\) points, each with a new, longer feature vector.

Stack levels with fewer centroids and larger radii; finish with a PointNet over the last level for classification.

FPS picks a start point, then repeatedly adds the point farthest from everything chosen so far. Its coverage is far better than random sampling for the same budget, it is deterministic given the start, and it adapts receptive fields to the data: centres go where the points are, not on a fixed lattice. It has become a standard 3D primitive.

import numpy as np

def farthest_point_sampling(P, m, start=0):
    """Pick m indices; each new point is the one farthest from all points chosen so far."""
    chosen = [start]
    dist = np.linalg.norm(P - P[start], axis=1)      # distance to the nearest chosen point
    for _ in range(m - 1):
        nxt = int(dist.argmax())
        chosen.append(nxt)
        dist = np.minimum(dist, np.linalg.norm(P - P[nxt], axis=1))
    return np.array(chosen)                          # O(n m) time, O(n) memory

def coverage(P, idx):                                # worst distance from a point to its nearest sample
    return np.linalg.norm(P[:, None] - P[idx][None], axis=2).min(axis=1).max()

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
P = np.r_[rng.normal(0, 0.1, (900, 3)), rng.uniform(-1, 1, (100, 3))]   # dense blob + sparse spread
fps = farthest_point_sampling(P, 64)
rnd = rng.choice(len(P), 64, replace=False)
print(f"FPS {coverage(P, fps):.2f}   random {coverage(P, rnd):.2f}")   # 0.32 vs 1.16 here

Ball query versus kNN. k-nearest neighbours gives every group the same number of points but a variable spatial extent: tiny in dense regions, huge in sparse ones. A ball gives every group the same spatial extent and a variable number of points. The paper prefers the ball because a fixed region scale makes local features mean the same thing everywhere, which generalises better across space, exactly as a convolution kernel covers the same number of pixels everywhere in an image.

The method: surviving uneven density

  • Multi-scale grouping (MSG). At each level, group with several radii and concatenate the resulting features, so the network can draw on large regions where points are sparse and small ones where they are dense.
  • Multi-resolution grouping (MRG). A cheaper alternative: concatenate the feature summarising a region's sub-regions (from the previous level) with a feature computed directly from all raw points in the region. When density is low, the first is unreliable and the network can lean on the second.
  • Random input dropout. During training, drop each point with probability \(\theta\), where \(\theta \sim U[0, p]\) is drawn per point cloud (\(p = 0.95\)). The network sees the same shapes at every density from complete to very sparse, which is what actually teaches MSG and MRG to weight their scales adaptively.
Definition · feature propagation for segmentation

Segmentation needs a feature at every original point, but the last level has few. Propagate back level by level: for each point \(x\) of the denser level, interpolate from its \(k = 3\) nearest points of the sparser level,

$$f(x) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i(x)\, f_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{k} w_i(x)}, \qquad w_i(x) = \frac{1}{d(x, x_i)^p},\ p = 2,$$

concatenate with that level's own features via a skip link, and apply a shared 1×1 “unit PointNet”. This is U-Net's decoder (L92) with inverse-distance interpolation in place of up-convolution.

Evidence

On ModelNet40 the authors report about 91% classification accuracy from points alone, a clear gain over PointNet, and higher with normals. The density experiment is the most convincing: as test inputs are thinned from 1,024 points down to a few hundred, models trained with random input dropout and MSG or MRG barely degrade, while the same architecture without dropout degrades sharply. On ScanNet semantic segmentation and on non-rigid shape classification (SHREC15), the hierarchy improves clearly over single-level PointNet. The evidence supports the framing: locality and hierarchy matter for points as they do for pixels.

Limits & what broke
  • Slow primitives. FPS is sequential and \(O(N N')\); ball queries need neighbour search and padding to \(K\). On million-point lidar scans this dominates runtime, and irregular memory access wastes GPU throughput.
  • Sparse convolutions took over large scenes. Voxelising sparsely and convolving only occupied cells (SparseConvNet, Graham et al. 2018; MinkowskiNet, Choy et al. 2019) became the standard for indoor and driving segmentation; point transformers (Zhao et al. 2021 and successors) followed.
  • The baseline was under-trained. PointNeXt (Qian et al., NeurIPS 2022) showed that PointNet++ with a modern training recipe and modest scaling matches many later architectures, another case of tuning the baseline before believing the delta.
  • Representation, not recognition. For reconstructing and rendering scenes the field moved to radiance fields and Gaussian splats, the next three lessons, where points return as explicit primitives (L106).

Bridge

NeRF: Representing Scenes as Neural Radiance Fields

55m videoLevel 3

Mildenhall, Srinivasan, Tancik, Barron, Ramamoorthi & Ng, ECCV 2020 (arXiv:2003.08934)

Represent a scene as an MLP from 3D position and viewing direction to colour and density, render pixels by differentiable volume rendering along camera rays, and fit the MLP to a few dozen photographs by gradient descent on the pixel error.

Cold open

Give a network about a hundred photos of a Lego bulldozer with known camera positions. Afterwards, ask it for a view from a camera that never existed, and it renders one with reflections, thin structures and correct occlusion. The scene is stored in a few megabytes of MLP weights, and the only supervision was “make these pixels match those photos”.

The problem

View synthesis methods before NeRF either stored explicit geometry (meshes, which are hard to optimise from images; or voxel grids, whose memory grows cubically with resolution, as in Neural Volumes and LLFF's multiplane images), or learned a scene representation with networks that produced oversmoothed results (Scene Representation Networks). None combined photorealism, high resolution and compact storage. Coordinate-based MLPs had been used for shape (occupancy networks, DeepSDF) but, fed raw coordinates, they could only represent smooth functions.

The method: the field and the renderer

Definition · neural radiance field

An MLP \(F_\Theta: (\mathbf x, \mathbf d) \mapsto (\mathbf c, \sigma)\) maps a 3D point \(\mathbf x = (x, y, z)\) and a viewing direction \(\mathbf d\) (the paper's \((\theta, \phi)\), passed as a 3D unit vector) to an emitted colour \(\mathbf c\) and a volume density \(\sigma \ge 0\). Density is the differential probability that a ray terminates at an infinitesimal particle at \(\mathbf x\).

Derivation · the volume rendering integral

Follow a camera ray \(\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf o + t\mathbf d\). Let \(T(t)\) be the probability that the ray travels from \(t_n\) to \(t\) without hitting anything. Over a short step \(dt\) it is stopped with probability \(\sigma(t)\,dt\), so

$$T(t + dt) = T(t)\big(1 - \sigma(t)\,dt\big) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dT}{dt} = -\sigma(t)\,T(t) \;\Rightarrow\; T(t) = \exp\!\Big(-\int_{t_n}^{t} \sigma(s)\,ds\Big).$$

The probability density of the ray terminating at \(t\) is “survive to \(t\)” times “stop at \(t\)”: \(T(t)\sigma(t)\). The expected colour seen by the pixel is therefore

$$C(\mathbf r) = \int_{t_n}^{t_f} T(t)\,\sigma(\mathbf r(t))\,\mathbf c(\mathbf r(t), \mathbf d)\,dt.$$
Derivation · the discrete quadrature

Split \([t_n, t_f]\) into \(N\) bins and draw one sample \(t_i\) uniformly in each (stratified sampling, so the network is queried at continuous positions over training). Assume \(\sigma\) and \(\mathbf c\) are constant, \(\sigma_i\) and \(\mathbf c_i\), on segment \(i\) of length \(\delta_i = t_{i+1} - t_i\). Inside that segment, at distance \(s\) past \(t_i\), \(T = T_i e^{-\sigma_i s}\), so the segment contributes

$$\int_0^{\delta_i} T_i e^{-\sigma_i s}\,\sigma_i\,\mathbf c_i\,ds = T_i\big(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\big)\,\mathbf c_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\Big(-\sum_{j<i} \sigma_j \delta_j\Big).$$

Summing over segments gives the equation that must be walked term by term:

$$\hat C(\mathbf r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \underbrace{T_i}_{\text{reaches } i}\; \underbrace{\big(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\big)}_{\alpha_i:\ \text{stops in } i}\; \mathbf c_i .$$

This is front-to-back alpha compositing with \(\alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}\), since \(T_i = \prod_{j<i}(1 - \alpha_j)\). The weights telescope: \(T_i \alpha_i = T_i - T_{i+1}\), so \(\sum_i w_i = 1 - T_{N+1} \le 1\), and the leftover \(T_{N+1}\) is the probability the ray hits nothing. Every operation is differentiable in \(\sigma_i\) and \(\mathbf c_i\), hence in \(\Theta\). That differentiability is the reason the method works at all: the loss on pixels flows back to every sample along every ray.

import numpy as np

def render_ray(sigma, rgb, t):
    """sigma: (N,), rgb: (N, 3) per sample; t: (N + 1,) bin edges. Returns colour and weights."""
    delta = np.diff(t)                                        # δ_i = t_{i+1} − t_i
    alpha = 1.0 - np.exp(-sigma * delta)                      # opacity of segment i
    T = np.exp(-np.concatenate([[0.0], np.cumsum(sigma * delta)[:-1]]))   # T_i = exp(−Σ_{j<i} σ_j δ_j)
    w = T * alpha                                             # w_i = T_i (1 − exp(−σ_i δ_i))
    return (w[:, None] * rgb).sum(axis=0), w

t = np.linspace(2.0, 6.0, 65)                                 # 64 bins between near and far planes
mid = 0.5 * (t[:-1] + t[1:])
sigma = np.where(np.abs(mid - 4.0) < 0.1, 50.0, 0.0)          # a thin, nearly opaque slab at depth 4
rgb = np.tile([0.9, 0.2, 0.1], (64, 1))
C, w = render_ray(sigma, rgb, t)
print(C.round(3), w.sum().round(4), (w * mid).sum().round(2))   # colour, opacity, expected depth
assert np.isclose(w.sum(), 1 - np.exp(-(sigma * np.diff(t)).sum()))   # Σ w_i = 1 − T_{N+1}

The method: making the MLP sharp

Positional encoding. Networks trained by gradient descent are biased towards low-frequency functions (spectral bias, Rahaman et al. 2019). Fed raw \((x, y, z)\), the MLP renders a blurred scene. NeRF first maps each coordinate \(p\), normalised to \([-1, 1]\), through

$$\gamma(p) = \big(\sin(2^0\pi p), \cos(2^0\pi p), \dots, \sin(2^{L-1}\pi p), \cos(2^{L-1}\pi p)\big),$$

with \(L = 10\) for position (60 numbers) and \(L = 4\) for direction (24). High frequencies are now available as inputs, so representing fine detail no longer requires the network to synthesise them. Tancik et al. (2020) explained why with the neural tangent kernel: Fourier features make the kernel stationary and tunable in bandwidth (⟶ L318).

View dependence, entering late. The MLP has 8 layers of 256 units with ReLU (the input re-injected at the fifth). It outputs \(\sigma\) and a 256-d feature from position alone; only then is \(\gamma(\mathbf d)\) concatenated and one 128-unit layer produces the colour. Density is view-independent by construction, a deliberate physical constraint: geometry does not change when you move your head, but specular highlights do. Without it, the network could explain each training view with its own inconsistent geometry.

Algorithm · hierarchical sampling
  1. Query a coarse network at \(N_c = 64\) stratified samples; compute weights \(w_i\).
  2. Normalise \(\hat w_i = w_i / \sum_j w_j\) into a piecewise-constant PDF along the ray.
  3. Draw \(N_f = 128\) more samples from it by inverse transform sampling: they land near surfaces.
  4. Query a fine network at all 192 samples and render. Train both: \(\mathcal L = \sum_{\mathbf r} \|\hat C_c(\mathbf r) - C(\mathbf r)\|^2 + \|\hat C_f(\mathbf r) - C(\mathbf r)\|^2\).

Evidence

On synthetic Blender scenes and real forward-facing captures (camera poses from COLMAP), NeRF beats Scene Representation Networks, Neural Volumes and LLFF on PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, by the authors' report, often by a wide margin and with far smaller storage than LLFF's voxel representations. The ablations remove one component at a time: without positional encoding the renders are blurry; without view dependence, specular surfaces are wrong; without hierarchical sampling, quality drops at equal sample counts. Training takes roughly 100–300k iterations, about 1–2 days on one V100, per scene.

Limits & what broke
  • One scene per network, days per scene. Nothing transfers between scenes, and fitting takes a day or two. Rendering one 800×800 image needs 800·800·192 ≈ 123 million MLP evaluations. This limitation spawned a field: Instant-NGP (L105) cut training to seconds; 3D Gaussian Splatting (L106) made rendering real-time.
  • Assumptions. Static scene, fixed lighting baked into colour, and accurate known poses. NeRF in the Wild relaxed appearance changes; BARF jointly optimised poses.
  • Aliasing. Point samples along infinitely thin rays alias when cameras change distance; Mip-NeRF (Barron et al. 2021) renders conical frustums with integrated encodings.
  • Floaters with few views. With sparse inputs the density field invents semi-transparent blobs that explain training pixels but not new views.

Bridge

NeRF's 8×256 MLP is both the memory of the scene and the reason it is slow: every sample pays for the whole network. Tomorrow's paper moves the memory into a multiresolution hash table of learned features and shrinks the MLP to almost nothing, and training falls from days to seconds.

Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (Instant-NGP)

40m videoLevel 3

Müller, Evans, Schied & Keller, SIGGRAPH 2022 (arXiv:2201.05989)

Store learnable feature vectors in a multiresolution set of hash tables indexed by grid corners, let gradient descent sort out hash collisions, and a tiny MLP in fused GPU kernels suffices: NeRF-quality scenes train in seconds instead of days.

Cold open

NeRF (L104) needed a day or two per scene. With this paper's released code, a NeRF of comparable quality appears on screen within seconds and keeps sharpening while you watch. The speedup is several orders of magnitude, and it comes from two places in roughly equal measure: a data structure, and a lot of GPU engineering.

The problem

In NeRF the MLP is the whole memory of the scene, so every one of the hundreds of samples per ray pays for a 256-wide, 8-layer network. Moving the memory into an explicit grid of learnable features (a parametric encoding) lets the MLP shrink, but dense 3D grids waste memory on empty space and grow as \(N^3\). Sparse structures (octrees, as in NSVF) fix that but must be pruned and subdivided during training, which is complex and task-specific. The goal was an encoding that is compact, adaptive, and needs no structural updates.

The method: multiresolution hash encoding

Definition · hash encoding

Use \(L\) grid levels with resolutions growing geometrically from \(N_{\min}\) to \(N_{\max}\):

$$N_l = \big\lfloor N_{\min}\, b^{\,l} \big\rfloor, \qquad b = \exp\!\Big(\frac{\ln N_{\max} - \ln N_{\min}}{L - 1}\Big).$$

Each level owns a table of \(T\) feature vectors of dimension \(F\). For an input \(\mathbf x\), at each level find the \(2^d\) corners of its grid cell, look up each corner's feature vector, and interpolate them \(d\)-linearly at \(\mathbf x\). A corner with integer coordinates \(\mathbf i\) is mapped to a table row by the spatial hash

$$h(\mathbf i) = \Big(\bigoplus_{k=1}^{d} i_k \pi_k\Big) \bmod T, \qquad \pi_1 = 1,\ \pi_2 = 2{,}654{,}435{,}761,\ \pi_3 = 805{,}459{,}861,$$

with \(\oplus\) bitwise XOR. Coarse levels with \((N_l + 1)^d \le T\) are indexed one-to-one, with no hashing at all. The \(L\) interpolated vectors are concatenated (with extra inputs such as an encoded view direction) and fed to the MLP. Table entries and MLP weights are both trained by backpropagation.

import numpy as np

PRIMES = np.array([1, 2654435761, 805459861], dtype=np.uint32)   # pi_1, pi_2, pi_3 from the paper

def spatial_hash(ijk, T):
    """Integer grid corners (..., 3) -> table indices; uint32 products wrap mod 2^32."""
    h = np.zeros(ijk.shape[:-1], dtype=np.uint32)
    for d in range(3):
        h ^= ijk[..., d].astype(np.uint32) * PRIMES[d]
    return h % np.uint32(T)

L, T, F, N_min, N_max = 16, 2 ** 19, 2, 16, 2048        # N_max here is an example value
b = np.exp((np.log(N_max) - np.log(N_min)) / (L - 1))   # per-level growth factor, about 1.38
res = np.floor(N_min * b ** np.arange(L)).astype(np.int64)
print(res)                                              # 16, 22, 30, ... 2048
print("collision-free levels:", int(((res + 1) ** 3 <= T).sum()))   # coarse levels index 1:1
print("trainable features:", L * T * F)                 # about 16.8 million

x = np.array([0.30, 0.70, 0.50])                        # a point in the unit cube
corner = np.floor(x * res[-1]).astype(np.int64)         # its cell on the finest level
offsets = np.array([[i, j, k] for i in (0, 1) for j in (0, 1) for k in (0, 1)])
print(spatial_hash(corner + offsets, T))                # 8 table rows, interpolated trilinearly

With these settings 5 of 16 levels are collision-free. Corners that differ only in \(x\) land in adjacent rows, because \(\pi_1 = 1\): that choice is for cache coherence.

The method: why collisions are fine

Argument · collisions resolved by the gradient

Suppose two corners, one near a visible surface and one in empty space, share a table row \(\theta\). The row's gradient is the sum of the two points' contributions, \(\partial \mathcal L / \partial \theta = g_{\text{surface}} + g_{\text{empty}}\). Training rays hit the surface constantly and its samples carry large rendering weights (L104), while samples in empty space or behind surfaces get near-zero weight and near-zero gradient. So \(g_{\text{surface}}\) dominates, and the row converges to what the surface point needs. The unimportant point inherits a wrong feature, which costs almost nothing because almost nothing depends on it. Three things keep the damage small: coarse levels have no collisions; two points that collide at one fine level almost never collide at all of them; and the MLP sees all levels at once and can learn to discount an inconsistent one.

This is a beautiful piece of engineering reasoning: rather than paying for a collision-free structure that adapts to the scene, use a fixed structure and let the loss decide who gets each slot. The table size \(T\) is the one knob: larger tables mean fewer collisions and higher quality, at the cost of memory and cache misses.

The split of labour. The paper's defaults are \(L = 16\), \(F = 2\), \(N_{\min} = 16\), and \(T\) between \(2^{14}\) and \(2^{24}\) depending on the task, with \(2^{19}\) a typical NeRF setting. The MLP is tiny: two hidden layers of 64 neurons for most tasks; for NeRF, a one-hidden-layer density network and a two-hidden-layer colour network, with the view direction encoded by spherical harmonics. The encoding carries the capacity (where the detail is); the MLP carries the smoothness and the cross-level decoding.

The method: the systems half

Cover this honestly; it is as much a GPU paper as an ML paper. The MLP is evaluated by fully fused CUDA kernels (the authors' tiny-cuda-nn library): a 64-wide network is small enough that a whole block of inputs can be pushed through every layer within one kernel, keeping weights and activations in registers and shared memory instead of writing each layer's output to global memory. Hash lookups are independent per level and parallelise well, and tables are stored in half precision. For NeRF, ray marching skips empty space with an occupancy grid that is updated during training, so most network queries land near surfaces. Without these, the encoding alone would still be fast, but not “instant”.

Evidence

The same encoding is applied to four tasks: fitting a gigapixel image, a signed distance function, neural radiance caching for path tracing, and NeRF. In each it matches or beats task-specific encodings in quality at a fraction of the training time. The abstract reports training high-quality primitives in seconds and rendering in tens of milliseconds at 1920×1080. The comparisons against frequency (positional) encodings and dense grids at equal parameter budgets support the claim that the hash table, not just the CUDA code, is responsible for the quality per parameter.

Limits & what broke
  • Collisions are cheap, not free. They leave a noise floor of wrongly shared features that only a larger \(T\) lowers, which is why table size trades memory directly for quality.
  • Not a smaller scene. Tables of tens of megabytes per scene are larger than NeRF's few-megabyte MLP; speed was bought with memory.
  • Aliasing remains. Grids are sampled at points, so scale changes alias; Zip-NeRF (Barron et al. 2023) combined hash grids with anti-aliasing.
  • Hardware lock-in. Much of the speed lives in hand-written CUDA; re-implementations in high-level frameworks are markedly slower. The hardware is part of the algorithm.

Bridge

Instant-NGP keeps NeRF's ray marching and shrinks the network. Tomorrow's paper removes the network from the representation entirely: a scene becomes millions of explicit 3D Gaussians, rasterised instead of ray-marched, rendering in real time.

3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering

45m videoLevel 3

Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler & Drettakis, SIGGRAPH 2023 (arXiv:2308.04079)

Represent a scene as millions of explicit, anisotropic 3D Gaussians with opacity and view-dependent colour, render them with a differentiable tile-based rasteriser instead of ray marching, and grow or prune them during optimisation: NeRF-level quality at real-time frame rates, with no neural network in the representation.

Cold open

The best NeRF variant of 2022, Mip-NeRF 360, took about two days to train per scene and seconds to render a frame. This paper matches or beats its quality on the same benchmark, trains in well under an hour, and renders 1080p at real-time rates, often above 100 frames per second. The scene is a list of ellipsoids. The pendulum from explicit to implicit representations had swung back.

The problem

Radiance fields (L104) render by marching along every ray and querying a field at many samples, most of them in empty space. Instant-NGP (L105) and Plenoxels made the field cheap to query and training fast, but rendering was still a per-pixel march, too slow for real-time at high resolution on unbounded scenes. Point-based graphics, which rasterises primitives directly, had always been fast but produced holes and needed good geometry. The question was whether a point-like representation could be optimised from photos to NeRF quality.

The method: the primitive

Initialise from the sparse point cloud that structure-from-motion (COLMAP) produces anyway while estimating camera poses. Each point becomes a 3D Gaussian with

  • a mean \(\boldsymbol\mu \in \R^3\) and a covariance \(\Sigma\), so its footprint is \(G(\mathbf x) = \exp\!\big(-\tfrac12 (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\big)\);
  • an opacity \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) (a sigmoid of a free parameter);
  • colour as spherical-harmonic (SH) coefficients, up to degree 3: 16 coefficients per channel, 48 numbers. Evaluating the SH basis at the viewing direction gives view-dependent colour, the job NeRF gave to the late direction input, with no network.
Derivation · why Σ must be parameterised as R S Sᵀ Rᵀ

A covariance matrix must be symmetric positive semi-definite: \(\mathbf v^\top \Sigma \mathbf v \ge 0\) for all \(\mathbf v\). Gradient descent on its six free entries knows nothing about this and will happily step outside the PSD cone; the snippet below takes one small step from a valid, flat Gaussian and gets a negative eigenvalue, a “Gaussian” with imaginary extent. Instead, write

$$\Sigma = R S S^\top R^\top, \qquad S = \operatorname{diag}(s_1, s_2, s_3),$$

with \(R\) built from a unit quaternion \(\mathbf q\) (normalised from a free 4-vector) and \(s_k = e^{\ell_k}\) from free log-scales. Then for any \(\mathbf v\), \(\mathbf v^\top \Sigma \mathbf v = \|S^\top R^\top \mathbf v\|^2 \ge 0\), and \(\Sigma\) is positive definite whenever all \(s_k > 0\). Every value of the seven unconstrained parameters is a valid ellipsoid, so plain unconstrained optimisation stays inside the feasible set. Geometrically, \(S\) stretches a unit sphere along the axes and \(R\) rotates it. The paper derives the gradients with respect to \(\mathbf q\) and \(\mathbf s\) explicitly.

import numpy as np

def quat_to_rot(q):
    w, x, y, z = q / np.linalg.norm(q)              # normalise: any nonzero 4-vector is a rotation
    return np.array([[1 - 2 * (y * y + z * z), 2 * (x * y - w * z), 2 * (x * z + w * y)],
                     [2 * (x * y + w * z), 1 - 2 * (x * x + z * z), 2 * (y * z - w * x)],
                     [2 * (x * z - w * y), 2 * (y * z + w * x), 1 - 2 * (x * x + y * y)]])

def covariance(q, log_s):
    M = quat_to_rot(q) @ np.diag(np.exp(log_s))     # exp keeps the three scales positive
    return M @ M.T                                  # Σ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for _ in range(1000):                               # unconstrained parameters, always a valid Σ
    assert np.linalg.eigvalsh(covariance(rng.normal(size=4), rng.normal(size=3))).min() > 0

Sigma = np.diag([1.0, 1.0, 0.01])                   # optimising Σ's six entries directly instead:
step = np.zeros((3, 3))
step[0, 2] = step[2, 0] = 0.2                       # one small, symmetric gradient step
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(Sigma + step).min())       # about -0.029: no longer a covariance matrix

The method: rasterising and optimising

Projection. For a view with transform \(W\), the 2D footprint of a Gaussian has covariance \(\Sigma' = J W \Sigma W^\top J^\top\), where \(J\) is the Jacobian of an affine approximation to the perspective projection (EWA splatting, Zwicker et al. 2001); drop the depth row and column to get a 2×2 matrix.

Image formation. A pixel's colour is front-to-back alpha blending of the Gaussians covering it, sorted by depth: \(C = \sum_i \mathbf c_i \alpha_i' \prod_{j<i}(1 - \alpha_j')\), where \(\alpha_i'\) is the Gaussian's opacity times its 2D footprint at that pixel. This is NeRF's discrete rendering equation exactly; only the way the \(\alpha_i\) and \(\mathbf c_i\) along the ray are produced has changed.

Algorithm · tile-based differentiable rasteriser
  1. Split the screen into 16×16-pixel tiles. Cull Gaussians outside the view frustum, and find which tiles each remaining 2D footprint overlaps (using a 99% confidence ellipse).
  2. Emit one copy of each Gaussian per overlapped tile, with a key combining tile index (high bits) and view-space depth (low bits). Sort all keys with one GPU radix sort.
  3. Per tile, one thread block loads the sorted list; each thread (pixel) blends front to back and stops once accumulated opacity saturates.
  4. Backward pass: traverse the same lists back to front, recovering intermediate transmittances from the final one instead of storing them per Gaussian.

The sort is the trick that makes it fast, and it is an approximation: Gaussians are ordered once per tile by their centre depth, not per pixel by the depth at which each pixel crosses them. Overlapping, intersecting Gaussians can be blended in the wrong order, which shows up as “popping” when the camera moves and the order flips. The authors note this; at convergence it is rarely visible.

Training and adaptive density control. Adam on \(\mathcal L = (1 - \lambda)\mathcal L_1 + \lambda \mathcal L_{\text{D-SSIM}}\) with \(\lambda = 0.2\). Periodically, Gaussians with a large average view-space positional gradient are densified: small ones are cloned (under-reconstruction, where geometry is missing) and large ones split into two with scales divided by 1.6 (over-reconstruction, where one blob covers detail). Nearly transparent Gaussians are pruned, and opacities are periodically reset near zero so that floaters must re-earn their opacity or be removed.

Evidence

On Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples and Deep Blending scenes, the authors report quality on par with or better than Mip-NeRF 360 on PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, training times of tens of minutes against roughly two days, and real-time rendering (at least 30 fps at 1080p, typically much more), versus Instant-NGP and Plenoxels which train fast but render slower and at lower quality on unbounded scenes. A shorter schedule trades some quality for training in a few minutes. Ablations show the SfM initialisation, both densification modes, anisotropic covariances and the unlimited number of Gaussians receiving gradients each matter.

Limits & what broke
  • Storage is the real drawback. Millions of Gaussians at 59 floats each means hundreds of megabytes per scene, against a few megabytes for a NeRF MLP. A wave of compression papers (quantised SH, pruning, codebooks) followed.
  • Approximate sorting. Popping artifacts and order errors for overlapping Gaussians; later work sorts per pixel or avoids sorting.
  • Aliasing and geometry. Zooming produces artifacts (fixed by Mip-Splatting), and the Gaussians are not a clean surface, so meshes extracted from them are poor; 2D Gaussian splatting and SuGaR target that.
  • Needs SfM. Initialisation and poses come from COLMAP; where SfM fails (texture-less or few views), splatting struggles too.

Bridge

Explicit primitives (points, surfels, EWA splats) gave way to an implicit MLP in 2020, and three years later explicit primitives won again, now optimised by the same differentiable rendering. Tomorrow's paper closes the 3D and motion arc: RAFT estimates dense optical flow, the input two-stream networks had to compute by hand, with a learned iterative optimiser.

RAFT: Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms for Optical Flow

35m videoLevel 3

Teed & Deng, ECCV 2020 (arXiv:2003.12039)

Compute the correlation between every pixel of one frame and every pixel of the next once, then refine a single high-resolution flow field with a recurrent update operator whose weights are shared across iterations: a learned, unrolled optimiser.

Cold open

Two-stream networks (⟵ L98) depended on optical flow computed by a 2004 variational method, slowly, offline. RAFT, trained only on synthetic data, cut the best published error on the Sintel benchmark's final pass by about 30% and on KITTI by about 16%, while running at practical speeds. It became the default flow estimator, and its design, iterative updates with shared weights, spread to stereo, SLAM and scene flow.

The problem

Optical flow assigns every pixel of frame 1 a 2D displacement to its match in frame 2. Classical methods minimise an energy (data term plus smoothness term) by iterative optimisation. Deep methods started with FlowNet (Dosovitskiy et al. 2015), a CNN regressing flow directly, and converged on coarse-to-fine designs such as PWC-Net: estimate flow at low resolution, warp, and refine at each finer level using a local cost volume. Coarse-to-fine has a classic failure: a small, fast-moving object vanishes at the coarse levels where large motions are estimated, and an error made at a coarse level is hard to undo at a fine one.

The method

Features and the all-pairs volume. A shared convolutional encoder \(g_\theta\) maps both frames to \(D = 256\)-dimensional features at 1/8 resolution. The correlation volume is the dot product of every frame-1 feature with every frame-2 feature,

$$C_{ijkl} = \sum_{h=1}^{D} g_\theta(I_1)_{ijh} \cdot g_\theta(I_2)_{klh}, \qquad C \in \R^{H \times W \times H \times W},$$

computed as one matrix multiply. The volume is then average-pooled over its last two dimensions with kernels 1, 2, 4 and 8, giving a four-level pyramid. The frame-1 dimensions stay at full 1/8 resolution, so every pixel keeps its own row, small fast objects included, while frame-2 dimensions become coarse enough to represent large displacements.

import numpy as np

H, W, D = 55, 128, 256                        # 1/8-resolution features of a 1024x440 frame pair
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
f1 = rng.normal(size=(H * W, D)).astype(np.float32)
f2 = rng.normal(size=(H * W, D)).astype(np.float32)

corr = (f1 @ f2.T).reshape(H, W, H, W)        # C[i,j,k,l] = <g1[i,j], g2[k,l]>: one matmul, once
print(corr.shape, f"{corr.nbytes / 2**20:.0f} MiB")          # (55, 128, 55, 128), 189 MiB

pyramid = [corr]                              # pool only the last two (frame-2) dimensions
for _ in range(3):
    c = pyramid[-1]
    h, w = c.shape[2] // 2, c.shape[3] // 2
    pyramid.append(c[:, :, :2 * h, :2 * w].reshape(H, W, h, 2, w, 2).mean(axis=(3, 5)))
print([p.shape for p in pyramid])             # frame-1 dims stay at full 1/8 resolution

full = (440 * 1024) ** 2 * 4 / 2**30          # the same volume at full resolution, fp32
print(f"full resolution would need {full:,.0f} GiB")
Memory analysis

A Sintel frame is 1024×436, padded to 1024×440. At 1/8 resolution that is \(128 \times 55 = 7{,}040\) pixels, so the volume has \(7{,}040^2 \approx 4.96 \times 10^7\) entries: about 190 MB in fp32, and the pyramid adds roughly a third more. The same volume at full resolution would have \(450{,}560^2 \approx 2 \times 10^{11}\) entries, about 750 GiB. The 1/8 resolution is not a detail; it is what makes “all pairs” affordable. The paper also describes computing lookups on demand, trading memory for compute at higher resolutions.

Lookup. Given the current flow \(\mathbf f_k\), each frame-1 pixel \(\mathbf x\) is mapped to its estimated match \(\mathbf x' = \mathbf x + \mathbf f_k(\mathbf x)\). From every pyramid level, the operator bilinearly samples correlations on a small grid of offsets of radius \(r\) around \(\mathbf x'\) (scaled to that level), and concatenates them. With the same radius at every level, coarse levels see a wide neighbourhood and fine levels a precise one.

The update operator. A convolutional GRU takes the looked-up correlations, the current flow and context features (from a second encoder applied to frame 1, which also initialises the hidden state), and outputs an update: \(\mathbf f_{k+1} = \mathbf f_k + \Delta\mathbf f\), starting from \(\mathbf f_0 = 0\). The same weights are applied at every iteration. That makes it a learned optimiser: like an iterative energy minimiser it repeatedly evaluates a local cost (the correlations around the current estimate) and takes a step, but the step rule is learned. Unrolling an optimisation into a network with tied weights is the idea behind LISTA, the learned version of the sparse-coding inference of L54. A final convex upsampling predicts, for each full-resolution pixel, softmax weights over its 3×3 coarse neighbours.

Definition · the sequence loss

Every iterate is supervised, with geometrically increasing weight: \(\mathcal L = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \gamma^{N-i} \|\mathbf f_{\text{gt}} - \mathbf f_i\|_1\), \(\gamma = 0.8\). Early iterates are pushed to be useful; the last matters most.

Evidence

The abstract reports an end-point error of 2.855 pixels on Sintel (final pass), a 30% reduction from the best published 4.098, and an F1-all error of 5.10% on KITTI, a 16% reduction from 6.10%. Trained only on synthetic FlyingChairs and FlyingThings, it generalises to Sintel and KITTI better than prior methods. The ablations support the main choices: tied weights do at least as well as untied ones with far fewer parameters, the correlation pyramid and a larger lookup radius help, the GRU beats a plain convolutional update, and convex upsampling beats bilinear. Running more iterations at test time than in training keeps improving or plateaus rather than diverging, as one would hope from an optimiser. Why iterative refinement generalises better than coarse-to-fine warping is argued rather than proven: the model never commits to a coarse estimate it cannot revise, and it always works at a single high resolution.

Limits & what broke
  • Quadratic memory. The volume grows with the square of the pixel count; 4K video needs on-demand lookups or cropping.
  • Local lookups. Each iteration sees only a radius around the current estimate; occluded or very large motions benefit from global reasoning, which GMA and FlowFormer added with attention.
  • Sequential iterations. Twelve to dozens of GRU steps cost latency; later variants (e.g. SEA-RAFT) cut iterations with better initialisation.
  • The idea outgrew flow. The same authors applied the recipe to stereo (RAFT-Stereo) and visual SLAM (DROID-SLAM).

Bridge

This closes the motion and 3D arc. The next four lessons step back two decades: before learning, vision ran on hand-designed features, and SIFT's descriptor, built from gradient histograms in a 4×4 grid of cells, is the ancestor of much of what came later.

Distinctive Image Features from Scale-Invariant Keypoints (SIFT)

45m videoLevel 1

Lowe, IJCV 2004

Find points that are stable across scale by searching for extrema of a difference-of-Gaussians scale space, give each a canonical scale and orientation, describe the patch around it with a 4×4 grid of gradient-orientation histograms, and match with a ratio test.

Cold open

Before deep learning, this was the vision paper: tens of thousands of citations, the engine of panorama stitching, object recognition and structure-from-motion. It is still the default feature extractor in COLMAP, the reconstruction tool that produced the camera poses for NeRF and Gaussian splatting (L104, L106). Every step is designed by hand, and every design choice is justified by an experiment.

The problem

To match two photos of the same object taken from different distances, angles and lighting, you need local features that are found again at the same physical locations (repeatable) and described in a way that tolerates the change (invariant) yet tells different places apart (distinctive). Harris corners are repeatable under rotation but not scale; raw-pixel correlation fails under rotation, scale and illumination.

The method: detection in scale space

Build the scale space \(L(x, y, \sigma) = G(x, y, \sigma) * I(x, y)\) and the difference of Gaussians

$$D(x, y, \sigma) = L(x, y, k\sigma) - L(x, y, \sigma).$$
Derivation · DoG approximates the scale-normalised Laplacian

The Gaussian satisfies the heat equation \(\partial G / \partial \sigma = \sigma \nabla^2 G\). Approximate the derivative by a finite difference between nearby scales:

$$\sigma \nabla^2 G \approx \frac{G(k\sigma) - G(\sigma)}{k\sigma - \sigma} \;\Rightarrow\; G(k\sigma) - G(\sigma) \approx (k - 1)\,\sigma^2 \nabla^2 G.$$

\(\sigma^2 \nabla^2 G\) is exactly the scale-normalised Laplacian of Gaussian that Lindeberg showed gives true scale invariance. The factor \(k - 1\) is the same at every scale, so it does not move the extrema. DoG is therefore nearly free: you need the blurred images anyway, and one subtraction replaces a second-derivative filter.

Each octave (a doubling of \(\sigma\)) is split into \(s\) intervals, \(k = 2^{1/s}\); Lowe's experiments pick \(s = 3\) and a base \(\sigma = 1.6\), and doubling the input image first increases the number of stable keypoints. A sample is a candidate if it is larger or smaller than all 26 neighbours: 8 at its own scale and 9 in each adjacent scale.

Localisation. Fit a quadratic through the samples: \(D(\mathbf x) \approx D + \nabla D^\top \mathbf x + \tfrac12 \mathbf x^\top \nabla^2 D\, \mathbf x\), so the sub-pixel, sub-scale extremum is at \(\hat{\mathbf x} = -(\nabla^2 D)^{-1} \nabla D\). Reject low-contrast points with \(|D(\hat{\mathbf x})| < 0.03\) (image values in [0, 1]).

Edge rejection via the Hessian ratio

DoG responds strongly along edges, where a point is well localised across the edge but not along it. Let \(H = \begin{pmatrix} D_{xx} & D_{xy} \\ D_{xy} & D_{yy} \end{pmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(\alpha \ge \beta\) (principal curvatures) and \(\alpha = r\beta\). Then

$$\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(H)^2}{\operatorname{Det}(H)} = \frac{(\alpha + \beta)^2}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{(r + 1)^2}{r},$$

which is minimal at \(r = 1\) and increasing for \(r > 1\). Keep the point only if \(\operatorname{Tr}^2/\operatorname{Det} < (r+1)^2/r\) with \(r = 10\), i.e. below 12.1, and reject it if the determinant is negative. No eigen-decomposition is needed: trace and determinant come straight from three second differences.

The method: orientation and descriptor

Orientation. Around the keypoint, at its scale, compute gradient magnitudes and orientations and accumulate a 36-bin (10°) histogram weighted by magnitude and a Gaussian window. The peak sets the orientation; any other peak within 80% of the highest creates an additional keypoint at the same location with that orientation. Everything after this is computed relative to this orientation, which buys rotation invariance.

The final normalisation handles illumination. A contrast change multiplies all gradients and is removed by unit normalisation; a brightness offset does not affect gradients at all. Non-linear effects such as camera saturation change magnitudes far more than orientations, so clipping at 0.2 and renormalising reduces the influence of a few large gradients relative to the distribution of orientations.

Draw this descriptor carefully: it is the design ancestor of much that follows. Local gradient-orientation histograms over a grid of cells became HOG tomorrow (L109); the first layers of trained CNNs learn oriented gradient-like filters; and a ViT's patch embedding (L115) is a learned linear summary of a patch, where SIFT's summary was designed.

The method: matching

Match each descriptor to its nearest neighbour in Euclidean distance, but decide whether to trust the match with a relative threshold: accept only if the distance to the closest neighbour is less than 0.8 times the distance to the second closest. Lowe reports that this rejects about 90% of false matches while discarding less than 5% of correct ones.

import numpy as np

def ratio_test(query, candidates, ratio=0.8):
    d = np.sort(np.linalg.norm(candidates - query, axis=1))
    return f"best {d[0]:.3f}  ratio {d[0] / d[1]:.2f}  accept {d[0] < ratio * d[1]}"

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
unit = lambda v: v / np.linalg.norm(v, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
q = unit(rng.random(128))                                   # a descriptor in image 1
others = unit(rng.random((500, 128)))                       # unrelated descriptors in image 2
unique = np.vstack([others, unit(q + 0.05 * rng.random(128))])        # its one true match
repeated = np.vstack([unique, unit(q + 0.05 * rng.random(128))])      # plus a look-alike window
print(ratio_test(q, unique))      # best 0.172, ratio 0.29: accept
print(ratio_test(q, repeated))    # best 0.172, ratio 0.99: reject as ambiguous

The best distance is identical in both cases; only the comparison with the runner-up separates a distinctive match from a repeated texture. An absolute threshold cannot make that distinction, because how close a correct match is varies enormously across features.

For object recognition the paper adds approximate nearest-neighbour search (best-bin-first on a k-d tree), Hough-transform clustering of matches that agree on a pose, and a least-squares affine verification; three agreeing matches suffice to detect an object.

Evidence

Every parameter comes with a curve: repeatability against the number of scales per octave (peaking around three), against the prior smoothing \(\sigma\), and descriptor matching accuracy against the number of cells and orientation bins (4×4×8 near the best trade-off between distinctiveness and robustness). The keypoints remain stable under substantial added noise, rotation, scale change and affine distortion, and matching degrades slowly as the database of keypoints grows very large. The recognition examples find small, heavily occluded objects in cluttered scenes.

Limits & what broke
  • Speed. Building the pyramid and 128-D descriptors is slow for real-time use; SURF and binary descriptors such as ORB traded accuracy for speed.
  • Limited affine invariance. Large out-of-plane rotations break matching; affine-adapted detectors and ASIFT followed.
  • Patents. SIFT was patented until 2020, which kept it out of OpenCV's main modules and pushed many systems to alternatives.
  • Learned features, and an honest minute. SuperPoint, SuperGlue and LightGlue outperform SIFT under hard conditions such as day-to-night changes and wide baselines. Yet the large benchmark of Jin et al. (2021) found that a carefully tuned SIFT pipeline remains competitive with many learned methods once RANSAC and matching are tuned properly, and SIFT is still the default in production reconstruction. Tune the baseline before believing the delta.

Bridge

SIFT describes sparse keypoints. Tomorrow, Dalal and Triggs take the same gradient histograms, compute them densely over a whole detection window, normalise them over overlapping blocks, and feed them to a linear SVM: the detection pipeline that R-CNN later replaced.

Histograms of Oriented Gradients for Human Detection

30m videoLevel 1

Dalal & Triggs, CVPR 2005

Describe a detection window densely with locally normalised histograms of gradient orientation, classify it with a linear SVM, and justify every design choice with a controlled experiment: the result was the strongest pedestrian detector of its decade and the template for feature-based detection.

Cold open

Read this paper for its ablations. Gradient filter, orientation bins, signed or unsigned angles, cell size, block size, overlap, normalisation scheme, window margin, SVM kernel: each gets its own curve, and the final detector is simply the best setting of each. It is feature engineering at its best, and a model of how to report design choices.

The problem

Detecting people is hard for appearance-based methods: clothing, pose, lighting and background vary enormously, while the useful signal, the silhouette, is mostly in the shape of local edges. Existing approaches used wavelets (Haar-like features, as in L110), sparse keypoints (SIFT-style, L108), or shape contexts. The MIT pedestrian database was close to solved, so the authors also built a harder one: INRIA Person, with 1,805 64×128 images of people in varied poses and backgrounds.

The method

  • Gradients. The simplest centred mask, \([-1, 0, 1]\), with no Gaussian smoothing, works best; Sobel, larger masks and pre-smoothing all hurt, because they blur exactly the fine edge information the histograms need. For colour images, take the channel with the largest gradient at each pixel.
  • Orientation binning. Each pixel votes for its orientation with weight equal to its gradient magnitude, interpolated bilinearly between neighbouring bins (and spatially between cells). Nine bins over 0–180° work best for people: unsigned orientation, because whether a person is darker or lighter than the background is arbitrary.
  • Cells and blocks. Cells of 8×8 pixels pool votes; blocks of 2×2 cells are the unit of contrast normalisation, with a Gaussian window downweighting pixels near the block edge.
Definition · block normalisation schemes

Let \(\mathbf v\) be a block's concatenated histograms and \(\epsilon\) a small constant. The paper compares

$$\text{L2: } \frac{\mathbf v}{\sqrt{\|\mathbf v\|_2^2 + \epsilon^2}}, \qquad \text{L1: } \frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|_1 + \epsilon}, \qquad \text{L1-sqrt: } \sqrt{\frac{\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|_1 + \epsilon}},$$

and L2-Hys: L2-normalise, clip every entry at 0.2, renormalise (the same trick as SIFT's descriptor). L2, L2-Hys and L1-sqrt perform about equally; plain L1 is clearly worse; and, by the authors' account, omitting normalisation costs about 27% at \(10^{-4}\) false positives per window, more than any other single choice.

Overlap is the ablation that mattered most. Normalising each cell several times, relative to each of the neighbourhoods it belongs to, looks redundant. It improves performance by several percent at \(10^{-4}\) FPPW in their experiments: a cell's contrast is judged against different surroundings, which makes the descriptor robust to local illumination changes at edges.

Classifier and search. A linear SVM (⟵ L14) scores each window; a Gaussian-kernel SVM gains a little accuracy at a much higher run-time cost. Detection slides the window over an image pyramid and merges overlapping detections with non-maximum suppression. The window needs margin: about 16 pixels of context around the person; shrinking it hurts noticeably.

Algorithm · hard negative mining
  1. Train an SVM on the positive windows and a random sample of windows from person-free images.
  2. Scan every person-free training image exhaustively with that detector; collect all its false positives (“hard examples”).
  3. Add them to the negatives and retrain.

The space of negatives is effectively infinite; random sampling wastes the training budget on easy ones. This loop, a precursor of focal loss's reweighting of easy examples (L87), improves the detector noticeably in their experiments.

Evidence

Results are reported as detection error trade-off (DET) curves, miss rate against false positives per window on log–log axes. On MIT the HOG detector is nearly perfect; on the new INRIA set it reduces the false-positive rate by more than an order of magnitude relative to the Haar-wavelet-based detector at comparable miss rates, by the authors' report. Each design ablation moves the curve in the direction the bullet points above describe. What the evidence does not show is performance on full images: per-window evaluation ignores how detections are merged and what happens near window borders.

Limits & what broke
  • The metric was misleading. Dollár et al. (2012) showed that per-window evaluation (FPPW) does not reliably predict per-image performance, and moved the field to false positives per image.
  • One rigid template. A single linear filter over the window cannot handle articulation. Deformable part models (Felzenszwalb, McAllester & Ramanan 2008; Felzenszwalb et al. 2010) added a root HOG filter, part filters on a finer grid, deformation costs and latent-SVM training, and dominated PASCAL VOC for several years.
  • Replaced by learned features. The pipeline, dense features then linear classifier then sliding window, is exactly what R-CNN (⟵ L82) replaced, keeping the SVM and swapping HOG for CNN features.
  • HOG came back. MaskFeat (Wei et al. 2022) used HOG of masked patches as the prediction target for self-supervised ViTs (compare L118–L119).

Bridge

HOG made accurate detection possible but not fast: scanning thousands of windows with a 3,780-D descriptor is expensive. Tomorrow's paper, four years older, solved speed instead, detecting faces in real time on a 2001 laptop CPU with rectangle sums and a cascade that rejects most windows after a couple of features.

Rapid Object Detection using a Boosted Cascade of Simple Features (Viola–Jones)

35m videoLevel 1

Viola & Jones, CVPR 2001

Three ideas make real-time face detection possible: rectangle features computed in constant time from an integral image, AdaBoost used to select a few useful features out of hundreds of thousands, and a cascade of increasingly complex classifiers that discards most windows after evaluating only a couple of features.

Cold open

In 2001 this detector found faces in 384×288 images at about 15 frames per second on a 700 MHz Pentium III, with accuracy comparable to the best neural-network detectors of the time and roughly 15 times faster than Rowley, Baluja and Kanade's. It shipped in cameras and in OpenCV, and the attentional cascade is the ancestor of every early-exit network.

The problem

A detector must classify every window at every position and scale: hundreds of thousands per image, nearly all of them not faces. Accurate classifiers of the time were too slow to run that many times, and fast ones were too weak. The insight is that the problem is wildly asymmetric: you can afford an expensive decision for the rare promising window if you can reject the overwhelming majority almost for free.

The method: features and the integral image

Features are differences of sums over adjacent rectangles: two-rectangle (edge-like), three-rectangle (line-like) and four-rectangle (diagonal). In a 24×24 base window, every position, size and type gives an exhaustive set of over 180,000 features, far more than the 576 pixels.

Derivation · the integral image

Define \(ii(x, y) = \sum_{x' \le x,\ y' \le y} i(x', y')\). It is computed in one pass with the recurrences \(s(x, y) = s(x, y-1) + i(x, y)\) and \(ii(x, y) = ii(x-1, y) + s(x, y)\). In the figure, \(ii(1) = A\), \(ii(2) = A + B\), \(ii(3) = A + C\), \(ii(4) = A + B + C + D\), so

$$D = ii(4) - ii(2) - ii(3) + ii(1).$$

Adjacent rectangles share corners: a two-rectangle feature needs six lookups, a three-rectangle feature eight, a four-rectangle feature nine. Because cost does not depend on size, the detector is scanned across scales by scaling the features, not by building an image pyramid. Variance normalisation of each window uses a second integral image of squared pixels.

import numpy as np

def integral_image(img):
    ii = np.zeros((img.shape[0] + 1, img.shape[1] + 1))   # a row and column of zeros in front
    ii[1:, 1:] = img.cumsum(axis=0).cumsum(axis=1)
    return ii

def rect_sum(ii, r, c, h, w):                    # sum of img[r:r+h, c:c+w]: four lookups
    return ii[r + h, c + w] - ii[r, c + w] - ii[r + h, c] + ii[r, c]

img = np.random.default_rng(0).random((24, 24))  # one 24 x 24 detection window
ii = integral_image(img)
assert np.isclose(rect_sum(ii, 3, 5, 6, 8), img[3:9, 5:13].sum())

def two_rect(ii, r, c, h, w):                    # upper band minus the band below it
    return rect_sum(ii, r, c, h, w) - rect_sum(ii, r + h, c, h, w)   # 6 distinct lookups
print(round(two_rect(ii, 4, 2, 4, 20), 3))

D, F = 0.99 ** 10, 0.30 ** 10                    # 10 stages: keep 99% of faces, 30% of non-faces each
print(f"cascade: detection {D:.3f}, false positives {F:.1e}")   # 0.904 and 5.9e-06

The method: boosting as feature selection

Each weak classifier is a decision stump on one feature: \(h_j(x) = 1\) if \(p_j f_j(x) < p_j \theta_j\), else 0, with a threshold \(\theta_j\) and a parity \(p_j\). AdaBoost (⟵ L11) runs for \(T\) rounds; in each round it trains a stump for every feature on the current example weights and keeps the one with the lowest weighted error \(\varepsilon_t\). So boosting is the feature selector: after \(T\) rounds, \(T\) features out of 180,000 have been chosen.

Algorithm · AdaBoost round, as in the paper
  1. Normalise the weights \(w_{t,i}\) to sum to 1.
  2. For each feature, fit the best stump; pick the stump \(h_t\) with minimal \(\varepsilon_t = \sum_i w_{t,i} |h(x_i) - y_i|\).
  3. Update \(w_{t+1,i} = w_{t,i}\, \beta_t^{1 - e_i}\), with \(\beta_t = \varepsilon_t / (1 - \varepsilon_t)\) and \(e_i = 0\) if \(x_i\) was classified correctly: correct examples are down-weighted.
  4. Final classifier: \(h(x) = 1\) if \(\sum_t \alpha_t h_t(x) \ge \tfrac12 \sum_t \alpha_t\), with \(\alpha_t = \log(1/\beta_t)\).

The first two features AdaBoost selects are interpretable: the eye region is darker than the upper cheeks, and the eyes are darker than the bridge of the nose.

The method: the attentional cascade

Arrange boosted classifiers in a sequence. A window that any stage rejects is discarded immediately; only windows that pass every stage are reported. Each stage's threshold is lowered so it keeps almost all faces, accepting many false positives, and each stage is trained on the non-faces that passed the stages before it, so later stages see progressively harder negatives (the same idea as hard negative mining, L109).

Derivation · cascade rates multiply

A face is detected only if every stage passes it, and a non-face is a false positive only if every stage passes it. With \(K\) stages of detection rates \(d_i\) and false-positive rates \(f_i\):

$$D = \prod_{i=1}^{K} d_i, \qquad F = \prod_{i=1}^{K} f_i .$$

Ten stages with \(d_i = 0.99\) and \(f_i = 0.3\) give \(D = 0.99^{10} \approx 0.90\) and \(F = 0.3^{10} \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-6}\): weak stages, multiplied, give a very selective detector. The expected cost per window is \(N = n_1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} n_i \prod_{j<i} p_j\), where \(n_i\) is the number of features in stage \(i\) and \(p_j\) the fraction of windows stage \(j\) passes. Since almost every window is a non-face, \(p_j \approx f_j\), and the sum is dominated by the first, cheapest stages.

Evidence

The final detector has 38 stages and over 6,000 features, yet evaluates about 10 features per window on average on the paper's test set. On the MIT+CMU frontal face benchmark its ROC curve is comparable to the neural-network and Bayesian detectors of Rowley et al. and Schneiderman & Kanade, at a small fraction of the computation. The evidence for the cascade is the speed; the evidence for boosting-as-selection is that a classifier with a few hundred features already performs well.

Limits & what broke
  • Frontal, upright faces. Rotation and profile views need separately trained detectors; Haar features capture little beyond coarse intensity layout.
  • Slow training. Every boosting round evaluates every feature on every example; building the full cascade took a very long time on 2001 hardware.
  • Superseded for accuracy. HOG-based detectors and then CNN face detectors (MTCNN, RetinaFace) are far more robust to pose, occlusion and lighting. Haar cascades survive as a cheap baseline in OpenCV and on low-power devices.
  • The idea survived. Spend computation where it is needed: early-exit networks, cascaded detectors, and model cascades that route easy inputs to small models all reuse the attentional cascade's logic.

Bridge

Three hand-designed methods in a row: the field ran on features like these until a dataset made learning them possible. Tomorrow's paper is that dataset, ImageNet, the subject of this series' most repeated theme: data quality and scale beat architecture.

ImageNet: A Large-Scale Hierarchical Image Database

35m videoLevel 1

Deng, Dong, Socher, Li, Li & Fei-Fei, CVPR 2009

Populate the noun hierarchy of WordNet with hundreds of clean, diverse images per concept, collected from search engines and verified by crowd workers with a consensus protocol: a dataset large and clean enough that it, more than any architecture, made deep learning in vision possible.

Cold open

The architecture papers of the last two months (L61 onward) all report one number first: ImageNet accuracy. AlexNet's 2012 win, a top-5 error of 15.3% against 26.2% for the runner-up, is usually told as an architecture story. It is at least as much a data story: nothing before ImageNet had over a million labelled training images, and a 60-million-parameter network would have simply memorised anything smaller. This paper is a CVPR poster about building a database.

The problem

Vision datasets in 2009 were small or noisy. Caltech-101 and Caltech-256 had tens of images per class, centred and uncluttered; PASCAL VOC had 20 classes; LabelMe had rich annotations but few images per concept; Tiny Images had 80 million 32×32 images gathered by search, with labels that were largely wrong. Models were tuned to these benchmarks, and there was no way to know whether a method would scale to thousands of categories, or whether more data would help.

The method

WordNet as the label set. WordNet groups English words into synonym sets (synsets), each a distinct concept, linked by “is-a” relations; it has around 80,000 noun synsets. ImageNet attaches images to synsets, so the labels inherit a hierarchy: a husky is a working dog is a dog is a canine is a carnivore is a placental mammal. The 2009 paper reports 12 subtrees with 5,247 synsets and 3.2 million images, with a target of 500–1,000 clean images per synset for most of WordNet.

Algorithm · collecting and cleaning
  1. Candidates. Query several image search engines with each synset's words, expanded with words from parent synsets and with translations into other languages, to gather many more candidates than needed. Search precision is low, so most candidates are wrong.
  2. Verification on Amazon Mechanical Turk. Workers see a candidate image with the synset's definition and decide whether it contains the concept. Each image is judged by several workers.
  3. Dynamic consensus. Agreement needed depends on the concept: almost everyone recognises “cat”, far fewer can confirm “Burmese cat”. For each synset, a small set of images is labelled by many workers to estimate how many agreeing votes give a target confidence; the rest are labelled until that threshold is reached.

The paper measures the result along four axes: scale, hierarchy (how densely the tree is populated), accuracy (sampled precision of labels at different tree depths), and diversity. Diversity is measured cleverly: average all images of a synset and compress the average losslessly; diverse images give a blurry average that compresses to a small file, so smaller is better. ImageNet averages are blurrier than Caltech-101's, whose objects are centred and aligned.

Evidence

The paper's applications are modest by later standards: nearest-neighbour recognition, a tree-based classifier that exploits the hierarchy, and localisation. They show that more clean data helps and that the hierarchy can be used. The real evidence arrived later. ILSVRC (2010–2017), built on a 1,000-class subset of 1.28M training images, 50k validation and 100k test images, became the benchmark that selected the architectures of L61–L78, and ImageNet pretraining became the default initialisation for nearly all of vision. Top-5 error exists because a single label per image is ambiguous: many images contain several objects, and some categories overlap.

Scale, by the numbers

The full collection grew to about 14 million images in about 21,800 synsets (often called ImageNet-21K). ILSVRC-2012, the “ImageNet-1k” of most papers, is a subset: 1,000 classes and 1.28M training images. People forget that, and it matters: the ViT data-scaling result of L115 contrasts exactly these two, plus a much larger private set.

Limits & what broke
  • Label noise. Beyer et al. (2020), “Are we done with ImageNet?”, re-annotated the validation set with multiple labels per image (“ReaL”) and found that recent gains on the original labels transfer only partly: some of the progress was fitting the quirks of single noisy labels. Northcutt et al. (2021) estimated label errors in several percent of the validation set.
  • Generalisation to a new test set. Recht et al. (2019), “Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?”, rebuilt a test set following the original protocol (ImageNetV2). Accuracy dropped by 11–14 points for a broad range of models, yet their ranking was preserved. Even a careful replication of the data process is a distribution shift.
  • People, faces and consent. In 2019–2020 the ImageNet team audited the “person” subtree of the full dataset, removing categories judged offensive or not visually identifiable (Yang et al. 2020), and later studied blurring faces in the ILSVRC images, finding only a small accuracy cost (Yang et al. 2022). The images were scraped without the consent of the people in them.
  • Bias in coverage. Over a hundred dog breeds but few everyday objects from many parts of the world; the photos skew towards certain countries and photographic conventions.

Bridge

ImageNet made large models trainable; the next two papers make them generalise better on it with almost no cost, by training on images that do not exist: convex mixtures of pairs of images and their labels.

mixup: Beyond Empirical Risk Minimization

30m videoLevel 2

Zhang, Cisse, Dauphin & Lopez-Paz, ICLR 2018 (arXiv:1710.09412)

Train on convex combinations of random pairs of examples and of their labels; this vicinal risk encourages the network to behave linearly between training points, and it improves generalisation, robustness to label noise and adversarial perturbations, at essentially zero cost.

Cold open

Take 70% of a photo of a cat, overlay 30% of a photo of an aeroplane, and tell the network the answer is “0.7 cat, 0.3 aeroplane”. No such image exists in the world, and the classes have nothing to do with each other. Training on such ghosts, a few lines of code, improves ImageNet, CIFAR, speech and tabular models.

The problem

Empirical risk minimisation (ERM) minimises the average loss on the training set, which is the expected loss under the empirical distribution \(P_\delta = \frac1n \sum_i \delta(x = x_i, y = y_i)\), a set of spikes. Large networks can drive this to zero even with random labels (Zhang et al. 2017), so ERM says nothing about how the network behaves between the spikes, and it behaves badly there: overconfident, and sensitive to small adversarial perturbations. Data augmentation helps, but it is designed by hand per dataset and assumes that nearby examples share the class.

The method

Derivation · from ERM to vicinal risk

Vicinal risk minimisation (Chapelle et al. 2000) replaces each spike with a distribution over a vicinity of the training point: \(P_\nu = \frac1n \sum_i \nu(\tilde x, \tilde y \mid x_i, y_i)\), and minimises the expected loss under \(P_\nu\) by sampling from it. Gaussian vicinities, \(\nu = \mathcal N(\tilde x; x_i, \sigma^2) \cdot \delta(\tilde y = y_i)\), recover training with additive noise. mixup's vicinity is generic and label-aware:

$$\mu(\tilde x, \tilde y \mid x_i, y_i) = \frac1n \sum_j \E_\lambda\big[\delta(\tilde x = \lambda x_i + (1-\lambda) x_j,\ \tilde y = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j)\big], \qquad \lambda \sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha).$$

Sampling from it is the whole algorithm:

$$\tilde x = \lambda x_i + (1 - \lambda) x_j, \qquad \tilde y = \lambda y_i + (1 - \lambda) y_j,$$

with \(y\) one-hot and \(j\) a random partner. In practice one shuffled copy of the minibatch supplies the partners and a single \(\lambda\) is drawn per batch, so the cost is one extra elementwise operation.

Mixing the labels is the part people drop, and it is essential. Cross-entropy is linear in its target, \(\ell(p, y) = -\sum_k y_k \log p_k\), so

$$\ell\big(f(\tilde x),\ \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j\big) = \lambda\, \ell\big(f(\tilde x), y_i\big) + (1-\lambda)\, \ell\big(f(\tilde x), y_j\big).$$

The mixed label says: at a point a fraction \(\lambda\) of the way from \(x_j\) to \(x_i\), predict class \(i\) with probability \(\lambda\). Keep a hard label instead and you ask the network to call a 50/50 blend 100% one class and, on another draw, 100% the other: contradictory targets that teach confidence rather than interpolation. The authors also note that interpolating only between examples of the same class does not give mixup's gains; the counterintuitive bit is that random, unrelated pairs are what work.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def mixup(x, y_onehot, alpha=0.2):
    lam = rng.beta(alpha, alpha)                     # one lambda per batch
    perm = rng.permutation(len(x))                   # partner for each example: a shuffled copy
    return lam * x + (1 - lam) * x[perm], lam * y_onehot + (1 - lam) * y_onehot[perm], lam, perm

def cross_entropy(logp, y):                          # works for soft (mixed) labels too
    return -(y * logp).sum(axis=1).mean()

x = rng.normal(size=(8, 5))
y = np.eye(3)[rng.integers(0, 3, size=8)]            # one-hot labels, 3 classes
xm, ym, lam, perm = mixup(x, y)
logp = np.log(rng.dirichlet(np.ones(3), size=8))     # stand-in for the model's log-probs on xm
mixed = cross_entropy(logp, ym)
split = lam * cross_entropy(logp, y) + (1 - lam) * cross_entropy(logp, y[perm])
assert np.isclose(mixed, split)                      # mixing the labels = mixing the two losses
print(round(lam, 3), round(mixed, 4))

The inductive bias. mixup is a prior that the model should behave linearly in between training examples: its predictions along the segment from \(x_j\) to \(x_i\) should move linearly from \(y_j\) to \(y_i\). Linear behaviour is the simplest possible behaviour in that region, which reduces oscillations of the decision function and keeps confidence from saturating far from the data.

Evidence

The authors report improvements over ERM on ImageNet (around a point of top-1 error for ResNet-50 and larger models, with \(\alpha \in [0.1, 0.4]\); larger \(\alpha\) underfits), on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 (with \(\alpha = 1\)), on Google speech commands and on UCI tabular datasets. Three robustness results matter more than the accuracy deltas. mixup networks memorise corrupted labels more slowly and generalise better under label noise; they are more robust to white-box FGSM and iterative FGSM attacks on ImageNet; and mixup stabilises GAN training when applied to the discriminator's real/fake inputs.

The calibration result comes from a follow-up: Thulasidasan et al. (NeurIPS 2019) found mixup-trained networks are markedly better calibrated, less overconfident, with much of the effect attributable to training on soft, mixed labels (⟶ L326). Later work found the interaction with post-hoc temperature scaling is subtle: a model that is calibrated by mixup can become less calibratable afterwards.

Cutout, the complementary augmentation

Cutout (DeVries & Taylor 2017) zeroes a random square of the image and keeps the label: an occlusion prior, forcing the network to use the whole object. mixup changes every pixel slightly; Cutout changes some pixels entirely. Tomorrow's CutMix combines the two ideas.

Limits & what broke
  • Manifold intrusion. A blend of two images can resemble a real image of a third class, and then its mixed label is wrong (Guo et al. 2019).
  • Underfitting and longer schedules. mixup is a strong regulariser; small models and short schedules can lose accuracy, and gains often need longer training.
  • Locally unnatural. A ghostly overlay is not a plausible image, which hurts tasks that need localisation; CutMix (L113) addresses exactly this.
  • Explanations came later. Subsequent theory frames mixup as a data-dependent regulariser on the network's gradients and curvature; the VRM framing explains the algorithm but not, by itself, why it generalises.

Bridge

mixup interpolates whole images; tomorrow's CutMix pastes a rectangle from one image into another and mixes labels by area, which is locally honest. The same lesson then asks whether expensive learned augmentation policies (AutoAugment) were ever necessary, and finds that two hyperparameters suffice.

CutMix and RandAugment

35m videoLevel 2

Yun et al. ICCV 2019 (arXiv:1905.04899); Cubuk et al. NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:1909.13719)

Two augmentation papers with one moral. CutMix pastes a patch from one image into another and mixes the labels by area, combining Cutout's occlusion with mixup's label mixing while keeping every pixel natural. RandAugment replaces AutoAugment's thousands of GPU-hours of policy search with two hyperparameters and matches it.

Cold open

Each of these is worth roughly a point or two of ImageNet accuracy and costs essentially nothing at training time. One of them also answers an uncomfortable question: was the expensive reinforcement-learning search for augmentation policies ever necessary? A grid over two integers says no.

The problem

Regional dropout (Cutout, ⟵ L112) makes networks look beyond the most discriminative part of an object, but the erased pixels are wasted: they carry no information and never appear at test time. mixup (L112) uses every pixel, but its blends are locally ambiguous and unnatural, which hurts localisation. Separately, AutoAugment (Cubuk et al. 2019) had shown that learned augmentation policies beat hand-designed ones, but finding them meant training an RNN controller with reinforcement learning on a proxy task, at a cost commonly cited as about 5,000 GPU-hours for CIFAR-10 and 15,000 for ImageNet.

The method: CutMix

Definition · CutMix

For images \(x_A, x_B\) with one-hot labels \(y_A, y_B\) and a binary mask \(M\) that is 0 inside a box and 1 elsewhere:

$$\tilde x = M \odot x_A + (1 - M) \odot x_B, \qquad \tilde y = \lambda y_A + (1 - \lambda) y_B, \qquad \lambda = 1 - \frac{r_w r_h}{WH}.$$

Draw \(\lambda \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)\), i.e. uniform; set the box to \(r_w = W\sqrt{1-\lambda}\), \(r_h = H\sqrt{1-\lambda}\) so its area is \((1-\lambda)WH\); place its centre uniformly; clip it to the image; then recompute \(\lambda\) from the clipped area.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def cutmix(xa, xb, ya, yb, alpha=1.0):
    """xa, xb: (H, W, C) images; ya, yb: one-hot labels. Paste a box of B into A, mix labels by area."""
    H, W = xa.shape[:2]
    lam = rng.beta(alpha, alpha)                          # target share of A; Beta(1, 1) = uniform
    rh, rw = int(H * np.sqrt(1 - lam)), int(W * np.sqrt(1 - lam))   # box area = (1 - lam) H W
    cy, cx = rng.integers(H), rng.integers(W)             # box centre, uniform over the image
    y0, y1 = np.clip([cy - rh // 2, cy + rh // 2], 0, H)
    x0, x1 = np.clip([cx - rw // 2, cx + rw // 2], 0, W)
    out = xa.copy()
    out[y0:y1, x0:x1] = xb[y0:y1, x0:x1]
    lam = 1 - (y1 - y0) * (x1 - x0) / (H * W)             # recompute from the clipped box
    return out, lam * ya + (1 - lam) * yb

xa, xb = np.zeros((224, 224, 3)), np.ones((224, 224, 3))  # A all 0s, B all 1s: easy to count
mixed, label = cutmix(xa, xb, np.array([1.0, 0.0]), np.array([0.0, 1.0]))
assert np.isclose(label[0], 1 - mixed[..., 0].mean())     # A's label weight = A's pixel share
print(label.round(3))

Why it beats both parents on localisation. Cutout's erased region is uninformative; CutMix fills it with another object, so no pixels are wasted and the network must recognise the pasted object from a partial view. mixup's labels describe a global blend that no local region matches; CutMix's labels are locally honest: every pixel genuinely belongs to the class it is credited to, in proportion. The network is pushed to attend to the whole extent of each object, which is what weakly supervised localisation needs.

The method: from AutoAugment to RandAugment

An AutoAugment policy consists of sub-policies, each a pair of operations with a learned probability and magnitude; the controller is trained with RL to maximise validation accuracy of a child model on a reduced proxy dataset. The RandAugment paper puts its search space at around \(10^{32}\) policies. RandAugment keeps the same list of 14 image operations and throws away everything else:

Algorithm · RandAugment(N, M)
  1. For each training image, sample \(N\) operations uniformly at random, with replacement, from the 14.
  2. Apply them in sequence, each at the single global magnitude \(M\).
  3. Choose \(N\) and \(M\) by an ordinary grid search on the actual task and model.

The insight that makes this work is also the paper's most important finding: the best augmentation strength depends on model size and dataset size. Larger models and larger datasets want stronger augmentation. A policy searched on a small proxy model and a reduced dataset is therefore tuned to the wrong strength, and the expensive search was partly optimising for the proxy.

Evidence

CutMix. The authors report that on ImageNet with ResNet-50, CutMix improves top-1 accuracy by roughly two points over the baseline, more than Cutout or mixup in the same comparison; it also improves CIFAR, weakly supervised object localisation (where mixup tends to hurt), transfer to detection and captioning, and robustness to occlusion and adversarial inputs. Class activation maps of CutMix-trained models cover more of each object.

RandAugment. With no separate search, it matches or exceeds AutoAugment and its faster successors on CIFAR-10/100, SVHN and ImageNet, and improves COCO detection. The experiments that vary model width and training-set size show the optimal magnitude increasing with both, which is the evidence for the proxy-task critique. It is one of the best “was the search necessary?” results in machine learning, and an instance of the series' running theme: tune the baseline before you believe the delta.

Limits & what broke
  • Label noise from boxes. A uniformly placed box can cover only background of B, or erase all of A's object, yet the label credits area, not content. Saliency-guided variants (PuzzleMix, SaliencyMix) target this.
  • Not free for every task. Mixed labels interact badly with tasks that need precise per-object semantics, and strong augmentation can hurt small models, which is RandAugment's own finding turned around.
  • One magnitude for all operations. A shared \(M\) is crude; TrivialAugment later showed that even sampling one operation with a random magnitude per image is competitive, pushing the “simpler baseline” point further.
  • Now part of the recipe. Both are standard in modern training recipes (DeiT, Swin, ConvNeXt, ⟵ L78), which makes old-versus-new architecture comparisons without them unfair.

Bridge

Augmentation squeezes more out of a dataset. Tomorrow's paper asks a different question about data: once a network is trained on one task, how much of what it learned transfers to another, layer by layer, and why does transfer sometimes fail in the middle of the network?

How Transferable Are Features in Deep Neural Networks?

35m videoLevel 2

Yosinski, Clune, Bengio & Lipson, NIPS 2014 (arXiv:1411.1792)

By copying the first n layers of one trained network into another and retraining the rest, layer by layer, the paper measures how general each layer's features are, and separates two different reasons transfer can fail: features that are specific to the original task, and fragile co-adaptation between neighbouring layers that breaks when a network is cut in two.

Cold open

Everyone in 2014 was fine-tuning ImageNet networks for other tasks, and everyone “knew” that low layers are general and high layers specific. This is the experiment that everyone should have run and one team did: a clean, controlled measurement, with a surprise in the middle of the network and a practical headline at the end.

The problem

First-layer features of nearly every image network look the same: Gabor-like oriented edges and colour blobs, regardless of dataset or objective, the same structures sparse coding finds in natural images (⟵ L54). Last-layer features are obviously task-specific: they are the class scores. Where does the transition happen, is it gradual or sudden, and is “specificity” the only thing that can make a copied layer stop working?

The method

Split the 1,000 ImageNet classes randomly into two halves, A and B, of 500 classes each, and train an 8-layer AlexNet-style network on each: baseA and baseB. Then, for each \(n\) from 1 to 7, build four networks evaluated on task B:

Definition · selffer and transfer networks
  • BnB (“selffer”): copy layers 1…n from baseB and freeze them; re-initialise layers n+1…8 and train them on B. Same task, so no specificity can hurt.
  • BnB+: as BnB, but fine-tune all layers.
  • AnB (transfer): copy layers 1…n from baseA, freeze them, train the rest on B.
  • AnB+: as AnB, but fine-tune all layers on B.
Argument · separating the two causes of transfer loss

Compare each network with baseB at the same \(n\).

  1. BnB isolates co-adaptation. Layers 1…n were trained for task B, so any drop cannot be specificity. Yet BnB drops at \(n = 3, 4, 5\) and recovers at 6 and 7. Explanation: features in the middle layers were learned jointly with the layers above them, in a fragile co-adaptation; cut the network there and the upper layers, trained afresh by gradient descent, cannot rediscover the complementary features. Near the top, only one or two layers remain to relearn, which is easy.
  2. AnB contains both. At \(n = 1, 2\) AnB matches baseB: these features are general. At \(n = 3\)–5 it drops, about as much as BnB: mostly co-adaptation. At \(n = 6, 7\) it keeps dropping while BnB recovered: that extra loss is specificity.
  3. So the drop is the sum of two effects that peak at different depths. Looking at AnB alone, you would wrongly attribute all of it to specificity.

Evidence

The headline for practice is AnB+: transferring and then fine-tuning everything not only recovers baseB's performance but exceeds it at every \(n\) in their plot, even though each half of ImageNet has around 645,000 training images. A boost to generalisation from pretraining on a different task lingers after extensive fine-tuning. BnB+ simply matches baseB, which rules out longer training as the explanation for AnB+.

Two further experiments complete the picture. With a dissimilar split, man-made versus natural classes (551 and 449 classes), transfer degrades more at higher layers, so the distance between tasks matters, but transferred features still beat random ones. Random, untrained lower layers, which an earlier study (Jarrett et al. 2009) had found surprisingly useful in shallow networks on small data, do much worse here: performance falls off sharply once more than a layer or two of random filters are frozen into a deep network.

How to read the curves

Spend time on the notation before the plot; most confusion about this paper is about what “A3B+” means. Then compare vertically at one \(n\) (BnB versus AnB isolates specificity) and horizontally along one curve (how a cause varies with depth).

Limits & what broke
  • One architecture, one era. An 8-layer AlexNet-style network without batch normalisation or residual connections. Co-adaptation in modern residual networks, where every layer writes into a shared residual stream, may look quite different.
  • Very similar tasks. Random halves of ImageNet are as close as two tasks can be; even the man-made versus natural split stays within ImageNet's photographic distribution. Raghu et al. (2019) found that for medical imaging, ImageNet transfer helps much less than assumed, and that much of its benefit comes from the scale of the initial weights.
  • Pretraining is not always needed. He, Girshick & Dollár (2019) showed detectors trained from scratch on COCO can match ImageNet-pretrained ones given longer schedules and appropriate normalisation, although pretraining still converges faster.
  • Frozen features got much better. Self-supervised and large-scale pretraining (DINOv2, L120) produced features that work well frozen, narrowing the gap between AnB and AnB+ that this paper made famous.

An Image is Worth 16x16 Words (Vision Transformer)

55m videoLevel 2

Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021 (arXiv:2010.11929)

Cut an image into 16×16 patches, embed each linearly, and feed the sequence to a standard Transformer encoder with almost no vision-specific design: it loses to ResNets when pretrained on ImageNet alone and beats them when pretrained on hundreds of millions of images, because inductive bias is a substitute for data.

Cold open

The model in this paper is the Transformer encoder from NLP (⟵ L129), nearly unchanged; the only vision-specific step is cutting the image into patches. Trained on ImageNet's 1.3M images it is worse than a ResNet. Trained on 300M images it is better than any CNN, for less pretraining compute. One figure, accuracy against pretraining dataset size, carries the whole argument.

The problem

Attention had entered vision as an add-on to CNNs (non-local blocks, DETR's encoder–decoder, ⟵ L90) or as specialised local and axial attention patterns that need custom kernels to run efficiently. Convolution's built-in assumptions, locality and translation equivariance, were treated as essential. The question the paper asks is simple: how far does a standard Transformer get on images with as few of those assumptions as possible, if it is trained at scale?

The method

Definition · the ViT forward pass

Reshape an image \(\mathbf x \in \R^{H \times W \times C}\) into \(N = HW/P^2\) flattened patches \(\mathbf x_p^i \in \R^{P^2 C}\). With a learned projection \(\mathbf E \in \R^{(P^2 C) \times D}\), a learned class token and learned position embeddings \(\mathbf E_{\text{pos}} \in \R^{(N+1) \times D}\):

$$\mathbf z_0 = [\mathbf x_{\text{class}};\ \mathbf x_p^1 \mathbf E;\ \dots;\ \mathbf x_p^N \mathbf E] + \mathbf E_{\text{pos}},$$ $$\mathbf z'_\ell = \operatorname{MSA}(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf z_{\ell-1})) + \mathbf z_{\ell-1}, \qquad \mathbf z_\ell = \operatorname{MLP}(\operatorname{LN}(\mathbf z'_\ell)) + \mathbf z'_\ell, \qquad \mathbf y = \operatorname{LN}(\mathbf z_L^0).$$

For a 224×224 RGB image with \(P = 16\): \(N = 196\) tokens, each patch a \(16 \cdot 16 \cdot 3 = 768\)-vector. The patch embedding is exactly a convolution with kernel 16 and stride 16. ViT-B/16 has 12 layers, width 768, 12 heads, about 86M parameters; ViT-L/16 has 24 layers, width 1024, about 307M; ViT-H/14 has 32 layers, width 1280, about 632M. The suffix is the patch size; smaller patches mean more tokens and quadratically more attention cost.

import numpy as np

def patchify(img, P=16):
    """(H, W, C) image -> (N, P*P*C) patch vectors, N = (H/P)(W/P), row-major over the grid."""
    H, W, C = img.shape
    x = img.reshape(H // P, P, W // P, P, C).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4)
    return x.reshape(-1, P * P * C)

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
img = rng.random((224, 224, 3))
patches = patchify(img)                              # (196, 768): 14 x 14 patches of 16x16x3
assert np.allclose(patches[1], img[0:16, 16:32].reshape(-1))   # patch 1 = top row, 2nd column

D = 768                                              # ViT-B width
E = rng.normal(0, 0.02, size=(16 * 16 * 3, D))       # patch embedding: one shared linear map
cls = np.zeros((1, D))                               # learnable [class] token
pos = rng.normal(0, 0.02, size=(197, D))             # learnable 1D position embeddings
z0 = np.vstack([cls, patches @ E]) + pos             # the encoder's input sequence
print(patches.shape, z0.shape)                       # (196, 768) (197, 768)

Fine-tuning at higher resolution keeps the patch size, so the sequence gets longer; the pretrained position embeddings are 2D-interpolated to the new grid. This and the patch split are the only places the 2D structure of images is put in by hand.

Inductive bias as a data-efficiency trade-off

In a CNN, locality, two-dimensional neighbourhood structure and translation equivariance are built into every layer. In ViT, only the per-token MLPs are local and translation-equivariant; self-attention is global from the first layer, and spatial relations between patches must be learned from data, starting from position embeddings that carry no 2D prior. Built-in assumptions act as a prior: when they are right they save data, and when data is plentiful they become a constraint. The prediction is a crossover: CNNs ahead on small data, ViTs ahead on large data.

Evidence

The crossover. Pretrained on ImageNet-1k, large ViTs underperform ViT-B and BiT ResNets, despite heavy regularisation. Pretrained on ImageNet-21k (about 14M images) they are comparable. Pretrained on JFT-300M, the larger ViTs come out ahead. The same pattern appears on random JFT subsets of 9M, 30M, 90M and 300M images: ResNets win on the smaller subsets, ViTs on the larger. The authors report about 88.5% ImageNet top-1 for ViT-H/14 pretrained on JFT-300M, ahead of the best CNNs of the time, using several times less pretraining compute by their accounting. This crossover plot is the paper.

Position embeddings. No position embedding is clearly worse, but 1D, 2D-aware and relative embeddings all perform about the same. That is a striking negative result: adding the 2D structure by hand gives nothing, because the learned 1D embeddings discover it. Their similarity maps show each patch's embedding most similar to those in its own row and column.

Mean attention distance. For each head, average the image-space distance between query and attended patches, weighted by attention: the analogue of a receptive field. In the lowest layers, some heads attend locally while others already attend across the whole image; the distance grows with depth. Local heads are the evidence for “it learns to behave like a CNN early on”; global heads in layer 1 are something no CNN can do. In hybrid models, which feed ResNet feature maps instead of raw patches, the early local heads are less pronounced, as if the convolutional stem had taken over their job.

What didn't matter. A class token versus global average pooling (both work, with different learning rates); the type of position embedding; and, at large compute, the hybrid stem, which helps for small models but whose advantage vanishes as models grow. A preliminary self-supervised experiment, predicting masked patches, helped but lagged supervised pretraining clearly: the seed of MAE (L118).

Limits & what broke
  • Private data. The winning results need JFT-300M, which only Google had. DeiT (L116) showed the ImageNet-1k gap was largely a training-recipe problem, and AugReg (Steiner et al. 2021) that strong augmentation and regularisation are worth roughly an order of magnitude of data.
  • Quadratic cost, single scale. Global attention over \(N\) tokens costs \(O(N^2)\), and a ViT produces one low-resolution feature map, awkward for detection and segmentation. Swin (L117) added windows and a hierarchy.
  • Attention-map artifacts. Large ViTs develop a few high-norm tokens in background patches that act as scratch space; “Vision Transformers Need Registers” (Darcet et al. 2024) fixed this with extra tokens (L120).
  • The CNN comeback. ConvNeXt (⟵ L78) showed a modernised ResNet trained with a ViT-era recipe matches Swin, so part of ViT's apparent advantage was the recipe.

Bridge

ViT needed 300 million images to beat CNNs. Tomorrow's paper trains a ViT on ImageNet-1k alone, on one 8-GPU machine in about three days, and gets competitive accuracy: the difference was augmentation, regularisation, and a distillation token that borrows a CNN's inductive bias.

Training Data-Efficient Image Transformers (DeiT)

35m videoLevel 2

Touvron, Cord, Douze, Massa, Sablayrolles & Jégou, ICML 2021 (arXiv:2012.12877)

A ViT trained on ImageNet-1k alone can match strong CNNs if the training recipe is right (heavy augmentation, regularisation and repeated augmentation), and a learned distillation token that imitates a CNN teacher adds a further gain, apparently by transferring the teacher's inductive bias.

Cold open

ViT (L115) concluded that transformers “do not generalize well when trained on insufficient amounts of data” and needed JFT-300M. Two months later this paper trained ViT-B on ImageNet-1k alone, on a single machine with eight GPUs in under three days, to about 82% top-1. Same architecture. The honest framing is that DeiT's main contribution is a training recipe, and saying so is the point of the lesson.

The problem

The ViT paper's ImageNet-1k results used a recipe tuned for CNNs and for large-data pretraining. A model without convolution's priors has to learn locality from data, so it is far more sensitive to how much effective data the recipe manufactures through augmentation, and to regularisation. Nobody had checked how good a ViT could be on ImageNet-1k with a recipe designed for it, nor whether a CNN's knowledge could be transferred into it.

The method: the recipe

  • Optimisation: AdamW with weight decay, cosine schedule with warm-up, batch 1024, 300 epochs. SGD works markedly worse for ViTs.
  • Augmentation: RandAugment, mixup and CutMix together (⟵ L112, L113), plus random erasing.
  • Regularisation: stochastic depth and label smoothing; no dropout, which the ablation finds harmful.
  • Repeated augmentation (Hoffer et al. 2020; Berman et al. 2019): each minibatch contains several differently augmented copies of the same image (three in DeiT), and correspondingly fewer distinct images. An underrated trick: the authors report it as one of the most important components in their ablation.

The ablation removes components one at a time: dropping repeated augmentation costs the most in their table, and removing the strong augmentations or stochastic depth also costs clear accuracy. The recipe matters more for ViT than for CNNs because augmentation is where the missing priors come from.

The method: the distillation token

Definition · hard and soft distillation

Let \(Z_s\) be the class-head logits, \(Z_d\) the distillation-head logits, \(Z_t\) the teacher's logits, \(\psi\) the softmax. Soft distillation (⟶ Hinton et al., L331) uses a temperature \(\tau\):

$$\mathcal L_{\text{soft}} = (1 - \lambda)\, \mathcal L_{\text{CE}}(\psi(Z_s), y) + \lambda \tau^2\, \KL\big(\psi(Z_t/\tau)\,\|\,\psi(Z_s/\tau)\big).$$

Hard-label distillation treats the teacher's top prediction \(y_t = \argmax_c Z_t(c)\) as a second label, here assigned to the distillation token:

$$\mathcal L_{\text{hard}} = \tfrac12\, \mathcal L_{\text{CE}}(\psi(Z_s), y) + \tfrac12\, \mathcal L_{\text{CE}}(\psi(Z_d), y_t).$$

Why hard won here. The authors find hard distillation clearly better than soft for this setting. One plausible reason is augmentation: under aggressive random crops and CutMix, the true label may not describe what is actually visible, while the teacher, looking at the same augmented image, gives a label for what it sees. The teacher's argmax is a crop-aware relabelling, and a hard target is a clean way to impose it.

Evidence

Without distillation, DeiT-B reaches about 82% top-1 at 224² on ImageNet-1k, by the authors' report, against the original ViT-B's high 70s with the same data; with distillation and fine-tuning at 384² the paper reports up to about 85%, competitive with EfficientNets at similar throughput. Smaller DeiT-S and DeiT-Ti give good accuracy–speed trade-offs, and the models transfer well to smaller datasets.

The distillation token learns something different from the class token. Their learned input embeddings are nearly orthogonal, and their output embeddings are highly but not perfectly similar. As a control, adding a second class token trained on the true labels gives two tokens that converge to the same vector and no gain. The agreement analysis supports the same reading: the distilled model agrees with the CNN teacher more than a non-distilled DeiT does, with the distillation head closest to the teacher and the class head closest to plain DeiT.

A CNN teacher beats a transformer teacher. A RegNetY teacher gives better students than a DeiT-B teacher of comparable accuracy, and the distilled student can even exceed its teacher's accuracy. The authors' interpretation, the lovely part of the paper, is that distillation transfers the teacher's inductive bias: the student learns to make the kinds of predictions a convolutional network makes, without being convolutional. This is inductive bias by proxy.

Limits & what broke
  • The recipe is the result. Most of the gain over ViT's ImageNet-1k numbers comes from training choices; the architecture is unchanged. DeiT III (Touvron, Cord & Jégou, ECCV 2022) later reached similar or better accuracy with a simpler recipe and no distillation at all.
  • You need a strong CNN teacher, and its forward pass on every training image. The distillation token did not become a standard component; later training recipes mostly dropped it.
  • “Inductive bias transfer” is an interpretation. The agreement statistics are consistent with it but do not isolate which biases transfer, and stronger augmentation alone narrows the gap.
  • Recipe sensitivity. ViTs trained this way remain sensitive to optimiser settings and augmentation strength; reproductions that change the recipe often lose accuracy.

Bridge

DeiT made ViT trainable on ImageNet. It did not make ViT a good backbone for detection and segmentation: one low-resolution feature map and attention cost quadratic in image size. Tomorrow's Swin Transformer fixes both with local windows that shift between layers and a hierarchy of resolutions.

Swin Transformer: Hierarchical Vision Transformer using Shifted Windows

45m videoLevel 3

Liu et al., ICCV 2021 (arXiv:2103.14030)

Restrict self-attention to non-overlapping local windows so cost grows linearly with image size, shift the windows by half a window in every other block so information crosses window boundaries, and merge patches between stages to build a CNN-like feature pyramid: a transformer that works as a drop-in backbone for detection and segmentation.

Cold open

ViT (L115) classified images but could not easily replace ResNet inside Mask R-CNN: one 1/16-resolution feature map, and attention cost quadratic in the number of pixels. Swin fixed both, and at release it set new state-of-the-art numbers on COCO detection and ADE20K segmentation by clear margins. The idea that made it work is one figure: windows that shift between consecutive blocks. Draw it slowly.

The problem

Dense prediction needs high-resolution features (stride 4 or 8) at several scales, an FPN-style pyramid (⟵ L88). Global attention over an \(h \times w\) map of tokens costs \(O((hw)^2)\); at stride 4 on an 800×1333 COCO image that is tens of thousands of tokens, which is prohibitive. Sliding-window local attention (each query attending to its own neighbourhood) is linear but slow in practice, because every query has a different key set and the memory access pattern defeats batched matrix multiplies.

The method: window attention

Split the token map into non-overlapping windows of \(M \times M\) tokens (\(M = 7\) by default) and run standard multi-head self-attention independently inside each window. Every window has the same shape, so all windows are one batched matrix multiply.

Derivation · global versus window attention cost

For \(hw\) tokens of width \(C\), the projections to Q, K, V and the output cost \(4hwC^2\); the attention matrix and its product with V cost \(2(hw)^2 C\) globally. Inside windows, each of the \(hw/M^2\) windows pays \(2(M^2)^2 C\), so

$$\Omega(\text{MSA}) = 4hwC^2 + 2(hw)^2 C, \qquad \Omega(\text{W-MSA}) = 4hwC^2 + 2M^2 hw C.$$

The quadratic term becomes linear in \(hw\). At Swin-T's first stage on a 224² image: \(h = w = 56\), \(hw = 3{,}136\), \(C = 96\), \(M = 7\). The projections cost about \(1.16 \times 10^8\) multiply–adds either way; the attention term is \(1.89 \times 10^9\) globally versus \(2.95 \times 10^7\) in windows, a factor \(hw/M^2 = 64\) smaller. In total, about 2.0 versus 0.15 GFLOPs for that one layer, and the gap grows linearly with image area.

The method: shifted windows

Windows alone never exchange information: a token can attend only to its own window, layer after layer. Swin alternates two partitions in consecutive blocks. Block \(l\) uses the regular partition; block \(l+1\) shifts it by \((\lfloor M/2 \rfloor, \lfloor M/2 \rfloor)\) tokens. The windows of block \(l+1\) straddle the boundaries of block \(l\), so after two blocks every token has exchanged information with tokens from neighbouring windows.

Algorithm · cyclic shift with masked attention
  1. Roll the token map by \((-\lfloor M/2 \rfloor, -\lfloor M/2 \rfloor)\) along height and width, so tokens wrap from the top and left edges to the bottom and right.
  2. Partition into regular \(M \times M\) windows: the same number of windows, all full-size, as the unshifted block.
  3. Inside each window, add a mask of large negative values to the attention logits between tokens that came from different sub-windows (different letters), so they cannot attend to each other.
  4. Apply attention, then roll back by \((+\lfloor M/2 \rfloor, +\lfloor M/2 \rfloor)\).
import numpy as np

H = W = 8; M = 4; s = M // 2                     # 8x8 patches, window M = 4, shift M/2 = 2
x = np.arange(H * W).reshape(H, W)               # stand-in feature map (one channel)
x_rolled = np.roll(x, shift=(-s, -s), axis=(0, 1))   # cyclic shift up-left: no padding needed

region = np.zeros((H, W), dtype=int)             # which shifted-partition window each position
bands = (slice(0, -M), slice(-M, -s), slice(-s, None))   # came from, in the rolled frame
for r, hs in enumerate(bands):
    for c, ws in enumerate(bands):
        region[hs, ws] = 3 * r + c

def windows(a):                                  # (H, W) -> (num_windows, M*M), same as layer l
    return a.reshape(H // M, M, W // M, M).transpose(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(-1, M * M)

ids = windows(region)
mask = np.where(ids[:, :, None] != ids[:, None, :], -100.0, 0.0)   # added to attention logits
print(windows(x_rolled).shape, [len(np.unique(w)) for w in ids])  # (4, 16) [1, 2, 2, 4]
x_back = np.roll(x_rolled, shift=(s, s), axis=(0, 1))             # reverse shift after attention
assert (x_back == x).all()

Why this is fast: the naive way to compute (b) pads the small border windows up to \(M \times M\), raising the window count from \(\lceil h/M \rceil \times \lceil w/M \rceil\) to \((\lceil h/M \rceil + 1) \times (\lceil w/M \rceil + 1)\) and wasting compute on padding. The cyclic shift keeps the batch shape of (a) exactly, so the shifted block costs the same as the regular one; the only extra work is adding a precomputed mask.

The method: hierarchy and position bias

Patch merging. Swin starts from 4×4-pixel patches (stride 4, \(C = 96\) for Swin-T). Between stages, each 2×2 group of neighbouring tokens is concatenated (\(4C\) channels) and linearly projected to \(2C\): resolution halves, width doubles, exactly like a strided convolution stage. Four stages give feature maps at strides 4, 8, 16 and 32, the pyramid FPN and U-Net-style decoders expect. That hierarchy, not attention as such, is what makes Swin a drop-in backbone for dense tasks, which ViT was not.

Relative position bias. Inside a window, attention is \(\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt d + B)V\), where \(B \in \R^{M^2 \times M^2}\) depends only on the relative offset between two tokens. Offsets along each axis lie in \([-M+1, M-1]\), so \(B\) is read from a small learned table \(\hat B \in \R^{(2M-1) \times (2M-1)}\) per head. Relative bias is translation-invariant within a window, which suits windows that move around the image.

Evidence

The abstract reports 87.3% top-1 on ImageNet-1K (Swin-L pretrained on ImageNet-22K), 58.7 box AP and 51.1 mask AP on COCO test-dev, and 53.5 mIoU on ADE20K val, surpassing the previous best by +2.7 box AP, +2.6 mask AP and +3.2 mIoU. The ablations are the persuasive part. Shifted windows improve over non-shifted windows on all three tasks, with the largest gains on detection and segmentation; the cyclic-shift implementation has much lower latency than sliding windows or padding; and relative position bias beats no position encoding and absolute embeddings, with absolute embeddings slightly helping ImageNet classification but hurting detection and segmentation, by the authors' report.

Limits & what broke
  • ConvNeXt's rebuttal. A ResNet modernised step by step with Swin's macro-design and training recipe matched Swin at equal FLOPs (⟵ L78). Hierarchy, large receptive fields and the recipe explain much of Swin's advantage.
  • Plain ViTs came back for detection. ViTDet (Li et al. 2022) showed a non-hierarchical ViT with a simple pyramid built from its last feature map, plus MAE pretraining (L118), is competitive, so a hierarchical backbone is not strictly necessary.
  • Fixed window size. \(M\) trades receptive field against cost, and the relative-bias table is tied to it, which complicates transfer across resolutions; Swin V2 introduced log-spaced continuous position bias for this.
  • Masking and rolling are awkward to fuse into optimised attention kernels, and windowed attention fits poorly with masked-image pretraining that drops tokens, which plain ViTs handle naturally.

Bridge

All of the transformers so far learned from labels. The last three lessons of the month learn from images alone: tomorrow, mask three quarters of an image and ask a ViT to paint it back, which turns out to be a fast and scalable way to pretrain.

Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision Learners (MAE)

45m videoLevel 2

He, Chen, Xie, Li, Dollár & Girshick, CVPR 2022 (arXiv:2111.06377)

Mask a random 75% of an image's patches, encode only the visible quarter with a ViT, and let a small decoder reconstruct the missing pixels: a pretraining task hard enough to teach semantics, and cheap enough to scale, because the encoder never sees the masked tokens.

Cold open

Delete three quarters of a photograph in random 16×16 squares. A trained MAE fills in a plausible dog, bus or bowl of fruit from what is left. The pretraining that teaches this is also unusually cheap: the encoder processes a quarter of the tokens, so training runs three or more times faster than processing the full image, and the representation, once fine-tuned, beat every ImageNet-only method of its time.

The problem

Masked prediction had transformed NLP: BERT (⟶ L137) masks about 15% of tokens and predicts them. Denoising autoencoders (⟵ L53) had tried the analogous idea for images long before. Yet vision self-supervision in 2021 was dominated by contrastive and self-distillation methods (MoCo, SimCLR, DINO, L120), which depend heavily on hand-designed augmentations. The paper asks why masked autoencoding lagged in vision, and answers with three differences: architecture (CNNs could not easily take mask tokens or positional inputs; ViT removes that obstacle), information density, and the role of the decoder.

The method

Algorithm · MAE pretraining step
  1. Patchify and embed the image (as in ViT, L115), add position embeddings, and keep a uniformly random 25% of the patches.
  2. Encoder: a standard ViT applied to the visible patches only.
  3. Decoder input: encoded visible tokens plus one shared, learned mask token at every masked position, each with a position embedding. The decoder is small (by default 8 blocks of width 512, under a tenth of the encoder's computation per token).
  4. Target: the pixels of each masked patch, normalised by that patch's own mean and standard deviation. Loss: mean squared error on masked patches only.
  5. After pretraining, discard the decoder; fine-tune or probe the encoder on full, unmasked images.
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
N, D, ratio = 196, 768, 0.75                       # ViT-B/16 at 224x224: 196 patch tokens
tokens = rng.normal(size=(N, D))                   # patch embeddings + position embeddings

keep = int(N * (1 - ratio))                        # 49 visible patches
shuffle = np.argsort(rng.random(N))                # random permutation by sorting noise
visible_idx = shuffle[:keep]
encoder_in = tokens[visible_idx]                   # the encoder sees only these 49 tokens

latent = encoder_in                                # stand-in for the encoder's outputs
mask_token = np.zeros(D)                           # one shared vector, learned in the real model
restore = np.argsort(shuffle)                      # inverse permutation
decoder_in = np.vstack([latent, np.tile(mask_token, (N - keep, 1))])[restore]
assert np.allclose(decoder_in[visible_idx], latent)   # every token back at its own position
print(encoder_in.shape, decoder_in.shape)          # (49, 768) (196, 768)
print(f"encoder attention matrix: {(keep / N) ** 2:.4f} of the full size")   # 0.0625

Masking by sorting random noise, as the paper's implementation does, needs no sparse operations: the encoder gets an ordinary, shorter dense sequence. That is only possible because a ViT accepts sequences of any length; a CNN needs the full grid.

Argument · where the speedup comes from

With masking ratio \(r\), the encoder processes \((1-r)N\) tokens. Its linear layers cost \(\propto (1-r)N\), a quarter at \(r = 0.75\); its attention costs \(\propto ((1-r)N)^2\), a sixteenth. The decoder sees all \(N\) tokens but is narrow and shallow. Since the encoder dominates total cost, pretraining is several times cheaper per image than running the encoder on full images; the authors report wall-clock speedups of 3× or more, and lower memory, which in turn allows larger models. Dropping masked tokens from the encoder is also better for accuracy: putting mask tokens into the encoder, as BERT does, makes it both slower and worse, because the encoder then trains on inputs it never sees at fine-tuning time.

Why 75% and not 15%

Language is a human-made signal, dense with meaning: a missing word is often hard to infer, so predicting 15% of tokens is already a demanding task. Images are natural signals with heavy spatial redundancy: a missing patch can usually be interpolated from its neighbours with no understanding of the scene at all. To force the model to reason about objects and context rather than copy local texture, most of the image has to be removed. Information density, not the modality, sets the right masking ratio.

Evidence

The masking-ratio curve, the paper's most quoted figure: fine-tuning accuracy is high across a broad range of ratios, and linear-probing accuracy rises steadily with the ratio up to about 75%, far above BERT's 15%. Other ablations: random masking beats block-wise and grid-wise masking; per-patch normalised pixel targets improve over raw pixels; BEiT-style discrete tokens (L119) do about as well as normalised pixels while requiring a pretrained tokenizer; MAE works with only random cropping, and even without it, unlike contrastive methods; longer pretraining keeps helping. A ViT-H pretrained and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1K alone reaches 87.8% top-1, by the authors' report the best among methods using only ImageNet-1K. Transfer to COCO detection and ADE20K segmentation beats supervised ImageNet pretraining.

Linear probing underrates MAE

MAE's linear-probe accuracy is lower than contrastive methods', which looks like a weaker representation. But fine-tuning only the last one or two transformer blocks closes most of the gap quickly. A linear probe measures one property, whether classes are linearly separable in the final layer, and MAE has no reason to put that property there: its last encoder layer feeds a decoder that must reconstruct pixels, not classify. The features are strong but nonlinear. Treat the linear-probe gap as partly a measurement artifact, and report partial fine-tuning alongside it.

MAE versus contrastive learning. Contrastive and self-distillation methods learn invariances chosen by their augmentations, which makes global features linearly separable and good for retrieval and k-NN classification. MAE learns to model the content of every patch, which suits fine-tuning and dense tasks such as detection and segmentation, needs almost no augmentation, and scales efficiently with model size. Later methods (iBOT, DINOv2) combine both objectives.

Limits & what broke
  • Not useful frozen. Without fine-tuning, MAE features lag contrastive ones on k-NN and linear evaluation; for frozen general-purpose features, DINOv2 (L120) won.
  • Blurry reconstructions. MSE on pixels predicts the mean of plausible completions; the reconstructions are blurry, which is fine for representation learning but means MAE is not a generator.
  • Plain ViTs only. Dropping tokens is natural for a plain ViT but awkward for hierarchical, windowed backbones (L117), which needed special variants.
  • The target debate continued. Feature targets from a teacher (data2vec, iBOT, EVA's CLIP targets, HOG in MaskFeat) later did better than pixels in many settings; tomorrow's paper is the token side of that argument.

Bridge

MAE predicts pixels. BEiT, published a few months earlier, predicted discrete visual tokens from a pretrained tokenizer, arguing that pixel regression wastes capacity on high-frequency noise, exactly the claim MAE disputes. Tomorrow presents that side of a live disagreement.

BEiT: BERT Pre-Training of Image Transformers

30m videoLevel 3

Bao, Dong, Piao & Wei, ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2106.08254)

Make BERT literal for images: a pretrained discrete tokenizer turns each patch into a visual word, a block of patches is masked, and a ViT is trained to predict the masked patches' token ids, on the argument that discrete targets spare the model from wasting capacity on pixel-level noise.

Cold open

BERT (⟶ L137) predicts masked words from a vocabulary. Images have no vocabulary. BEiT borrows one: DALL·E's discrete VAE (⟶ L265) already maps an image to a grid of codes from a dictionary of 8,192. Mask some patches, predict their codes with a softmax, and the BERT recipe transfers almost unchanged. It clearly beat training the same ViT from scratch on ImageNet-1K, months before MAE.

The problem

Self-supervised ViTs at the time were trained contrastively or by self-distillation (DINO, L120; MoCo v3). Masked prediction was the obvious transplant from NLP, but what should the model predict for a masked patch? Regressing raw pixels, the authors argue, makes the model spend capacity on short-range dependencies and high-frequency details, which matter for reconstruction but not for semantics. A softmax over discrete tokens also matches BERT's objective exactly.

The method

Algorithm · BEiT pretraining
  1. Tokenise. Map the image to a 14×14 grid of visual-token ids \(z_i \in \{1, \dots, 8192\}\), one per 16×16 patch, with the publicly released dVAE from DALL·E (trained beforehand, frozen).
  2. Mask blockwise. Repeatedly choose a rectangular block of patches (at least 16 patches, random aspect ratio) until about 40% of the 196 patches are masked. Blocks rather than independent patches, because isolated masked patches are too easy to fill in from their neighbours.
  3. Corrupt. Replace masked patch embeddings with a shared learnable embedding \(e_{[M]}\), add position embeddings, run the ViT on all tokens.
  4. Predict. A softmax classifier on each masked position's output; maximise \(\sum_{i \in \mathcal M} \log p(z_i \mid x^{\mathcal M})\), the log-likelihood of the correct ids given the corrupted image.
Derivation · BEiT as the second stage of a VAE

The paper frames the whole procedure as maximising an evidence lower bound. With \(x\) the image, \(\tilde x\) its masked version and \(z\) the visual tokens:

$$\sum_{(x, \tilde x)} \log p(x \mid \tilde x) \;\ge\; \sum_{(x, \tilde x)} \Big( \E_{z \sim q_\phi(z \mid x)} \big[\log p_\psi(x \mid z)\big] - \KL\big[q_\phi(z \mid x),\ p_\theta(z \mid \tilde x)\big] \Big).$$

Stage one learns the tokenizer \(q_\phi\) and decoder \(p_\psi\) as a discrete VAE with a uniform prior: that is what DALL·E's dVAE already did. Stage two keeps them fixed and learns the prior \(p_\theta(z \mid \tilde x)\), the BEiT network. Replacing \(q_\phi\) by a point mass at its most likely tokens turns the KL term into exactly the masked-token cross-entropy above. The decoder \(p_\psi\) is never used during pretraining; only the targets are.

Why discrete targets? A token id summarises a patch at the level of detail the tokenizer chose to keep. Predicting it is a classification over a learned vocabulary, so noise and fine texture that the tokenizer discards are not in the target, and a single softmax can express a multimodal guess where squared error would average. That is the paper's argument; the next paragraphs are about whether it holds.

Evidence

The abstract reports that BEiT-B reaches 83.2% top-1 on ImageNet-1K after fine-tuning, against 81.8% for DeiT-B trained from scratch with the same setup (L116), and that BEiT-L reaches 86.3% using only ImageNet-1K, above a ViT-L supervised on ImageNet-22K. It also improves ADE20K semantic segmentation over supervised pretraining. The ablations support both design choices in BEiT's own setting: replacing visual tokens with pixel regression lowers accuracy, removing blockwise masking hurts, especially for segmentation, and removing both hurts most.

A live disagreement: what should a masked image model predict?

BEiT says pixel regression is “too easy, too noisy” and wastes capacity. MAE (L118) disputes exactly this: with a much higher masking ratio (75%) and per-patch normalised pixel targets, pixel regression matched dVAE tokens in its ablation, with no tokenizer to pretrain. Both can be right, because their pixel baselines differ in masking ratio and normalisation. The resolution that followed was neither pixels nor dVAE codes but semantic targets from a teacher: BEiT v2 distilled its tokenizer from CLIP; iBOT and data2vec predicted an online teacher's features; MaskFeat predicted HOG descriptors (⟵ L109). The question shifted from “discrete or continuous” to “how semantic is the target?”

Limits & what broke
  • The tokenizer is a hidden dependency. BEiT needs a dVAE trained beforehand, by another lab, on a very large image collection. Its quality and training data shape every downstream result; comparisons with methods that use nothing but ImageNet are not quite like for like. MAE's elegance is precisely the absence of this stage.
  • No speedup. The encoder processes every position, [M] tokens included, so pretraining costs as much as a full forward pass; and [M] tokens never appear at fine-tuning time, a train–test mismatch MAE avoids.
  • Low masking ratio. About 40%, chosen in the BERT spirit; MAE showed that images tolerate, and benefit from, far more.
  • dVAE codes are not very semantic. They were trained for reconstruction; later tokenizers trained for semantics worked better, which partly vindicates MAE's critique of BEiT's specific targets rather than of discrete targets in general.

Bridge

BEiT and MAE learn by reconstruction and are at their best after fine-tuning. Tomorrow's paper, DINO, learns without any reconstruction and without negative pairs, by making a student match an averaged copy of itself, and its frozen features segment objects in their attention maps with no labels at all.

Emerging Properties in Self-Supervised ViTs (DINO) + DINOv2

50m videoLevel 3

Caron et al. ICCV 2021 (arXiv:2104.14294); Oquab et al. 2023 (arXiv:2304.07193)

Train a student network to match the output distribution of a teacher that is an exponential moving average of the student, across different crops of the same image, and prevent collapse by centring and sharpening the teacher's output: no labels, no negative pairs, and features good enough frozen; DINOv2 then scales the recipe with a carefully curated dataset.

Cold open

Train a ViT with no labels, look at where its [CLS] token attends in the last layer, and the heads outline the foreground object. This was probably the most shared image of 2021 self-supervised learning. Be precise: it is the final layer's self-attention from the class token to the patches, one map per head, and nothing was trained to segment.

The problem

Contrastive methods (SimCLR, MoCo) pull two views of an image together and push other images away, which needs many negatives. BYOL dropped negatives by having a student with an extra predictor regress an EMA teacher's features, though why it avoids collapse was debated. DINO asks what self-supervision gives ViTs that labels do not.

The method: self-distillation with no labels

Definition · the DINO objective

Each network ends in a head with \(K\) outputs (\(K = 65{,}536\) in the paper), turned into a distribution by a temperature softmax: \(P_s(x)^{(i)} = \exp(g_{\theta_s}(x)^{(i)}/\tau_s) / \sum_k \exp(g_{\theta_s}(x)^{(k)}/\tau_s)\), and \(P_t\) likewise with \(\tau_t\) after subtracting a centre \(c\). With global views \(x_1^g, x_2^g\) and the full view set \(V\) (globals plus locals), minimise

$$\sum_{x \in \{x_1^g, x_2^g\}} \ \sum_{x' \in V,\ x' \ne x} H\big(P_t(x), P_s(x')\big), \qquad H(a, b) = -\textstyle\sum_k a_k \log b_k,$$

with respect to \(\theta_s\) only. The teacher follows \(\theta_t \leftarrow \lambda \theta_t + (1 - \lambda)\theta_s\), with \(\lambda\) increased from 0.996 towards 1 on a cosine schedule. There are no negative pairs, no contrastive loss and, for ViTs, no predictor head. That is the surprise.

Multi-crop (from SwAV) supplies the learning signal: two global crops covering more than half the image and several small local crops. Because the teacher sees only global crops, matching them from local crops forces local-to-global correspondence: a patch of fur has to predict “this is a dog”. The EMA teacher is a temporal ensemble of past students, and the paper shows it outperforms the student throughout training, so the student is always chasing a slightly better target.

The method: collapse, and the two fixes

A student matching a teacher that is a copy of itself has trivial solutions. There are two: every image maps to the same one-hot output (one dimension dominates), or every image maps to the uniform distribution. Either makes \(H(P_t, P_s)\) easy to minimise and carries no information.

Argument · centring and sharpening push in opposite directions
  • Centring subtracts \(c\), an EMA of the teacher's mean output over batches (momentum \(m = 0.9\)). A dimension that is large for every image is large on average, so subtracting the mean removes it: centring prevents one dimension from dominating. But on its own it drives outputs towards uniform.
  • Sharpening uses a low teacher temperature (\(\tau_t\) of 0.04 to 0.07, below the student's 0.1), making each teacher distribution peaked. That prevents uniform outputs. But on its own it encourages one dominant dimension.

Each fix encourages the other failure, so neither works alone, and the balance between them is the anti-collapse mechanism. The paper's diagnostic plots show it: with only one of the two, the teacher's entropy goes to zero or to its maximum and the KL between teacher and student drops to zero, the signature of collapse; with both, neither happens.

import numpy as np

def softmax(z, temp):
    z = z / temp
    e = np.exp(z - z.max(axis=1, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)

def dino_loss(student, teacher, center, tau_s=0.1, tau_t=0.04):
    """student: list of (B, K) logits for every crop; teacher: logits for the 2 global crops."""
    total, n = 0.0, 0
    for i, t in enumerate(teacher):
        p_t = softmax(t - center, tau_t)              # centre, then sharpen; no gradient flows here
        for j, s in enumerate(student):
            if j == i:
                continue                              # never compare a view with itself
            total += -(p_t * np.log(softmax(s, tau_s) + 1e-12)).sum(axis=1).mean()
            n += 1
    return total / n

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
B, K = 256, 64
logits = rng.normal(size=(B, K))
logits[:, 0] += 5.0                                   # one dimension dominates for every image
center = logits.mean(axis=0)                          # in DINO: an EMA of teacher batch means

def report(p):                                        # mean entropy; share of images on dim 0
    H = -(p * np.log(p + 1e-12)).sum(axis=1).mean()
    return f"entropy {H:.2f}, share on dim 0: {(p.argmax(axis=1) == 0).mean():.2f}"

print("sharpen only:    ", report(softmax(logits, 0.04)))          # low entropy, all on dim 0
print("centre + sharpen:", report(softmax(logits - center, 0.04)))  # low entropy, spread out
print("centre only:     ", report(softmax(logits - center, 1.0)))   # high entropy: near uniform
crops = [rng.normal(size=(B, K)) for _ in range(4)]   # student outputs: 2 global + 2 local crops
print(f"loss {dino_loss(crops, crops[:2], np.zeros(K)):.2f}")   # teacher sees the 2 global crops

Sharpening alone puts 99% of images on the dominant dimension; centring plus sharpening gives peaked but diverse outputs; centring alone gives near-uniform ones (entropy 3.7 of a possible ln 64 ≈ 4.16).

Evidence

DINO. Frozen features are excellent k-NN classifiers: the abstract reports 78.3% ImageNet top-1 with k-NN on a small ViT (ViT-S with 8×8 patches) and 80.1% with linear evaluation for ViT-Base. The k-NN number is the headline because nothing is trained on top of the features. The attention maps work directly as object masks, including for video object segmentation; supervised ViTs' maps are far less object-centric.

DINOv2. The underrated half is the data. The authors build LVD-142M, 142 million images, by automatic curation of a much larger uncurated web pool: remove near-duplicates with copy detection, then use self-supervised embeddings to retrieve uncurated images close to those of curated datasets (ImageNet-22k, ImageNet-1k, Google Landmarks and fine-grained collections). Training combines DINO's image-level loss with iBOT's masked patch-level loss, Sinkhorn–Knopp centring and a KoLeo regulariser that spreads features out, with a short high-resolution phase at the end; a ViT-g of about 1.1B parameters is trained and distilled into smaller models. The frozen features, with only linear heads, do well across classification, fine-grained recognition, segmentation and monocular depth, competitive with or better than weakly supervised models such as OpenCLIP on many benchmarks, by the authors' report, without any text. Ablations show the curated data beating uncurated data of the same size.

Limits & what broke
  • Fragile balance. Temperatures, centring momentum, EMA schedule and head design must be tuned together; small changes can cause partial collapse, and the paper's ablations show how narrow some settings are.
  • Attention-map artifacts. Large DINOv2 models (and other large ViTs) put a few high-norm tokens in uninformative background patches, used as global scratch space, which corrupts attention maps. “Vision Transformers Need Registers” (Darcet et al. 2024) added a few extra tokens to absorb this.
  • Curation is hand-designed. The retrieval seeds come from existing curated datasets, so DINOv2 inherits their coverage and biases; the pipeline needs a strong embedding model to start.
  • Scale is expensive. The billion-parameter model and curated set put the recipe beyond most labs; people use the distilled checkpoints.
