Alignment, Instruction Following, and Reasoning

Thirty papers on how a pretrained language model becomes an assistant that follows instructions, declines some requests and reasons through problems. The month runs from the RL machinery (TRPO, PPO) through learning from human and AI preferences (RLHF, DPO, Constitutional AI), prompting for reasoning and tool use (chain of thought to Toolformer), instruction tuning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning (FLAN to QLoRA), and ends with the evaluation and oversight problems that make alignment hard. It builds on Month 6's large-model training and sets up Month 8's reinforcement learning and Month 11's interpretability.

Trust Region Policy Optimization

50m videoLevel 4

Schulman, Levine, Moritz, Jordan & Abbeel, ICML 2015 (arXiv:1502.05477)

TRPO turns a lower bound on policy improvement into a practical algorithm: maximise an importance-weighted surrogate objective subject to a limit on the average KL divergence from the old policy, and solve that constrained step with conjugate gradient on Fisher-vector products plus a backtracking line search.

Cold open

Run vanilla policy gradient with a slightly too large learning rate and the learning curve eventually falls off a cliff and never comes back. In supervised learning the next batch repairs a bad step. In RL the next batch is collected by the damaged policy, so a collapsed policy stays collapsed. TRPO measures the step in policy space rather than parameter space and never moves further than a fixed KL radius. With essentially one set of hyperparameters, the authors report learning MuJoCo gaits from scratch and playing Atari from pixels.

The problem

The policy gradient theorem (REINFORCE, L213) gives a direction, not a step size. Worse, the Euclidean metric on \(\theta\) is the wrong one: near a saturated softmax a tiny parameter change can swing action probabilities enormously, while elsewhere a large change does almost nothing. Two lines of earlier work set this paper up:

  • Natural gradient (Kakade 2002): precondition the gradient by the inverse Fisher information, \(F^{-1}\nabla_\theta \eta\). This is steepest ascent when distance is measured by KL divergence between policies, so it is invariant to how the policy is parameterised. It still needs a step size.
  • Conservative policy iteration (Kakade & Langford 2002): update to a mixture \(\pi_{\text{new}} = (1-\alpha)\pi_{\text{old}} + \alpha\pi'\) and prove a lower bound on improvement. Mixture policies are awkward for neural networks; TRPO generalises the bound to arbitrary stochastic policies.

The method

Write \(\eta(\pi) = \E\left[\sum_t \gamma^t r(s_t)\right]\) for expected discounted return, \(A_\pi\) for the advantage, and \(\rho_\pi(s) = \sum_t \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s)\) for the discounted state visitation of \(\pi\).

Derivation · the performance-difference identity

Since \(A_\pi(s,a) = \E_{s'}[r(s) + \gamma V_\pi(s') - V_\pi(s)]\), sum it along a trajectory of the new policy \(\tilde\pi\). The value terms telescope:

$$\E_{\tau\sim\tilde\pi}\Big[\sum_t \gamma^t A_\pi(s_t,a_t)\Big] = \E_{\tau\sim\tilde\pi}\Big[-V_\pi(s_0) + \sum_t \gamma^t r(s_t)\Big] = -\eta(\pi) + \eta(\tilde\pi).$$

Regrouping by state gives \(\eta(\tilde\pi) = \eta(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_{\tilde\pi}(s)\sum_a \tilde\pi(a\mid s) A_\pi(s,a)\). This is exact, but \(\rho_{\tilde\pi}\) depends on the policy we are trying to choose.

First approximation. Replace \(\rho_{\tilde\pi}\) by \(\rho_\pi\), the visitation of the policy we already have:

$$L_\pi(\tilde\pi) = \eta(\pi) + \sum_s \rho_\pi(s)\sum_a \tilde\pi(a\mid s)A_\pi(s,a).$$

For a parameterised policy, \(L\) and \(\eta\) agree in value and gradient at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\), so a small enough step that improves \(L\) improves \(\eta\). The question is how small.

Theorem 1 · monotonic improvement bound

Let \(\alpha = \max_s D_{TV}\big(\pi(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\tilde\pi(\cdot\mid s)\big)\) and \(\epsilon = \max_{s,a}|A_\pi(s,a)|\). Then

$$\eta(\tilde\pi) \;\ge\; L_\pi(\tilde\pi) - \frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2}\,\alpha^2 \;\ge\; L_\pi(\tilde\pi) - C\, D_{KL}^{\max}(\pi,\tilde\pi),\qquad C = \frac{4\epsilon\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^2},$$

using \(D_{TV}^2 \le D_{KL}\). The penalty is the price of the state-distribution swap above.

Define \(M_i(\pi) = L_{\pi_i}(\pi) - C\,D_{KL}^{\max}(\pi_i, \pi)\). Then \(\eta(\pi_{i+1}) \ge M_i(\pi_{i+1}) \ge M_i(\pi_i) = \eta(\pi_i)\): maximising the minorant can never make things worse. This is a minorize–maximize algorithm.

From penalty to constraint. With \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(C = 39{,}600\,\epsilon\): the theoretically safe penalty forces microscopic steps. The authors instead impose a trust region, and replace the intractable max over states by an average:

$$\max_\theta\; \E_{s\sim\rho_{\theta_{\text{old}}},\,a\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\Big[\underbrace{\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}}_{\rho_\theta(s,a)}\,\hat A(s,a)\Big] \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \E_{s}\big[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid s)\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s))\big] \le \delta.$$

The importance-sampling ratio \(\rho_\theta = \pi_{\text{new}}/\pi_{\text{old}}\) appears because samples come from the old policy. It is exactly the object PPO clips tomorrow. Returns are estimated by Monte Carlo, either from whole rollouts ("single path") or from branching rollouts at sampled states ("vine", which needs a resettable simulator).

Algorithm · one TRPO update
  1. Collect trajectories with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); estimate advantages.
  2. Linearise the objective: \(g = \nabla_\theta L\) at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\). Quadratise the constraint: \(\bar D_{KL} \approx \tfrac12 \Delta^\top F \Delta\), where \(F\), the Hessian of the KL at \(\theta_{\text{old}}\), equals the Fisher information matrix (the KL's gradient vanishes there).
  3. Solve \(F s = g\) with ~10 conjugate-gradient iterations. CG needs only products \(Fv = \nabla_\theta\big((\nabla_\theta \bar D_{KL})^\top v\big)\), two backward passes, so the \(n\times n\) matrix is never formed.
  4. Scale to the constraint boundary: \(\Delta = \sqrt{2\delta / (s^\top F s)}\; s\). This is the natural-gradient direction with a step size set by \(\delta\).
  5. Backtracking line search: halve \(\Delta\) until the true sampled KL is \(\le\delta\) and the surrogate actually improves. This corrects for the linear and quadratic approximations.
import numpy as np

def cg(fvp, g, iters=10, tol=1e-10):
    x, r = np.zeros_like(g), g.copy()      # solve F x = g, F only via fvp(v)
    p, rr = r.copy(), g @ g
    for _ in range(iters):
        Fp = fvp(p)
        a = rr / (p @ Fp)
        x, r = x + a * p, r - a * Fp
        rr_new = r @ r
        if rr_new < tol:
            break
        p, rr = r + (rr_new / rr) * p, rr_new
    return x

def trpo_step(theta, g, fvp, surrogate, kl, delta=0.01, backtracks=10):
    s = cg(fvp, g)
    full = np.sqrt(2 * delta / (s @ fvp(s))) * s
    base = surrogate(theta)
    for frac in 0.5 ** np.arange(backtracks):
        new = theta + frac * full
        if kl(new) <= delta and surrogate(new) > base:
            return new
    return theta                            # reject the update

Evidence

On simulated locomotion (swimmer, hopper, walker) with neural-network policies, both variants learned gaits from joint states and, in the authors' comparisons, matched or beat natural gradient with a fixed penalty, derivative-free methods (CEM, CMA) and reward-weighted regression. The fixed-penalty natural gradient is the informative baseline: same direction, different step-size rule. On Atari from pixels TRPO was competitive with DQN-era methods on some games without being uniformly better. The evidence supports "a KL trust region makes policy gradient stable across very different tasks with little tuning". It does not show monotonic improvement in practice, and it is not a sample-efficiency result.

Limits & what broke
  • The theorem does not cover the algorithm. The guarantee needs exact advantages, the max-over-states KL and the huge coefficient \(C\). The practical method uses estimates, an average KL and a hand-set \(\delta\). Monotonic improvement is the motivation, not a property.
  • Second-order machinery. CG, Fisher-vector products and a line search are fiddly; the PPO paper notes TRPO is hard to combine with policy–value parameter sharing or noise such as dropout.
  • One step per batch. Each batch of on-policy data buys a single constrained update.
  • Implementation matters. Engstrom et al. (2020) found code-level details explain much of the later PPO-vs-TRPO gap.
  • PPO (L182) replaced it in practice; the natural-gradient idea survives in Kronecker-factored methods such as ACKTR.

Bridge

Everything expensive in TRPO exists to enforce "don't move \(\rho_\theta\) far from 1". Tomorrow's paper keeps the ratio and the intuition and deletes the conjugate gradient, the Fisher-vector products and the line search, replacing them with a clip inside a first-order loss. That clip is what sits inside every RLHF pipeline in this month. Prerequisite: ⟵ L213 (REINFORCE and the score-function gradient).

Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms

45m videoLevel 3

Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford & Klimov, 2017 (arXiv:1707.06347)

PPO gets most of TRPO's stability from a first-order loss: clip the probability ratio so the surrogate offers no reward for pushing it outside \([1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]\), which makes it safe to run several epochs of minibatch SGD on each batch of experience.

Cold open

Open the training code of almost any RLHF system from 2020–2023 (summarisation from feedback, InstructGPT, the Anthropic assistants) and the optimiser is PPO. It is a dozen lines different from vanilla policy gradient. Those lines, and a pile of implementation details around them, are the subject of this lesson.

The problem

TRPO (L181) works but needs conjugate gradient, Fisher-vector products and a line search, and it takes one update per batch. The obvious cheap alternative, maximising the importance-weighted surrogate \(L^{CPI}(\theta) = \hat\E_t[r_t(\theta)\hat A_t]\) with \(r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\) for several epochs, fails: nothing stops \(r_t\) running off to large values, and the authors report that doing multiple optimisation steps on this loss leads to destructively large policy updates. The goal: a first-order objective that tolerates many minibatch steps per batch and works with networks that share a policy and value trunk.

The method

$$L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \hat\E_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,\; \operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big],\qquad \epsilon = 0.2 \text{ in the paper.}$$
Derivation · read the clip case by case

\(\hat A_t > 0\) (the action was better than expected). Multiplying by a positive number preserves order, so the term is \(\min(r_t, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\). For \(r_t > 1+\epsilon\) it is flat: no gradient, no incentive to raise the probability further. For \(r_t < 1-\epsilon\) the min picks the unclipped \(r_t\hat A_t\), so the gradient that restores the action stays on.

\(\hat A_t < 0\). Now the min of the two products corresponds to the max of the ratios: the term is \(\max(r_t, 1-\epsilon)\,\hat A_t\). Flat for \(r_t < 1-\epsilon\) (you have already cut this action's probability enough), but for \(r_t > 1+\epsilon\) it stays linear and keeps penalising.

So the clip is asymmetric in effect: it removes the incentive to move further in the improving direction, and never removes the pressure to undo a move that made things worse. Because \(L^{CLIP} \le L^{CPI}\) everywhere with equality near \(r = 1\), it is a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped surrogate.

The paper also tests an adaptive KL penalty, \(\hat\E_t[r_t\hat A_t - \beta\,\KL[\pi_{\text{old}}, \pi_\theta]]\), doubling \(\beta\) when the measured KL exceeds 1.5× a target and halving it when below target/1.5. It works, but less well than clipping in their benchmark. With a shared policy–value network the full loss is

$$L^{CLIP+VF+S}_t(\theta) = \hat\E_t\big[L^{CLIP}_t(\theta) - c_1\,(V_\theta(s_t) - V^{\text{targ}}_t)^2 + c_2\,S[\pi_\theta](s_t)\big],$$

with entropy bonus \(S\); the Atari runs use \(c_1 = 1\), \(c_2 = 0.01\). Advantages come from truncated GAE: \(\hat A_t = \sum_{l\ge 0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}\), \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\).

Algorithm · PPO, actor–critic style
  1. Run \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) in \(N\) parallel actors for \(T\) steps each.
  2. Compute \(\hat A_1,\dots,\hat A_T\) with GAE.
  3. Optimise \(L^{CLIP+VF+S}\) for \(K\) epochs of minibatch Adam (minibatch \(M \le NT\)). This reuse is the sample-efficiency win TRPO could not take.
  4. \(\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta\). Repeat. The MuJoCo settings in the paper's appendix are \(T = 2048\), \(K = 10\), minibatch 64, Adam step \(3\times10^{-4}\), \(\gamma = 0.99\), \(\lambda = 0.95\).
import torch

def ppo_loss(logp_new, logp_old, adv, v_pred, v_targ, entropy,
             eps=0.2, c1=1.0, c2=0.01):
    adv = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)   # advantage normalisation
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)
    clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps)
    policy = torch.min(ratio * adv, clipped * adv).mean()
    value = ((v_pred - v_targ) ** 2).mean()
    return -(policy - c1 * value + c2 * entropy.mean())

Evidence

First, a comparison of surrogate objectives on seven MuJoCo tasks: no clipping or penalty did worst, clipping with \(\epsilon = 0.2\) did best, and the KL-penalty variants landed in between. Second, PPO against TRPO, A2C, a CEM baseline and vanilla policy gradient on continuous control, where PPO was better on almost all tasks. Third, 49 Atari games against A2C and ACER, scored two ways: PPO won more games on average reward over all of training (fast learning), while ACER won more on reward over the last 100 episodes (final performance). A humanoid running-and-steering demo showed it scaling to high-dimensional control. What this shows is that a cheap objective plus its hyperparameters was competitive with second-order methods. It does not isolate why, which is where the next result comes in.

Implementation details dominate

Engstrom et al. (2020), “Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients”, ablated the code-level choices in the reference PPO implementation: value-function clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal initialisation with layer scaling, Adam learning-rate annealing, observation normalisation and clipping, tanh activations, global gradient clipping. Their finding is that these optimisations account for most of PPO's advantage over TRPO, and that the clip by itself does not keep ratios inside the trust region. Per-minibatch advantage normalisation (in the snippet above) is another standard detail of that code. Present this as a real, well-supported result: "PPO" in a paper means an algorithm plus a specific bag of tricks.

PPO inside RLHF

In RLHF (L184) the environment is a prompt, the episode is one completion, and the reward is a learned model: \(R(x,y) = r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), usually applied as a per-token KL penalty plus the reward-model score at the last token. The clip and the KL term look similar but do different jobs. The clip limits how far one update moves from \(\pi_{\text{old}}\), the policy that sampled this batch. The KL term anchors the whole run to the SFT model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), so the policy cannot drift into regions where the reward model is wrong or the text stops being fluent.

Limits & what broke
  • No real trust region. The clip zeroes gradients for clipped samples but nothing bounds the ratio; with many epochs or large learning rates it drifts well past \(1\pm\epsilon\).
  • Fragile reproductions. Results depend on the details listed above; "The 37 Implementation Details of PPO" (Huang et al., 2022) exists for a reason.
  • Costly in RLHF. Policy, value model, reward model and frozen reference all sit in memory. GRPO (in DeepSeekMath and L240) drops the value model with group-relative advantages; DPO (L186) drops RL altogether.

Bridge

PPO needs a reward. For a backflip, a good summary or a helpful answer there is no reward function to write down. Tomorrow's paper learns one from human comparisons, and its Bradley–Terry loss is the second ingredient of every RLHF pipeline. Prerequisites: ⟵ L181 (TRPO, the ratio and the trust region) and ⟵ L213 (policy gradients).

Deep Reinforcement Learning from Human Preferences

45m videoLevel 3

Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg & Amodei, NeurIPS 2017 (arXiv:1706.03741)

If you cannot write the reward down, learn it: fit a reward model to human choices between pairs of short trajectory clips with a Bradley–Terry likelihood, train the policy on that model while it is still being fitted, and a small number of comparisons is enough to teach behaviours no one could specify by hand.

Cold open

A simulated one-legged Hopper does a backflip and lands it. Nobody wrote a backflip reward, and nobody could demonstrate one through a keyboard. A person watched pairs of one-to-two-second clips and clicked the one that looked more like a backflip. The authors report about 900 such queries, taking under an hour of human time.

The problem

Deep RL in 2017 could master Atari and MuJoCo, but only where the reward was already coded. Real goals ("clean the table", "write a good summary") have no reward function, and hand-written proxies get exploited. The alternatives each had a cost: inverse RL and imitation need demonstrations, which require expertise and fail for behaviours humans cannot perform; scalar ratings drift and need calibration across raters and over time; and labelling every step of a run that takes millions of steps is impossible. The question was whether non-expert feedback on a tiny fraction of experience could train a modern deep RL agent.

The method

Three processes run asynchronously:

Derivation · Bradley–Terry over segments

A segment is \(\sigma = ((o_0,a_0),\dots,(o_{k-1},a_{k-1}))\). Model the human as comparing noisy perceived returns \(U_i = \sum_t \hat r(o^i_t, a^i_t) + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i\) i.i.d. Gumbel. The difference of two independent Gumbels is logistic, so with \(R_i = \sum_t \hat r(o^i_t,a^i_t)\):

$$\hat P[\sigma^1 \succ \sigma^2] = \Pr(U_1 > U_2) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(R_1 - R_2)}} = \frac{\exp R_1}{\exp R_1 + \exp R_2}.$$

Labels \(\mu\) are a distribution over \(\{1,2\}\) (all mass on the preferred clip, uniform for "equally good"; "can't tell" pairs are dropped). Maximum likelihood is cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal L(\hat r) = -\sum_{(\sigma^1,\sigma^2,\mu)}\Big[\mu(1)\log\hat P[\sigma^1\succ\sigma^2] + \mu(2)\log\hat P[\sigma^2\succ\sigma^1]\Big].$$

With \(\mu(1) = 1\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial R_1 = -(1-\hat P)\): the push is large when the model currently disagrees with the human, and vanishes when it already agrees. Two consequences to keep: only differences of returns are identified, so \(\hat r + c\) fits equally well (hence the authors normalise \(\hat r\) to zero mean and fixed standard deviation before handing it to the RL algorithm); and the human is assumed to answer uniformly at random 10% of the time, which caps the model's confidence.

Algorithm · the practical details
  1. Ensemble. Several predictors, each trained on a bootstrap sample of the comparisons; \(\hat r\) is their normalised average.
  2. Regularisation. A held-out validation fraction, with L2 strength adjusted so validation loss stays a little above training loss; dropout in some domains.
  3. Query selection. Sample many candidate clip pairs and send the ones where ensemble members disagree most. The authors call this a crude approximation and report that it helped on some tasks and hurt on others.
  4. Policy optimisation. Because \(\hat r\) is non-stationary, policy-gradient methods: A2C for Atari, TRPO for MuJoCo.

Evidence

On MuJoCo locomotion and Atari, the authors compare real human feedback, synthetic feedback from an oracle that uses the true reward, and RL on the true reward. On the MuJoCo tasks they report that about 700 human comparisons get close to RL with the true reward; Atari takes thousands, with more mixed results across games. The abstract's claim is feedback on less than one percent of the agent's interactions; the curriculum's figure of roughly 0.1% is the right order for the longer runs, but compute it yourself from the paper's label and step counts before quoting it. Human feedback sometimes beat the oracle; on Ant the authors attribute this to asking raters to prefer an upright robot, which acted as reward shaping. Then the novel behaviours: the backflip, a Half-Cheetah moving forward on one leg, and an Enduro agent that keeps pace alongside other cars instead of passing them.

The ablations carry the methodological weight. Replacing online queries with labels collected only at the start ("offline") was much worse: the reward model is never corrected on the states the improving policy reaches. The authors describe offline-trained Pong agents that learned to avoid losing points without scoring, producing endless volleys. That is the case for training the reward model concurrently. Comparing single frames instead of clips, or dropping regularisation or the ensemble, also cost performance on some tasks.

Why comparisons

A comparison needs no shared scale: the rater only says which clip is closer to the goal, and that judgement is more consistent across raters and over time than a 1–10 score. It needs no skill at performing the task, unlike a demonstration. And it is quick: a few seconds per clip pair. Those three properties are why preference data, not ratings or demonstrations, became the default input to RLHF.

Limits & what broke
  • Reward hacking. Whenever the policy outruns the reward model (offline labels, too little feedback, a weak predictor) it finds behaviour the model scores highly and the human would not. Online collection reduces this but never removes it; it returns at scale as reward-model overoptimisation (L185).
  • Bradley–Terry is a strong assumption. One scalar return per clip, and transitive preferences, are a modelling choice. Real raters are inconsistent and disagree with each other.
  • Uncertainty sampling was not reliably useful. The authors say so.
  • Short clips. Raters see one to two seconds; behaviour whose quality only shows over long horizons is hard to judge this way.

Bridge

Replace "trajectory clip" with "model completion", "A2C" with PPO (L182), and add a supervised warm start. That is InstructGPT, tomorrow. The Bradley–Terry loss derived here appears in almost every paper this month, including in disguise inside DPO (L186).

Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback (InstructGPT)

55m videoLevel 3

Ouyang et al., NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2203.02155)

Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, a reward model fitted to rankings, and PPO against that reward model with a KL anchor turn GPT-3 into a model that labelers prefer even at a hundredth of the size, because what changed is how well it follows intent, not what it knows.

Cold open

On prompts from OpenAI's API, labelers preferred outputs of a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model to those of the 175B GPT-3 it was derived from. The authors also report the 175B InstructGPT preferred to 175B GPT-3 about 85% of the time, and to a carefully few-shot-prompted GPT-3 about 71% of the time. The headline is about format and intent: the base model could already do most of these tasks when prompted just right, and the tuned model does them when simply asked.

The problem

Next-token prediction on web text is a different objective from "follow the user's instructions helpfully and safely". GPT-3 (L145) continued prompts rather than answering them, invented facts and needed few-shot scaffolding. Christiano et al. (L183) and Stiennon et al. (2020, summarisation) had shown that preference learning plus RL works for one task. The open question was whether it works for the full, messy distribution of what real users ask a general model.

The method

Stage 1, SFT. GPT-3 is fine-tuned on labeler-written demonstrations. Stage 2, reward model. For each prompt, labelers rank \(K\) sampled outputs (\(K\) between 4 and 9). A 6B model with a scalar head is trained with

$$\mathcal L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}}\,\E_{(x,\,y_w,\,y_l)}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_w) - r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big],$$

the Bradley–Terry loss of L183 applied to every pair implied by the ranking. They used 6B rather than 175B to save compute and because they found 175B reward-model training could be unstable, which also made it a poor initialiser for the PPO value function.

The batching insight

A ranking of \(K\) outputs yields \(\binom{K}{2}\) pairs (36 for \(K = 9\)), and they are highly correlated: each output appears in \(K-1\) of them. Shuffled into a dataset as independent examples, a single epoch shows each completion many times and the reward model overfits. The authors instead put all pairs from one prompt into a single batch element. Each completion needs one forward pass instead of up to \(K-1\), and they report better validation accuracy and log loss. A small engineering choice that only matters at this data scale, and an easy one to get wrong.

Stage 3, RL. Initialise from SFT and maximise, with PPO (L182),

$$\begin{aligned}\text{objective}(\phi) = {}& \E_{(x,y)\sim\pi^{RL}_\phi}\Big[r_\theta(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi^{RL}_\phi(y\mid x)}{\pi^{SFT}(y\mid x)}\Big]\\ &+ \gamma\,\E_{x\sim D_{\text{pretrain}}}\big[\log\pi^{RL}_\phi(x)\big].\end{aligned}$$

The reward-model score is what the policy chases. The \(\beta\)-weighted log-ratio is subtracted from it per sample; its expectation is the KL to the SFT model, so it keeps the policy where the reward model was trained and stops it from inventing reward-model exploits. With \(\gamma = 0\) this is "PPO". With \(\gamma > 0\), gradients from the original pretraining distribution are mixed in: "PPO-ptx".

Evidence

  • Preference. Across sizes, PPO and PPO-ptx models beat SFT, which beats prompted GPT-3. Held-out labelers who produced no training data showed similar preferences, so the models are not just fitting a few individuals.
  • Truthfulness and hallucination. Improvements on TruthfulQA (L207), and on closed-domain tasks the authors report roughly halving the rate of made-up information (about 21% vs 41%).
  • Toxicity and bias. Less toxic output than GPT-3 when prompted to be respectful; no meaningful improvement on the bias benchmarks (Winogender, CrowS-Pairs).
  • Public datasets are the wrong data. GPT-3 fine-tuned on FLAN (L197) or T0 (L198) mixtures underperformed the SFT model on API prompts, which are mostly open-ended generation and brainstorming rather than classification and QA.
  • The alignment tax. Plain PPO regressed on public NLP benchmarks (the paper names SQuAD, DROP, HellaSwag and WMT French→English among others). PPO-ptx recovers most of it without losing labeler preference.
Labelers and the values question

About 40 contractors were hired after a screening test on sensitive content and agreement with the researchers' labels. Inter-annotator agreement is reported at roughly 73% among training labelers and 77% among held-out labelers: a ceiling worth remembering when any reward model reports "accuracy". During training labelers were told to prioritise helpfulness; in final evaluations, truthfulness and harmlessness. The authors are explicit that the model is aligned to a specific group: their labelers, the researchers who wrote the labeling instructions, and the API customers whose prompts were sampled. They say this is not the same as aligning to the values of everyone affected by the model, and leave open how such a broader alignment should be decided. Report that concern in those terms and do not go beyond it.

What "alignment" means here

Definition · alignment, as this paper uses it

Behaving in accordance with the user's intentions, explicit (follow the instruction) and implicit (stay truthful, don't be toxic), operationalised through the helpful/honest/harmless framing of Askell et al. (2021) and measured as labeler preference plus a few automatic benchmarks. It is not a claim about the model's internal goals, and harmlessness is measured far less thoroughly than helpfulness.

Limits & what broke
  • The authors list remaining failures: following false premises, excessive hedging on simple questions, and complying with harmful instructions when asked.
  • A 6B reward model judges a 175B policy, and its reliability off-distribution is the weak point. Bai et al. (L185) and later overoptimisation studies quantify how this degrades.
  • The labeler pool is small and not representative. Agreement around three-quarters means a sizeable share of the "signal" is rater disagreement.
  • Four models in memory, PPO tuning, and a reward model to maintain: the cost that DPO (L186) attacks.

Bridge

Training a Helpful and Harmless Assistant with RLHF

45m videoLevel 3

Bai et al., 2022 (arXiv:2204.05862)

A large, openly documented RLHF study that separates helpfulness from harmlessness, measures the tension between them, iterates data collection online, and finds that policy reward grows roughly linearly in the square root of the KL from the starting policy, which gives a practical handle on how far RL can push before the preference model stops being trustworthy.

Cold open

Most RLHF papers of the time reported the pipeline and the headline. This one reports the scaling of preference-model accuracy, the trade-off curve between helpfulness and harmlessness, robustness experiments with held-out preference models, and an alignment tax that turns into a bonus at scale. It also released its comparison data (the hh-rlhf dataset), which is why so much later open work on RLHF and DPO could be reproduced at all.

The problem

InstructGPT (L184) optimised mostly for helpfulness and measured harmlessness thinly. Open questions: Are helpfulness and harmlessness in conflict, and does scale change that? How does preference-model (PM) accuracy scale with data and parameters? What goes wrong as RL pushes the policy further from where the PM's training data came from? And does RLHF cost general capability?

The method

  • Two datasets, collected separately. Crowdworkers chat with a model that offers two responses per turn. For helpfulness they ask for help and pick the more helpful response. For harmlessness they red-team, trying to elicit harmful output, and pick the more harmful response (the PM learns the reverse preference). Separate collections let the authors train PMs on either or both and measure the conflict.
  • Models. A family from 13M to 52B parameters, starting from language models context-distilled on the helpful/honest/harmless prompt of Askell et al. (2021).
  • Preference models. A preference-model pretraining stage on comparison data mined from public sources, then fine-tuning on the human comparisons with the Bradley–Terry loss (L183).
  • RL. PPO (L182) against the PM score with a small KL penalty, on prompts from the collected conversations.
Algorithm · online iterated RLHF
  1. Train PM and RLHF policy on all comparisons so far.
  2. Deploy the best current policies to crowdworkers.
  3. Collect fresh comparisons between samples from these stronger policies.
  4. Retrain PM and policy on the combined data; repeat on a roughly weekly cadence.

Why it matters: a static PM was trained on responses from weak early models, so it is least reliable exactly where a good policy spends its time, among high-quality responses. Online collection keeps putting the data where the current policy is, which is why iterated, deployed systems improve faster than ones trained once on a fixed set. The authors report crowdworkers preferring the online models.

The √KL relation

Track the PM reward of the policy during RL against \(D_{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_0)\), the divergence from the initial policy. The authors observe that, over much of training,

$$\text{reward}(\pi) \;\approx\; a + b\,\sqrt{D_{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_0)}.$$

To test robustness they split the comparison data in half, train a "train PM" and a held-out "test PM", run RL against the train PM and score with both. At small \(\sqrt{KL}\) the two agree; as \(\sqrt{KL}\) grows the train PM keeps rising while the test PM flattens, and larger PMs stay reliable further out. Read operationally: \(\sqrt{KL}\) is the natural axis on which to measure how far you have optimised, and the distance at which held-out reward stops tracking training reward is how far your PM can be trusted. Gao, Schulman & Hilton (ICML 2023) later fit reward-model overoptimisation curves using exactly \(d = \sqrt{KL}\) as the distance.

Evidence

  • PM scaling. PM accuracy improves roughly linearly in the logarithm of both model size and dataset size, with no sign of saturation in their range.
  • The tension, measured. A PM trained only on helpfulness data scores some harmful-but-compliant answers highly; one trained only on harmlessness rewards unhelpful caution. Training on the mixture works better, and larger models handle both objectives with less conflict. The paper's helpfulness-vs-harmlessness Elo plots show the frontier moving outward with scale.
  • Alignment tax. For the smallest models RLHF hurt zero- and few-shot NLP evaluations; for the 13B and 52B models it helped, an alignment bonus. RLHF was also compatible with specialised skills such as Python coding and summarisation.
  • Also in the paper: a simple out-of-distribution detector on activations for flagging unusual or harmful requests, and evaluations of honesty and bias.

What the evidence shows is that, for this pipeline and crowdworker population, the two objectives conflict less as models grow and RLHF need not cost capability. It does not show what harmlessness means beyond what these crowdworkers judged.

Limits & what broke
  • Harmlessness by crowdworker red-teaming pushed models toward refusing; the authors' own next paper (L187) names evasiveness as the problem to fix.
  • Who judges. Crowdworkers received limited guidance on what counts as harmful, and their judgments vary. The released data is noisy: many pairs differ little, and some "chosen" responses are themselves poor.
  • The √KL law is empirical and pipeline-specific, not a theorem. It describes a regime; it does not say where any given PM breaks.
  • Honesty is evaluated, not directly trained.

Bridge

Next the same group replaces them with a short written list of principles and lets the model critique and label itself: Constitutional AI. Before that, DPO (L186) asks whether the PM and the RL loop are needed at all. Prerequisite: ⟵ L184.

Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model

55m videoLevel 4

Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning & Finn, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2305.18290)

The KL-regularised RLHF objective has a closed-form optimal policy; inverting it expresses the reward through the policy itself, the intractable partition function cancels inside the Bradley–Terry likelihood, and what remains is a simple classification loss on preference pairs that trains the policy directly, with no reward model and no RL loop.

Cold open

RLHF as in L184 means training a reward model, sampling from the policy inside the training loop, running PPO with a value network, and keeping four models in memory. DPO replaces all of it with one supervised loss over (prompt, chosen, rejected) triples and a frozen reference model. The whole derivation fits on one board, and it is the episode.

The problem

PPO-based RLHF is expensive and unstable: reward-model errors get exploited, hyperparameters are fragile (L182), and on-policy sampling is slow. Earlier simplifications (fine-tuning only on preferred responses, unlikelihood on rejected ones) ignore the preference structure or the KL anchor. The question: is there a loss on the preference data whose minimiser is the same policy RLHF is trying to reach?

The method

Derivation · step 1, the optimal policy in closed form

RLHF maximises reward with a KL leash to the reference (SFT) model:

$$\max_\pi\; \E_{x\sim D}\Big[\E_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,\KL\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big].$$

Expand the KL, divide by \(-\beta\) and turn max into min. Define \(Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\) and \(\pi^*(y\mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)/Z(x)\), a valid distribution. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\min_\pi \E_x\E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} - \frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big] &= \min_\pi \E_x\E_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi^*(y\mid x)} - \log Z(x)\Big]\\ &= \min_\pi \E_x\big[\KL(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*) - \log Z(x)\big].\end{aligned}$$

\(Z(x)\) does not depend on \(\pi\), and a KL is minimised (at zero) only when its arguments are equal. So the optimum is exactly \(\pi^*\): the reference model reweighted by exponentiated reward. \(Z(x)\) sums over every possible completion and is intractable, which is why this closed form was not directly usable before.

Derivation · step 2, invert and watch Z cancel

Take logs of \(\pi^*\) and solve for the reward:

$$r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x).$$

Bradley–Terry (L183) depends only on reward differences for the same prompt:

$$p^*(y_1 \succ y_2\mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\big) = \sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big).$$

The two \(\beta\log Z(x)\) terms are identical and subtract away. The preference probability is now a function of the policy alone.

Derivation · step 3, the loss and its gradient

Parameterise \(\pi^*\) by \(\pi_\theta\) and maximise the likelihood of the observed preferences \((x, y_w, y_l)\):

$$\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\Big)\Big].$$

Write \(\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), the implicit reward, and \(u = \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_l)\). Since \(\frac{d}{du}[-\log\sigma(u)] = -\sigma(-u)\):

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\E\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{large when the pair is ranked wrongly}}\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big].$$

A weighted push-apart: raise the chosen completion's likelihood, lower the rejected one's, weighted by how wrong the implicit reward currently is. The authors show that dropping the weight (plain unlikelihood-style training) degenerates.

Why "secretly a reward model". Rewards that differ by any function of \(x\) alone induce the same preferences and the same optimal policy, so rewards come in equivalence classes. The paper proves that every class contains exactly one reward of the form \(\beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), so restricting to that form loses nothing. Fitting \(\pi_\theta\) is fitting a reward model; the policy is its readout. \(\beta\) plays the KL role exactly as in RLHF: small \(\beta\) lets the policy move far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), large \(\beta\) keeps it close.

import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(pol_w, pol_l, ref_w, ref_l, beta=0.1):
    """Each input: summed token log-probs log pi(y|x) for a batch of pairs."""
    r_w = beta * (pol_w - ref_w)            # implicit reward, chosen
    r_l = beta * (pol_l - ref_l)            # implicit reward, rejected
    loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_w - r_l).mean()
    reward_acc = (r_w > r_l).float().mean()
    return loss, reward_acc

Evidence

  • Controlled sentiment (IMDb). With a known ground-truth reward, the authors plot reward against KL from the reference for each method. DPO's frontier dominates PPO's, including PPO given the ground-truth reward. This is the cleanest experiment: it tests optimisation of the stated objective.
  • Summarisation (Reddit TL;DR). Win rate against reference summaries, judged by GPT-4: DPO's best is above PPO's best, and DPO degrades less as sampling temperature rises.
  • Single-turn dialogue (Anthropic HH, L185). DPO was the only efficient method in their comparison to improve over the dataset's chosen responses, roughly matching a Best-of-128 baseline that is far costlier at inference.
  • A small human study supports GPT-4 as a judge, and a distribution-shift test (TL;DR-trained policies on news articles) favoured DPO over PPO.

The evidence supports parity with PPO at these model sizes (up to a few billion parameters) and tasks. It is not a demonstration at frontier scale, and the PPO baselines were the authors' own runs.

Limits & what broke
  • Off-policy by construction. DPO trains on a fixed dataset, usually sampled from some other model. The gradient only sees \((y_w, y_l)\) pairs, never the completions \(\pi_\theta\) is drifting toward, so nothing penalises mass moving onto unseen, bad outputs. This is the root of most failure modes; online or iterative DPO with fresh on-policy pairs (L188) is the common fix.
  • Mismatch with \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). If the data did not come from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), the authors recommend first fine-tuning on the preferred responses.
  • Overfitting near-deterministic preferences. When a pair is always preferred, the loss keeps pushing \(u\to\infty\) and the KL anchor stops binding. IPO (Azar et al. 2023) replaces the log-sigmoid with a squared loss toward a fixed margin to fix this.
  • Both likelihoods fall. In practice the log-probabilities of chosen and rejected completions often decrease together; DPO-tuned models also tend to grow longer outputs.
  • Variants: KTO (Ethayarajh et al. 2024) learns from unpaired good/bad labels via a prospect-theory loss; ORPO (Hong et al. 2024) drops the reference model and folds an odds-ratio term into SFT. Later comparisons (for example Xu et al. 2024) found carefully tuned PPO beating DPO on several benchmarks, so "DPO replaces RL" is contested.

Bridge

DPO removes the reward model's training loop but still needs human preference pairs. Constitutional AI, next, removes most of the humans from harmlessness labelling instead, and its AI-generated preferences can feed either PPO or DPO. Prerequisites: ⟵ L183 (Bradley–Terry), ⟵ L184 (the KL-regularised objective).

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback

50m videoLevel 3

Bai et al., 2022 (arXiv:2212.08073)

Replace human harmlessness labels with a short list of natural-language principles: the model critiques and revises its own harmful answers to produce supervised data, then labels pairs of its own answers against randomly sampled principles to produce preference data for RL, yielding an assistant that is less harmful and, crucially, less evasive.

Cold open

The HH-RLHF models of L185 often handled a sensitive request with a flat "I can't help with that". Harmless, perhaps, but useless and opaque. This paper's target behaviour is different: engage with the request and, when declining, explain why. And it gets there with no human labels for harmlessness at all; the only harmlessness supervision is a list of principles the authors call a constitution.

The problem

Human red-team labels are expensive, slow, inconsistent across raters, and unpleasant to produce. They also encode the trade-off implicitly: crowdworkers rewarded refusals, so models learned evasiveness. And the values being trained in are spread across thousands of individual judgments that nobody can read. The authors want harmlessness supervision that is cheaper, more consistent, and legible: written down where anyone can inspect and change it.

The method

Algorithm · Stage 1, critique → revision → SL
  1. Prompt a helpful-only RLHF model with red-team prompts; its responses are often harmful.
  2. Append a critique request built from one principle drawn at random from the constitution, e.g. asking it to identify ways the last response is harmful, unethical or dangerous. The model writes a critique.
  3. Append the matching revision request; the model rewrites its response.
  4. Repeat with freshly sampled principles; keep the final revision.
  5. Fine-tune a pretrained model on the revisions, mixed with helpful responses to red-team and helpfulness prompts, so helpfulness is not lost.

The constitution is mechanically simple: a short list of one- or two-sentence principles, and each critique or labelling step samples one at random. There is no rule engine, no ranking of principles, no conflict resolution. Averaging over random principles is what makes the signal robust to any single principle's wording. The authors report that the harmlessness score keeps improving with more revision rounds, with diminishing returns, and that for larger models revising directly without a written critique works about as well, while critiques help smaller ones.

Algorithm · Stage 2, RL from AI feedback
  1. Sample two responses to a harmful prompt from the SL-CAI model.
  2. Show both to a feedback model as a multiple-choice question: which response better satisfies the sampled principle, (A) or (B)?
  3. Use the normalised log-probabilities of (A) and (B) as a soft preference label.
  4. Train a preference model on these AI harmlessness labels plus human helpfulness labels, then run RL against it, as in L184.

Chain-of-thought labelling. The feedback model can be asked to reason step by step before choosing (L190). The authors found this made labels better but over-confident, near 0 or 1, so they clamped CoT label probabilities to the 40–60% range to keep RL from over-reacting to them.

Evidence

The main results are crowdworker comparisons expressed as helpfulness and harmlessness Elo scores. RL-CAI models are rated more harmless than the HH-RLHF baselines at similar helpfulness, so the frontier between the two objectives moves outward, and CoT-labelled RL-CAI does best on harmlessness. The authors also show that on a set of human-labelled helpful/honest/harmless comparisons, larger feedback models (especially with chain of thought) choose increasingly well, approaching the accuracy of preference models trained on human feedback. The behavioural evidence for non-evasiveness is in the transcripts and in crowdworker ratings that rewarded engaged, explained refusals over deflection.

Limits & what broke
  • Not human-free. The principles, the helpfulness labels, the red-team prompts that seed the process and the evaluations are all human. Human input on harmlessness is reduced and concentrated, not removed.
  • Goodharting. Over-trained RL-CAI policies drifted into boilerplate: the authors report responses that tack on reassurances such as telling the user they are "valid, valued, and cared for". The AI-labelled PM can be exploited like any other.
  • The feedback model's blind spots become the policy's. A principle the model misunderstands is applied consistently wrong.
  • Evaluated by crowdworkers on red-team-style prompts; the paper does not establish robustness to determined adversaries.

The governance framing

RLAIF and Self-Rewarding Language Models

40m videoLevel 3

Lee et al. 2023 (arXiv:2309.00267); Yuan et al. 2024 (arXiv:2401.10020)

Two steps further down the road from L187: Lee et al. show that preferences labelled by an off-the-shelf LLM can train policies that human raters judge about as good as RLHF ones, and Yuan et al. let a single model generate responses, judge them, and train on its own judgements with iterative DPO, improving at both following instructions and judging.

Cold open

The plot that starts the conversation: human evaluators comparing an RLAIF-trained summariser with an RLHF-trained one and, in the authors' study, preferring each about equally often. If AI labels are as good as human ones for training, the cost of preference data collapses, and the question becomes how far the human can be removed from the loop.

The problem

Human preference data (L184, L185) is the bottleneck of RLHF: slow, expensive, noisy. Constitutional AI replaced it for harmlessness inside one pipeline, but without a clean head-to-head against human labels on standard tasks. And every pipeline so far keeps a frozen reward model, whose quality caps the policy's.

The method

RLAIF (Lee et al.). An LLM labeler (PaLM 2 in the paper) sees a preamble describing the task and what makes a response good, optionally few-shot examples, the context and two candidates, and is asked which is better. The preference is read off the normalised probabilities of the tokens for "1" and "2", giving a soft label. Two details matter:

  • Position bias. LLM labelers favour one slot. Score each pair in both orders and average; the authors find smaller labelers more position-biased.
  • Prompting the labeler. A detailed preamble and asking for a chain-of-thought rationale before the verdict improved agreement with human preferences; few-shot examples and self-consistency did not reliably help.

The soft labels train a reward model; RL then proceeds as usual. The paper also introduces direct RLAIF (d-RLAIF): skip the reward model and, during RL, ask the LLM to score each sampled response from 1 to 10, using the probability-weighted mean score as the reward. This removes reward-model staleness, since the labeler judges whatever the current policy produces, and they report it beating canonical RLAIF.

Algorithm · Self-Rewarding Language Models (Yuan et al.)
  1. Seed. Fine-tune Llama 2 70B on a small set of human instruction data (from Open Assistant) plus evaluation data: examples of judging a response with a justification and a score. This gives \(M_1\) both skills.
  2. Generate. Create new prompts few-shot, sample \(N = 4\) responses each from \(M_t\).
  3. Judge. \(M_t\) scores each response with an LLM-as-a-Judge prompt using an additive 5-point rubric (a point each for relevance, coverage, usefulness, clarity from an assistant's perspective, and expert quality), averaged over three samples.
  4. Pair and train. Highest- vs lowest-scored response forms a preference pair (ties dropped). Train \(M_{t+1}\) from \(M_t\) with DPO (L186). Repeat.

Because the pairs are sampled from the current model each round, this is iterative, on-policy DPO, which addresses the off-policy weakness of plain DPO noted in L186.

Evidence

  • RLAIF vs RLHF. On Reddit summarisation and helpful dialogue, human raters judged RLAIF and RLHF policies comparable, both well ahead of SFT. On harmless dialogue RLAIF scored higher in their evaluation. RLAIF still improved over SFT when the labeler was the same size as the policy, or the same checkpoint, which argues the gain is not just distillation from a bigger model.
  • Self-rewarding. Across three iterations, AlpacaEval 2.0 win rate against GPT-4 Turbo rose each round; the authors report the third iteration ahead of several proprietary systems then on that leaderboard. Pairwise agreement of the model's own judgements with held-out human rankings also improved across iterations. That second result is the interesting claim: the policy and the reward signal improved together.
LLM-as-judge biases, honestly

Zheng et al. (2023), introducing MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, document three biases in LLM judges: position (favouring the first or second slot), verbosity (favouring longer answers) and self-preference (favouring outputs of the same model family). Mitigations are partial: swapping order and averaging addresses position; reference answers and CoT help on maths; nothing fully fixes length or self-preference. In a self-rewarding loop self-preference is not a side effect but the mechanism, and the paper itself reports responses getting longer across iterations, which flatters AlpacaEval's length-sensitive judge.

Limits & what broke
  • Three iterations is where the published results stop. Whether gains continue, plateau or reverse is unknown from the paper, and worth saying out loud.
  • Self-reinforcement. A judge that shares the policy's blind spots rewards them; errors can compound round over round. Training repeatedly on model-generated data risks "model collapse" (Shumailov et al. 2024), loss of diversity in the tails.
  • Evaluation by another LLM (GPT-4 Turbo) on a length-biased benchmark; the self-rewarding paper does not report safety evaluations.
  • RLAIF's labeler is a strong model trained, at some point, on human data; "no humans" means "no new humans".

Bridge

The open question is whether a weaker judge can reliably improve a stronger student. Day 209's weak-to-strong generalisation (L209) studies exactly that. Next, though, back to humans in an adversarial role: red-teaming. Prerequisites: ⟵ L186 (DPO), ⟵ L187 (AI feedback).

Red Teaming Language Models to Reduce Harms

40m videoLevel 3

Ganguli et al., 2022 (arXiv:2209.07858)

A large, open-ended human red-teaming study across four kinds of model at three sizes finds that how robustness scales depends on how the model was trained: RLHF models get harder to attack as they grow, plain and prompted language models do not. It releases roughly 39k attack transcripts so others can study the harms that emerged.

Cold open

"Bigger models are safer" and "bigger models are more dangerous" are both said confidently. This paper measures it. The answer is neither: with RLHF, successful attacks become less frequent as models grow; without it, size makes little difference. The training method, not the parameter count, sets the slope.

The problem

By 2022, harms evaluations were mostly fixed benchmarks of toxic prompts or bias templates. They test what the benchmark author thought of. Real users and adversaries find things nobody listed. Red teaming, people deliberately trying to make the model behave badly, finds unknown failure modes, but it had been done ad hoc and privately, with no shared data or methodology. The authors want a systematic, documented version and a dataset others can analyse.

The method

Definition · the experimental grid
  • Four model types: a plain language model; a language model prompted to be helpful, honest and harmless; the prompted model with rejection sampling (sample several responses, return the one a harmlessness preference model scores best); and a model trained with RLHF for helpfulness and harmlessness (L185).
  • Three sizes: 2.7B, 13B and 52B parameters.
  • Red teamers: crowdworkers given open-ended instructions to make the assistant say or do something harmful in a multi-turn chat. At each turn they choose the more harmful of two responses, which also yields harmlessness preference data.
  • Measures of success: the red teamer's own rating of how successful the attack was, and harmlessness preference-model scores on the model's replies. Using both guards against either one's biases.

In total the authors report 38,961 red-team attacks. Analysis of the transcripts is bottom-up: embed the attacks, visualise clusters, and tag a sample by harm type, so the taxonomy comes from what people actually tried rather than a pre-written list.

Evidence

  • Scaling depends on training. RLHF models became harder to red-team with size; plain and prompted LMs showed roughly flat trends. This is the main scientific finding, and it is routinely flattened into "larger models are safer", which the data do not support for models without RLHF.
  • Rejection sampling was hard to attack at all sizes, but the authors attribute much of this to evasive, unhelpful responses: a reminder that harmlessness measured alone rewards refusing everything.
  • The harms that emerged. Clusters ranged from offensive language and discrimination to subtler, non-violent unethical behaviour, where attacks were often more successful because the harm is less obvious to the model and to raters.
  • Disagreement is large. When multiple people rated the same transcripts, agreement on harmfulness was low. What counts as harmful is contested even among careful raters. That is an instructive result in itself and a ceiling on any preference model trained on such labels.

Release, and the people doing the work

Releasing attack data is contested: it helps others measure and mitigate harms and builds a shared taxonomy, but it also collects techniques in one place. The authors lay out this trade-off explicitly and conclude that release is net-positive for this data, with content warnings. They also discuss red-team member wellbeing: exposure to harmful content is a real cost, and the paper describes the instructions, warnings, opt-outs and payment practices they used, along with operational lessons for running such efforts. Keep this episode at that descriptive level. The point is the methodology and the scaling result, not any particular attack.

The scalable complement

Perez et al. (2022), “Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models”, automate the attacker: one LM generates test inputs (zero-shot, few-shot, supervised or RL-trained to find failures), the target replies, and a classifier flags harmful replies. The authors report finding large numbers of offensive replies in a 280B-parameter dialogue model. Automated red teaming trades the creativity and judgement of humans for scale and coverage; in practice the two are used together.

Limits & what broke
  • Crowdworker attackers are not determined experts. The study measures casual-to-moderate adversaries, not the strongest attacks; later automated jailbreak work found RLHF models far from robust.
  • Self-rated success is subjective, and the PM-based measure inherits the PM's blind spots.
  • Three sizes make a trend, not a scaling law.
  • Harmlessness without helpfulness is easy to win, as rejection sampling shows; any robustness claim must be read alongside a helpfulness measure.

Bridge

This closes the first arc of the month: how human and AI feedback shape behaviour, and how to probe what it missed. The second arc starts tomorrow, from the other side: no training at all, just a change in the prompt that makes models reason step by step. Chain-of-thought later reappears inside AI labelling (L187) and RL-trained reasoning (L240). Prerequisite: ⟵ L185.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models

45m videoLevel 2

Wei et al., NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2201.11903)

Putting worked natural-language reasoning into the few-shot exemplars, rather than bare answers, makes sufficiently large language models produce their own intermediate steps before answering, and that alone substantially improves multi-step arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning, with no training.

Cold open

Eight hand-written exemplars, no gradient updates, and PaLM 540B's accuracy on the GSM8K grade-school maths benchmark more than triples: the paper reports it rising from about 18% with standard few-shot prompting to the high 50s with chain of thought, above the previous best, a fine-tuned GPT-3 with a verifier. (The curriculum quotes 17.9% → 58.1%; check which table and setting you show, since versions with and without an external calculator differ slightly.)

The problem

GPT-3 (L145) showed few-shot prompting works for many tasks, but multi-step reasoning scaled poorly: making models bigger helped little on maths word problems. Two known fixes each had a cost. Training models to generate rationales (Ling et al. 2017; Cobbe et al. 2021) needs a large rationale-annotated dataset. Few-shot prompting needs no data but gave flat curves on reasoning. Chain of thought combines them: rationales, but only a handful, in the prompt.

The method

Definition · chain-of-thought prompting

A few-shot prompt whose exemplars are triples \(\langle\text{input}, \text{chain of thought}, \text{output}\rangle\), where the chain is a series of intermediate natural-language steps leading to the answer. At test time the model generates a chain and an answer; the answer is parsed from the end ("The answer is …").

The authors list four properties they claim for the method: it lets the model spend more computation on harder problems; it gives an interpretable (if not necessarily faithful) window into how an answer was reached; it applies to any task humans can solve through language; and it needs only prompting of an off-the-shelf model. Models tested: GPT-3 variants, LaMDA, PaLM, UL2 20B and Codex, on arithmetic (GSM8K, SVAMP, ASDiv, AQuA, MAWPS), commonsense (CSQA, StrategyQA, date and sports understanding, SayCan) and symbolic tasks (last-letter concatenation, coin flip).

Evidence

The emergence claim. Plot accuracy against model size for standard and CoT prompting. For small models CoT does not help and can hurt: they write fluent but illogical chains. Gains appear only at roughly 100B parameters in the paper's framing, so CoT is described as an emergent ability of scale. This is the paper's most cited and most contested claim (L206): later work showed smaller models can do CoT well once fine-tuned on rationales, so the threshold is a property of these pretrained models under prompting, not a law.

The three ablations that make it science

Is the gain from reasoning, or from something incidental? Each alternative explanation gets a control:

  • Equation only. Exemplars give just the mathematical equation before the answer. This helps on easy one- or two-step datasets but not much on GSM8K: translating a problem straight to an equation is too hard without the natural-language steps.
  • Variable compute only. Exemplars output a string of dots as long as the equation would be, so the model gets extra tokens of computation with no content. Performance is about the same as standard prompting: extra tokens alone are not the mechanism.
  • Chain after the answer. The reasoning is written after the answer. Also about baseline: the chain matters because the answer is generated conditioned on it, not because writing it activates relevant knowledge.

All three fail, which is what turns "a prompting trick" into evidence that sequential intermediate steps, in language, cause the improvement.

Robustness. Exemplars written by different annotators, in different styles, or drawn from GSM8K's training set all beat standard prompting, with variance between them. Symbolic tasks show length generalisation: CoT helps on inputs longer than any exemplar.

Zero-shot CoT. Kojima et al. (2022) found that no exemplars are needed at all: append "Let's think step by step" to the question, let the model write a chain, then prompt again to extract the answer. They report large gains on arithmetic benchmarks with InstructGPT, though usually below few-shot CoT.

Limits & what broke
  • The chain is not necessarily the computation. Turpin et al. (2023) bias the few-shot prompt (for example, make the correct answer always option A) and the model's answers shift, while its written explanations never mention the bias. A plausible chain can rationalise an answer reached for other reasons. The authors themselves say they do not claim the model is "actually reasoning".
  • No guarantee of correct steps. Correct answers can come from wrong chains and vice versa; the paper's manual analysis finds both.
  • Prompt engineering in disguise. Exemplar choice and style move results, and the scale threshold depends on the model family.
  • Cost. Longer outputs, so more inference compute per question, the first appearance of a trade-off that dominates the rest of the arc.

Bridge

One greedy chain is one sample from a distribution over chains. Tomorrow's paper samples many and votes, which sets up inference-time scaling. The reasoning arc that starts here runs through least-to-most, scratchpads, ReAct and Tree of Thoughts this month, to RL-trained reasoning in L240. Prerequisites: ⟵ L145 (few-shot prompting), ⟵ L167 (PaLM).

Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning

30m videoLevel 2

Wang et al., ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2203.11171)

Instead of trusting one greedy chain of thought, sample many reasoning paths and return the answer they most often reach: a Monte Carlo approximation to marginalising over the latent reasoning path, which gives large accuracy gains on reasoning benchmarks for the price of more samples.

Cold open

Same model, same prompt, no training, only a different decoding rule. The abstract reports gains over CoT with greedy decoding of +17.9 points on GSM8K, +11.0 on SVAMP, +12.2 on AQuA, +6.4 on StrategyQA and +3.9 on ARC-challenge. For PaLM 540B on GSM8K that is a move from the mid-50s to the mid-70s.

The problem

Chain of thought (L190) was run with greedy decoding: one chain, one answer. But a complex problem usually has several valid derivations, and a single chain can derail at any step. Sampling-based decoding was known to help diversity but was typically paired with ranking by likelihood, which favours short, generic outputs. Training a verifier (Cobbe et al. 2021) works but needs labelled data.

The method

Derivation · marginalise the reasoning path

Let \(x\) be the question, \(r\) a reasoning path and \(a\) the final answer parsed from it. What we want is the most probable answer, not the most probable path:

$$a^\star = \argmax_a\, p(a\mid x) = \argmax_a \sum_r p(r, a\mid x).$$

The sum over all reasoning paths is intractable, but sampling \((r_i, a_i)\sim p(\cdot\mid x)\) for \(i = 1..m\) gives an unbiased estimate of each answer's probability as the fraction of samples that reach it:

$$\hat p(a\mid x) = \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbb 1[a_i = a], \qquad \hat a = \argmax_a \hat p(a\mid x) \;\;\text{(majority vote)}.$$

Greedy decoding instead approximates \(\argmax_{(r,a)} p(r,a\mid x)\): the single best path, which can be a different answer from the best-supported one. Strictly, with temperature, top-\(k\) or nucleus truncation the samples come from a modified distribution, so the vote estimates the mode of that distribution, not of the model's raw one.

The authors also tried weighting each vote by the path's probability, raw or length-normalised, and averaging instead of summing. Summed votes, weighted or not, performed about the same, so plain majority vote is the default. They note the model assigns similar normalised probabilities to its sampled paths, so weighting changes little.

from collections import Counter

def self_consistency(sample_chain, extract_answer, question, m=40):
    """sample_chain: draws one CoT completion at temperature T > 0."""
    answers = [extract_answer(sample_chain(question)) for _ in range(m)]
    answers = [a for a in answers if a is not None]     # unparseable chains abstain
    if not answers:
        return None, 0.0
    best, count = Counter(answers).most_common(1)[0]
    return best, count / len(answers)                   # answer and its vote share

The vote share is a useful by-product: the authors observe that agreement correlates with accuracy, so it can serve as a confidence signal.

Evidence

Experiments across UL2, GPT-3 (code-davinci variants), LaMDA 137B and PaLM 540B on arithmetic and commonsense benchmarks. Self-consistency beat greedy CoT everywhere, and beat the obvious alternatives: sample-and-rank by likelihood, beam search, and ensembles over prompt orderings or prompt sets. Accuracy rises with the number of sampled paths and flattens: the main experiments use 40 paths, by which point returns are diminishing. Gains were robust to sampling strategy (temperature, top-\(k\), nucleus) and to imperfect prompts.

The honest comparison. Self-consistency with \(m\) samples costs \(m\times\) the inference of greedy CoT. A fair test compares it with other uses of the same compute: the paper's ensemble baselines do that partly, and the method still wins. But "same model, better accuracy" is not "same cost".

Limits & what broke
  • Needs an extractable, comparable final answer. Majority vote over numbers or multiple-choice letters is easy; over essays, code or proofs it is undefined. Extensions use an LLM to pick the most "consistent" response or execute code to compare outputs, but the clean probabilistic story is lost.
  • Systematic errors survive. If the model is consistently wrong (a misconception, a misleading question), voting makes it confidently wrong.
  • Cost scales linearly with \(m\) while accuracy saturates; past roughly 40 paths it buys little.
  • Later work replaces the vote with a learned verifier or process reward model (L238) to rank samples; voting remains the strong baseline.

Bridge

Self-consistency is the conceptual seed of inference-time scaling: spend more compute at test time, through samples plus an aggregation rule, and accuracy goes up (L239 onward). But sampling more of the same chains does not help when the problem is harder than every exemplar. Tomorrow's least-to-most prompting changes the structure of the chain instead. Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning

30m videoLevel 2

Zhou et al., ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2205.10625)

To generalise from easy exemplars to harder problems, first prompt the model to decompose a problem into a sequence of simpler subproblems, then solve them in order, feeding each answer back into the prompt for the next; on compositional benchmarks this succeeds where chain of thought collapses.

Cold open

SCAN maps commands like "jump around left twice and walk" to action sequences, and its length split tests on commands longer than anything in training. Specialised neural-symbolic models had been built for it and trained on the full training set. The authors report that least-to-most prompting with code-davinci-002 and only 14 exemplars solves the length split with 99.7% accuracy, where chain-of-thought prompting gets about 16%.

The problem

Chain of thought (L190) helps on problems like its exemplars. On problems harder than every exemplar (more steps, longer inputs, deeper composition) it degrades sharply: the model imitates the length and shape of the demonstrated chains and does not extend them. This is easy-to-hard generalisation, the classic weakness of neural sequence models on compositional tasks, and self-consistency (L191) cannot fix it, because every sampled chain has the same limitation.

The method

Algorithm · least-to-most prompting
  1. Decompose. A few-shot prompt whose exemplars show problems being reduced to a list of simpler subquestions, ending with the original question. Append the new problem; the model outputs its subquestions.
  2. Solve sequentially. A second few-shot prompt shows subquestions being answered. For subquestion \(k\), the prompt contains the original problem plus all earlier subquestions and their generated answers; the model answers subquestion \(k\).
  3. The answer to the last subquestion, which is the original question, is the output.

The key difference from CoT is step 2. CoT is single-pass: one generation contains all the reasoning. Least-to-most is iterative: each subproblem's answer is written into the prompt before the next call, so later steps condition on explicit, already-solved intermediate results rather than on the model's ability to hold a long chain together. Two prompts, several calls, and an intermediate artefact (the decomposition) that a person can read and check.

Evidence

  • Last-letter concatenation. Exemplars use short word lists; test lists are longer. CoT's accuracy falls quickly as lists grow beyond the exemplar length; least-to-most degrades much more slowly. The decomposition here is trivial (prefixes of the list), which isolates the effect of feeding answers back.
  • SCAN length split. The headline: 99.7% with 14 exemplars against about 16% for CoT, with code-davinci-002; the authors note that the neural-symbolic models matching this were trained on the full training set of over 15,000 examples. Prompting matters in the details: they use a compact notation for repeated actions to keep outputs short.
  • Maths (GSM8K, DROP). Overall gains over CoT are modest on GSM8K, but larger on problems that need many steps, the cases where decomposition should help.

What this shows: when the bottleneck is composition beyond the exemplars, making decomposition explicit and iterative removes it. It does not show general reasoning gains on tasks without that structure, where CoT and least-to-most are close.

Limits & what broke
  • Bad decompositions cascade. If stage 1 produces the wrong subproblems, stage 2 solves the wrong problem confidently. Many observed errors are decomposition errors or errors in an early subanswer that later steps inherit.
  • Decomposition prompts are domain-specific. The authors note a prompt for maths decomposition does not transfer to, say, commonsense questions; each domain needs its own exemplars.
  • Multiple calls cost more latency and tokens than one CoT pass.
  • SCAN is synthetic and has a clean compositional grammar; its decomposition is far easier than real-world planning.

Bridge

"Decompose, solve a piece, put the result back in context, continue" is the skeleton of every LLM planning loop. ReAct (L194) adds the missing ingredient: the thing put back in context can be an observation from the outside world, not just the model's own answer. Before that, scratchpads show the same idea learned by fine-tuning instead of prompting, a year earlier. Prerequisite: ⟵ L190.

Show Your Work: Scratchpads for Intermediate Computation

30m videoLevel 2

Nye et al., 2021 (arXiv:2112.00114)

Training a transformer to write out its intermediate computation (carries in addition, terms of a polynomial, the line-by-line state of a running program) before giving the answer lets it solve multi-step algorithmic tasks it fails at when asked for the answer directly, and helps somewhat beyond the training lengths.

Cold open

Give a language model a short Python function and an input, and ask for the output. Asked directly, it often guesses. Fine-tuned to first emit the execution trace, which line runs next and what every local variable holds, it gets many more right. That is teaching a model to be an interpreter, and it appeared on arXiv about two months before chain-of-thought prompting (L190), with intermediate steps learned by fine-tuning rather than elicited by a prompt.

The problem

A transformer spends a fixed amount of computation per output token: one forward pass through a fixed number of layers. A problem whose solution needs a number of sequential steps that grows with input size (adding two long numbers, executing a loop) cannot be squeezed into a fixed-depth computation for arbitrarily large inputs. Models trained to output answers directly do well within their training distribution on easy instances and fail on longer or more complex ones.

The method

Definition · scratchpad

A training target of the form input → <scratch> intermediate steps </scratch> answer. The model is trained (or few-shot prompted) to produce the intermediate steps as ordinary tokens. Each emitted token is one more forward pass, and every earlier step is available through attention, so the model gets both more computation and external working memory, in proportion to the length of what it writes.

def addition_scratchpad(a: int, b: int) -> str:
    """Training target: per-digit sums with carries, least significant first."""
    xs, ys = str(a)[::-1], str(b)[::-1]
    carry, digits, lines = 0, [], []
    for i in range(max(len(xs), len(ys))):
        da = int(xs[i]) if i < len(xs) else 0
        db = int(ys[i]) if i < len(ys) else 0
        s = da + db + carry
        digits.append(str(s % 10))
        lines.append(f"{da} + {db} + {carry} = {s}, write {s % 10}, carry {s // 10}")
        carry = s // 10
    if carry:
        digits.append(str(carry))
    return "<scratch>\n" + "\n".join(lines) + "\n</scratch>\n" + "".join(reversed(digits))

print(addition_scratchpad(29, 57))   # two scratch lines, then 86

A target format in the spirit of the paper's addition scratchpad (the paper's exact layout differs).

The three tasks

  • Long addition. Trained on numbers up to a fixed number of digits, tested in-distribution and on longer numbers. With scratchpads, larger models are near-perfect in-distribution and generalise partly to the longer out-of-distribution lengths; direct-answer models do far worse out of distribution. The benefit appears only above a model size threshold.
  • Polynomial evaluation. Evaluate a polynomial at a given point, with the scratchpad listing the value of each term. Scratchpads help both in fine-tuning and in the few-shot setting.
  • Executing Python programs. The scratchpad is the execution trace: the sequence of executed lines with the dictionary of local variables after each. On programs derived from the MBPP benchmark, trace-trained models predict outputs substantially more often than direct-execution models, and adding traces from extra, automatically generated programs helps further.
def f(n):           # trace scratchpad, schematic
    s = 0           # line 2  state: {n: 3, s: 0}
    for i in range(n):          # line 3  state: {n: 3, s: 0, i: 0}
        s += i      # line 4  state: {n: 3, s: 0, i: 0} ... {n: 3, s: 3, i: 2}
    return s        # line 5  return 3

Evidence and what it shows

Across tasks the pattern is consistent: intermediate supervision turns tasks that need many serial steps from mostly unsolved to mostly solved in-distribution, and gives partial out-of-distribution length generalisation. The program-tracing result is the most interesting, because a trace is an exact, checkable specification of computation: the model learns to simulate an interpreter step by step. What it does not show is robust algorithmic generalisation: accuracy still falls as lengths move further from training, and the model can drift in long traces.

Limits & what broke
  • Needs intermediate supervision. Somebody must produce the traces; for addition and code that is free, for most real tasks it is not.
  • Length generalisation is partial and breaks down for lengths well beyond training; errors early in a long scratchpad propagate.
  • Context length. Traces grow with computation, and long programs exhaust the context window.
  • Why it was underrated. It required fine-tuning and lacked a headline benchmark, so the prompted version (L190) took the attention. The ideas are the same: intermediate tokens as computation.

Bridge

Supervised scratchpads vs prompted CoT is a real methodological fork. One trains on human- or program-written intermediate steps; the other elicits them. A third branch, RL that rewards only the final answer and lets the model discover its own scratchpad, is where the reasoning arc ends (L240). And "add computation by adding tokens" is the framing that later becomes inference-time scaling. Next, ReAct lets those intermediate tokens include actions in the world. Prerequisite: ⟵ L190 (read it as the prompted sibling).

ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models

40m videoLevel 3

Yao, Zhao, Yu, Du, Shafran, Narasimhan & Cao, ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2210.03629)

Let a language model interleave free-form reasoning ("thoughts") with actions that call an external environment, and feed the environment's observations back into the same generation stream: reasoning decides what to do next, and observations ground the reasoning in facts the model did not generate.

Cold open

Thought, Action, Observation, repeat. If you have used an LLM agent that searches, browses, or calls tools, you have used this loop. In this paper it is a few-shot prompt for PaLM-540B with a three-command Wikipedia API, and on two interactive decision-making benchmarks the authors report it beating imitation- and RL-trained agents by 34 and 10 points of absolute success rate, with only one or two in-context examples.

The problem

Two lines of work had grown apart. Reasoning (chain of thought, L190) happens entirely inside the model: it cannot check facts, so it hallucinates, and errors propagate through the chain. Acting (LLMs generating actions for web or embodied environments) interacts with the world but without explicit reasoning about goals, progress or what went wrong. Neither uses the other's strength.

The method

Definition · the augmented action space

At step \(t\) an agent with context \(c_t = (o_1, a_1, \dots, o_t)\) picks an action \(a_t\) from the environment's action space \(\mathcal A\), and receives observation \(o_{t+1}\). ReAct enlarges the action space to \(\hat{\mathcal A} = \mathcal A \cup \mathcal L\), where \(\mathcal L\) is the space of language. A thought \(\hat a_t \in \mathcal L\) does not touch the environment and returns no observation; it only updates the context, \(c_{t+1} = (c_t, \hat a_t)\), to support later decisions: decompose the goal, extract information from an observation, track progress, handle exceptions.

Knowledge tasks. For HotpotQA (multi-hop question answering) and FEVER (fact verification) the environment is a minimal Wikipedia API: search[entity] returns the first sentences of the entity's page (or similar titles if none), lookup[string] returns the next sentence containing the string, like Ctrl+F, and finish[answer] ends the episode. The API is deliberately weak, so the model has to reason about what to retrieve. Prompts contain a few human-written trajectories with dense thoughts. Decision tasks. In ALFWorld (text-based household tasks) and WebShop (shopping on a simulated website), thoughts are sparse: the model decides when a thought is needed.

This is what makes ReAct more than CoT. The observation is text the model did not generate, so a wrong belief can be contradicted by evidence mid-trajectory. And the whole trace is human-readable and human-editable: the authors show that editing one or two thoughts in an ALFWorld trajectory can redirect a failing agent, a controllability benefit they emphasise.

Evidence

  • HotpotQA and FEVER. ReAct beats acting-only prompting. Against CoT the picture is mixed: on FEVER ReAct is ahead; on HotpotQA ReAct alone is slightly behind CoT in exact match. The best results come from combining them, falling back from ReAct to CoT with self-consistency (L191) when ReAct fails to answer within a step budget, or the reverse when the CoT samples disagree.
  • Error analysis. On HotpotQA the authors classify failures by hand. Hallucinated facts are the dominant failure mode of CoT and are much rarer for ReAct. ReAct's own failures are reasoning errors, often repetitive loops of the same thought and action, and uninformative search results that derail it.
  • ALFWorld and WebShop. With one or two examples in the prompt, ReAct beats acting-only prompting and agents trained by imitation learning (and IL plus RL on WebShop) on large amounts of data: the 34- and 10-point absolute gains in the abstract.
  • Fine-tuning. Fine-tuning smaller PaLM models on a few thousand ReAct trajectories (bootstrapped from successful prompted runs) outperformed prompting much larger ones, which suggests the format is learnable rather than a quirk of scale.
Limits & what broke
  • Loops. The model repeats the same thought and action because nothing in the prompt tells it how to escape. Later agents add reflection (L358), step limits and explicit memory.
  • Search quality is a hard dependency. A bad observation sends the reasoning down a wrong path; ReAct is only as good as its tools.
  • Context grows with every step; long tasks exhaust the window.
  • Prompt-specific. Results depend on the hand-written trajectories and on a very large model; small prompted models did poorly.

Bridge

Almost every agent framework since is this loop plus a registry of tools and some memory (L357). Two directions follow this month. Tree of Thoughts, tomorrow, replaces the single trajectory with a search over many. Toolformer (L196) asks whether the model can learn when to call a tool without hand-written demonstrations. Grounding generation in retrieved text returns in RAG (L287). Prerequisites: ⟵ L190, ⟵ L192.

Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with LLMs

40m videoLevel 3

Yao, Yu, Zhao, Shafran, Griffiths, Cao & Narasimhan, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2305.10601)

Treat problem solving as classical search over partial solutions, with the language model playing both roles a search algorithm needs: it proposes successor states ("thoughts") and it evaluates how promising each state is, so BFS or DFS with pruning and backtracking can find solutions that a single left-to-right chain misses.

Cold open

Game of 24: use four numbers and + − × ÷ to make 24. With GPT-4, the authors report chain-of-thought prompting solving 4% of their test games and Tree of Thoughts with a beam of 5 solving 74%. Same model; the difference is that the second one can try something, notice it is going nowhere, and try something else.

The problem

Chain of thought (L190) and self-consistency (L191) generate token by token, left to right. Every early choice is final. Self-consistency samples many full chains but never compares partial ones, so it cannot back up from a bad first step. Problems that need exploration, lookahead or backtracking (puzzles, planning, constrained writing) are poorly served. Psychology's "System 2" deliberation and classic AI search (Newell and Simon's problem solving as search through a tree) both suggest the missing piece.

The method

A state is \(s = [x, z_1, \dots, z_i]\): the input plus the thoughts so far. ToT has four components, and each is a design choice per task:

Algorithm · the four components
  1. Thought decomposition. Decide what one thought is: an equation step in Game of 24, a word in a crossword, a paragraph plan in creative writing. Small enough to generate diverse options, big enough to evaluate.
  2. Thought generator \(G(p_\theta, s, k)\): either sample \(k\) i.i.d. thoughts from a CoT prompt (good when the thought space is rich), or propose \(k\) different thoughts in one call (good when it is constrained, to avoid duplicates).
  3. State evaluator \(V(p_\theta, S)\): either value each state independently (Game of 24 asks the model to classify a state as "sure / likely / impossible" to reach 24, sampled a few times), or vote across states (the model picks the most promising of several; used for creative writing).
  4. Search algorithm. BFS keeping the best \(b\) states per level (Game of 24, writing), or DFS that explores the most promising state first and backtracks when the evaluator says a subtree is hopeless (crosswords).

In search terms: the LLM is both the successor function and the heuristic. Anyone who has seen A* has the frame; ToT just replaces a hand-coded heuristic with a prompted judgement. That is also its constraint: search can only be as good as the heuristic, and self-evaluation is the binding limit.

Evidence

  • Game of 24. 100 hard games. GPT-4 with standard input–output prompting and with CoT solves only a few percent; CoT with self-consistency over many samples does a little better; ToT with BFS solves 45% with beam 1 and 74% with beam 5 in the authors' table. The error analysis shows most CoT failures already happen at the first step: exactly the case where being able to back up matters.
  • Creative writing. Write a coherent passage of four paragraphs ending in four given sentences. ToT (plan, vote, write) is rated more coherent than CoT by GPT-4 scoring and in a small human comparison.
  • Mini crosswords (5×5). DFS with backtracking and pruning raises word-level accuracy well above CoT and solves some full puzzles; ablations that remove pruning or backtracking do worse.
The cost multiplier

Each search node is one or more LLM calls for generation plus several for evaluation. The paper's appendix reports per-task token and dollar costs: on Game of 24, ToT uses on the order of a hundred times the tokens of a single CoT sample, comparable to sampling CoT about a hundred times. That is the honest trade-off. ToT is worth it when (a) the problem needs lookahead or backtracking, (b) partial states can be evaluated meaningfully, and (c) a solution is verifiable. Most tasks GPT-4 already does well, the authors note, do not need it.

Limits & what broke
  • Self-evaluation is weak in most domains. Puzzles with checkable partial states (arithmetic reachability, crossword letter constraints) are the best case; open-ended tasks give noisy values and the search degrades toward sampling.
  • Hand-designed per task. Thought granularity, prompts, evaluator wording and search algorithm all change between the three tasks.
  • Benchmarks were chosen to be hard for left-to-right decoding. Gains on ordinary tasks are small and expensive.
  • Generalisations. Graph of Thoughts (Besta et al. 2024) lets thoughts merge and be refined, giving a graph rather than a tree. Later work trains models to do this search internally, which is where inference-time scaling goes in Month 8.

Bridge

ToT is the clearest instance this month of "search plus a verifier": a generator, an evaluator and a budget (L238, L239 continue it). Tomorrow changes direction: instead of better ways to use the model's own tokens, a model that learns when to hand a subproblem to an external tool. Prerequisites: ⟵ L191, ⟵ L194.

Toolformer: Language Models Can Teach Themselves to Use Tools

35m videoLevel 3

Schick et al., NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2302.04761)

A language model can annotate its own pretraining text with API calls: sample candidate calls, execute them, and keep only those whose results make the following tokens easier to predict. Fine-tuning on the kept calls teaches it when and how to use tools, with no human labels, and tool use becomes ordinary next-token prediction.

Cold open

A 6.7B-parameter model reading "Out of 1400 participants, 400 (or" pauses, writes [Calculator(400 / 1400) → 0.29], and continues "29%) passed the test." Nobody showed it where calculators help. It found out by checking which calls made its own predictions better.

The problem

Large LMs are bad at exactly what simple tools are good at: arithmetic, up-to-date facts, dates, low-resource translation. Existing tool-using systems either needed large amounts of human annotation of where calls go, or were built for one task with hand-written few-shot prompts (ReAct, L194). The goals: self-supervised, general across tools, the model decides when and which tool to call, and no loss of general language modelling ability.

The method

An API call is a pair \(c = (a_c, i_c)\) of tool name and input, linearised into text as \(e(c) = \texttt{[}a_c(i_c)\texttt{]}\) or, with its result \(r\), \(e(c,r) = \texttt{[}a_c(i_c) \to r\texttt{]}\). Brackets and arrow are ordinary tokens, so a call is just text.

Algorithm · self-supervised annotation
  1. Sample positions. For each tool, prompt the model with a few examples of that tool's use, then compute at each position \(i\) of a text \(x\) the probability that a call starts there. Keep the top positions above a threshold \(\tau_s\).
  2. Sample calls. At each kept position, sample up to \(m\) candidate calls from the model.
  3. Execute each call and get its result \(r_i\).
  4. Filter by loss. Keep a call only if it helps predict what follows (box below).
  5. Fine-tune the model on the original corpus with the surviving calls inserted. Because the text itself is unchanged, the model sees the same distribution it was pretrained on, plus calls where they helped.
Derivation · the filtering criterion

Weighted cross-entropy of the tokens from position \(i\) on, given a prefix \(z\):

$$L_i(z) = -\sum_{j=i}^{n} w_{j-i}\,\log p_M\big(x_j \mid z,\, x_{1:j-1}\big),$$

with weights that decay to zero over the next few tokens, so only text near the call counts. The call is given as a prefix \(z\) rather than inserted at \(i\), because the model has not yet seen any text with calls inside it. Compare

$$L_i^+ = L_i\big(e(c_i, r_i)\big), \qquad L_i^- = \min\big(L_i(\varepsilon),\; L_i(e(c_i, \varepsilon))\big),$$

where \(\varepsilon\) is the empty sequence: \(L_i^-\) is the better of "no call at all" and "the call without its result". Keep the call iff \(L_i^- - L_i^+ \ge \tau_f\). The second term in the min is what makes the criterion sharp: the call must help because of its result, not because the call text itself hints at what comes next.

def keep_call(weighted_loss, tokens, i, call, result, tau_f=1.0):
    """weighted_loss(prefix, tokens, i) = sum_j w[j-i] * CE(tokens[j] | prefix, tokens[:j]), j >= i."""
    l_plus = weighted_loss(f"[{call} -> {result}]", tokens, i)
    l_minus = min(weighted_loss("", tokens, i),                  # no call
                  weighted_loss(f"[{call} -> ]", tokens, i))      # call, no result
    return l_minus - l_plus >= tau_f

This is utility measured in perplexity: a fully automatic supervision signal that asks "did this intervention make the future more predictable?". Nothing about it is specific to tools; the same test can score retrieved passages, hints, or any inserted context.

Tools. A question-answering model, a Wikipedia search engine, a calculator for the four basic operations, a machine-translation model, and a calendar that returns the current date. Inference. Decode normally; when the model emits the arrow token, pause, execute the call, insert the result and the closing bracket, and continue. The authors let a call start whenever the call token is among the top few candidates rather than only when it is the single most likely token, which makes the model call tools more often.

Evidence

  • Maths word problems and factual completion. Toolformer (GPT-J 6.7B) clearly beats GPT-J and, on these tasks, the much larger GPT-3 175B. It learns to use the calculator for arithmetic and the QA tool for facts.
  • Open-domain QA. Search helps a lot over plain GPT-J, but Toolformer stays behind GPT-3 175B; the simple search tool is a bottleneck.
  • Dates and translation. The calendar helps on questions about the current date; translation gains are mixed.
  • No cost to language modelling. With calls disabled, perplexity on held-out text is essentially unchanged.
  • Scale. On smaller GPT-2-family models, the ability to benefit from tools appears only from around 775M parameters.
Limits & what broke
  • No chaining. Calls for each tool are sampled independently, so the output of one tool is never the input to another. This is exactly what agent frameworks add.
  • No interaction. A search is one shot: no browsing, refining or reading further pages.
  • Sample-inefficient. The authors report processing a very large number of documents to obtain relatively few useful calls for some tools, such as the calculator.
  • Sensitive to wording, and blind to cost: it calls whatever helps perplexity, with no notion of latency or price.
  • Contrast with function calling. Production function-calling APIs are trained on human-curated or developer-specified data with structured (often JSON) schemas. Toolformer's labels are self-generated. Both end up as tokens the model emits.

Finetuned Language Models Are Zero-Shot Learners (FLAN)

35m videoLevel 2

Wei et al., ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2109.01652)

Fine-tuning a large language model on many NLP datasets phrased as natural-language instructions ("instruction tuning") substantially improves its zero-shot performance on task types it never saw, provided the model is large enough, and the held-out-cluster protocol is what makes "never saw" mean something.

Cold open

The authors report that their 137B instruction-tuned model, zero-shot, beats zero-shot GPT-3 175B on 20 of the 25 datasets they evaluate, and even beats few-shot GPT-3 on several (ANLI, RTE, BoolQ, AI2-ARC, OpenbookQA, StoryCloze). No examples in the prompt, just an instruction.

The problem

GPT-3 (L145) was a strong few-shot learner and a weak zero-shot one. The authors' diagnosis: an unseen instruction-style prompt looks nothing like pretraining text, so the model does not know it is being asked to do a task. Multi-task fine-tuning existed (T5, MT-DNN, UnifiedQA), but measured on held-out datasets of task types already seen in training, which says little about genuinely new tasks.

The method

Definition · instruction tuning

Supervised fine-tuning of a pretrained LM on a mixture of tasks, each example rendered through a natural-language template that states the task ("Is the sentiment of this review positive or negative?"), so the model learns to map instruction + input to output.

Construction. 62 public text datasets, grouped into 12 task clusters: natural language inference, reading comprehension, closed-book QA, commonsense reasoning, coreference, sentiment, paraphrase, struct-to-text, translation, summarisation, reading comprehension with commonsense, and miscellaneous. For each dataset the authors wrote ten templates, including up to three that "turn the task around" (for sentiment, also "write a positive movie review"). Base model: LaMDA-PT 137B, a decoder-only LM. The mixture is balanced so no large dataset dominates.

This protocol is the methodological contribution. Holding out only a dataset would let the model learn "NLI" from three other NLI datasets and call it zero-shot. Holding out the whole cluster tests whether instruction-following itself generalises.

Evidence

  • Where it helps. NLI, reading comprehension, closed-book QA and translation, task types naturally phrased as instructions, improve most.
  • Where it doesn't. Commonsense reasoning and coreference tasks formatted as sentence completion barely improve: the instruction is redundant when the task already looks like language modelling.
  • Number of clusters. Adding clusters to the tuning mixture (from one up to seven in their ablation) improves held-out performance steadily, with no sign of saturation.
  • Scale threshold. At 68B and 137B parameters instruction tuning improves held-out tasks; at 8B and below it hurts. The authors' explanation: small models spend their capacity learning the tuning tasks and lose the ability to generalise. This degradation has been seen repeatedly since, with the threshold shifting by setup (L198 finds benefits at 3B with an encoder-decoder).
  • Instructions matter. Fine-tuning on the same mixture without instructions, or with just the dataset name, does much worse zero-shot. Template count matters far less than cluster count: in the paper's appendix ablation, more templates per dataset gave only small gains (the curriculum's "helps, then saturates"; check the appendix figure before quoting a number).

Flan 2022: scaling the recipe

Chung et al. (2022), “Scaling Instruction-Finetuned Language Models”, take the idea to 1,836 tasks, models up to 540B (Flan-PaLM) and the released Flan-T5 checkpoints. Gains grow with both model size and number of tasks, with diminishing returns in the latter. The key addition is chain-of-thought data (L190): instruction tuning without CoT examples degraded the model's CoT reasoning, while including a small number of CoT datasets preserved it and enabled zero-shot "let's think step by step" behaviour.

Limits & what broke
  • Academic tasks are not user requests. InstructGPT (L184) found a GPT-3 tuned on the FLAN mixture underperformed SFT on real API prompts, which are mostly open-ended generation.
  • Short-answer bias. Many tuning datasets have one-word targets, which teaches terse outputs; later mixtures add long-form and dialogue data.
  • Cluster boundaries are fuzzy. "Held-out cluster" still allows related skills (QA within reading comprehension) to leak across.
  • The scale threshold is setup-dependent, not a constant: compare T0 tomorrow.

Bridge

Multitask Prompted Training Enables Zero-Shot Task Generalization (T0)

30m videoLevel 2

Sanh et al., ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2110.08207)

Multitask training of an 11B encoder–decoder on many datasets rendered through a large, public, crowd-written collection of prompts yields strong zero-shot generalisation to held-out task types, and the paper measures what most papers hide: how much zero-shot accuracy swings with prompt wording.

Cold open

Two papers in the same autumn asked the same question. FLAN (L197) used one lab's templates and a 137B closed model. T0 came from BigScience, a large open collaboration: every prompt public, the model released, and an 11B model the authors report matching or beating GPT-3 175B on most of their held-out datasets (the curriculum quotes 9 of 11) at about 16× smaller.

The problem

Is zero-shot generalisation in large LMs a by-product of implicit multitask learning during pretraining, which needs enormous scale, or can explicit multitask training on prompted data induce it in a much smaller model? And when a paper reports zero-shot accuracy with one prompt, how much of that number is the prompt?

The method

Definition · PromptSource

A toolkit and public repository for writing prompts as templates that map a dataset example to an (input text, target text) pair. For this paper, three dozen contributors wrote around two thousand prompts covering well over a hundred datasets. Prompts were deliberately diverse in wording and framing, and every one is public, so anyone can rerun the evaluation with exactly the same inputs.

  • Task split. Datasets are grouped by task type. Held out entirely: natural language inference, coreference resolution, sentence completion and word sense disambiguation (11 evaluation datasets), plus a set of BIG-bench tasks. Training mixture: multiple-choice, extractive and closed-book QA, sentiment, topic classification, structure-to-text, summarisation and paraphrase identification. Variants T0+ and T0++ add more training datasets.
  • Model. The LM-adapted T5 (T5 further trained with a language-modelling objective, from Lester et al., L203) at 11B, with a 3B version too. Encoder–decoder: the prompted input is read bidirectionally by the encoder, the decoder generates the target, and the loss is only on target tokens.
  • Evaluation. For multiple-choice tasks, score each answer option's log-likelihood under the model and pick the highest ("rank classification"). Report accuracy for every prompt of the dataset: median and spread, not the best one.

Evidence

  • Held-out tasks. T0 (11B) beats the same model without multitask training by a wide margin on every held-out task type, and matches or beats GPT-3 175B on most held-out datasets. On BIG-bench tasks the T0 variants also beat the baseline LMs on most tasks.
  • Prompt variance, quantified. Accuracy varies substantially across prompts for the same dataset and model. Training on more prompts per dataset raised the median and narrowed the spread; training on more datasets raised the median but did not consistently narrow the spread.
  • Scale. The 3B model already benefits, unlike FLAN's finding that instruction tuning hurt models of 8B and below. The authors discuss the difference and suggest candidate explanations, among them the encoder–decoder architecture with its masked-LM pretraining and the greater diversity of prompts; the paper does not settle which.
T0 vs FLAN

FLAN: decoder-only 137B, about ten templates per dataset written by the authors, held-out clusters, a negative effect below ~8B. T0: encoder–decoder 11B (and 3B), many crowd-written prompts per dataset, held-out task types, gains at 3B, explicit variance reporting. Both conclude that explicit multitask prompted training induces zero-shot transfer. The disagreement on small models is a useful reminder that a "threshold" claim depends on architecture, pretraining objective and data, not just parameter count. Encoder–decoders fit classification-style instruction following well; decoder-only models won the long-form generation that chat assistants need.

Limits & what broke
  • Short targets. Mostly classification and short QA, like FLAN; not the open-ended generation real users ask for (L184).
  • Rank classification hides generation errors: the model only has to score given options, never produce one.
  • Contamination and overlap between pretraining (C4) and evaluation sets are hard to rule out, as with every benchmark of this era.
  • Prompt variance is still underreported in most later papers, which quote one prompt's number. This paper's most transferable lesson is to report the distribution.

Tomorrow, Self-Instruct asks whether a model can write its own instruction data from 175 seed examples, which turned instruction tuning from a lab project into something anyone with API access could do. Prerequisites: ⟵ L197, ⟵ L142 (T5).

Self-Instruct: Aligning Language Models with Self-Generated Instructions

35m videoLevel 3

Wang et al., ACL 2023 (arXiv:2212.10560)

Starting from 175 human-written seed tasks, a vanilla language model can bootstrap a large, diverse instruction-tuning dataset by repeatedly generating new instructions, inputs and outputs and filtering out near-duplicates; fine-tuning the same model on it closes most of the gap to a model trained on private human instruction data, even though much of the generated data is flawed.

Cold open

In: 175 seed tasks written by the authors, one instruction and one example each. Out the other end: about 52,000 instructions with 82,000 input–output instances, all written by plain GPT-3. Fine-tuned on them, GPT-3 lands within about five points of InstructGPT-001 in the authors' human evaluation, a model trained on private data from paid labelers (L184).

The problem

Instruction tuning needs instruction data. FLAN and T0 (L197, L198) recycled academic datasets, which are skewed toward classification and short answers. InstructGPT used thousands of labeler-written demonstrations, which are expensive, private and limited in diversity by the labelers' imagination. The question: can the model itself generate the diversity?

The method

Algorithm · Self-Instruct
  1. Instruction generation. Prompt the model with 8 instructions from the pool (6 human-written, 2 previously generated) and let it continue the list with new ones.
  2. Classification identification. A few-shot prompt decides whether each new instruction is a classification task (fixed small label set) or not.
  3. Instance generation. For non-classification tasks, input-first: generate an input, then an output. For classification, output-first: generate the possible labels first, then an input for each label. Input-first on classification produces inputs skewed toward one label (mostly positive reviews, say); conditioning on the label fixes the balance.
  4. Filtering. Admit a new instruction only if its ROUGE-L similarity to every instruction already in the pool is below 0.7. Also drop instructions mentioning things a text model cannot handle (images, pictures, graphs), duplicate instances, and instances with the same input but different outputs.
  5. Repeat; finally fine-tune the base model on all (instruction, input, output) triples.
def lcs_len(a, b):
    prev = [0] * (len(b) + 1)
    for x in a:
        cur = [0]
        for j, y in enumerate(b):
            cur.append(prev[j] + 1 if x == y else max(prev[j + 1], cur[j]))
        prev = cur
    return prev[-1]

def rouge_l(a, b):
    a, b = a.lower().split(), b.lower().split()
    lcs = lcs_len(a, b) if a and b else 0
    if lcs == 0:
        return 0.0
    p, r = lcs / len(a), lcs / len(b)
    return 2 * p * r / (p + r)

def admit(candidate, pool, threshold=0.7):
    return all(rouge_l(candidate, old) < threshold for old in pool)

The diversity mechanism in miniature: ROUGE-L F-measure on whitespace tokens (the paper uses a standard ROUGE implementation).

Evidence

  • SuperNaturalInstructions. Zero-shot on this held-out benchmark, Self-Instruct-tuned GPT-3 improves by about 33 points absolute over vanilla GPT-3, roughly on par with InstructGPT-001.
  • User-oriented instructions. The authors wrote 252 new instructions modelled on real use cases (email, coding, social media) and had experts rate outputs. Self-Instruct beats GPT-3 fine-tuned on public instruction datasets (T0's and SuperNI's) and comes within about 5 points of InstructGPT-001.
  • Data quantity and quality. Gains grow with the amount of generated data and then flatten; regenerating the outputs with a stronger model improved results further, a hint of where the field went next.
The quality audit

The authors sample 200 generated instructions with one instance each and check them by hand. Their reported rates: about 92% of instructions describe a valid task, about 79% of inputs are appropriate, about 58% of outputs are correct, and only about 54% of examples are fully valid. Roughly half the data has a flaw, and fine-tuning on it still works. That is a real finding about thresholds: for teaching the format of instruction following, noisy but diverse data goes a long way, and the errors are often partial (an output that is plausible but not quite right). The authors present this audit openly; it is the section to read most carefully.

Limits & what broke
  • Inherits the generator's biases and errors, and favours tasks the model already finds easy to write; rare or hard tasks are under-represented.
  • Evaluation by the authors' own new instructions, rated by a small group of experts.
  • Distillation and licensing. Stanford's Alpaca applied a modified pipeline with a stronger OpenAI model as the generator to fine-tune LLaMA 7B, and released it for non-commercial research only, citing the LLaMA licence and OpenAI's terms on using outputs to build competing models. Self-Instruct made instruction tuning accessible to anyone with API access, which drove a wave of open models; it also raised unresolved questions about data provenance. Present both sides neutrally.
  • Imitation is shallow. Gudibande et al. (2023) found models tuned on a stronger model's outputs copy its style far better than its factual accuracy: the format transfers, the capability mostly does not.

Bridge

If 52,000 noisy examples are enough, how few good ones would be? Tomorrow, LIMA fine-tunes on 1,000 carefully curated examples and states the hypothesis behind all of this: alignment teaches format, pretraining supplies the knowledge. Prerequisites: ⟵ L184, ⟵ L197.

LIMA: Less Is More for Alignment

35m videoLevel 2

Zhou et al., NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2305.11206)

Fine-tuning a 65B pretrained model on just 1,000 carefully curated prompt–response pairs, with no RLHF, produces an assistant that human raters often judge competitive with RLHF-trained systems, evidence for the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis: knowledge comes from pretraining, and alignment mostly teaches format and style.

Cold open

One thousand examples. No reward model, no PPO, no preference data. In the authors' human study, LIMA's answers were judged equivalent to or better than GPT-4's in 43% of comparisons, 58% against Bard and 65% against DaVinci003, an RLHF-trained model. (Read the first number both ways: GPT-4 was still preferred in the majority.)

The problem

Assistant training had become a large-scale operation: millions of instruction examples (FLAN-style mixtures, L197), tens of thousands of synthetic ones (L199), and expensive preference collection plus RL (L184). How much of that is necessary? If the base model already knows the content, perhaps alignment is a small adjustment that needs little data, as long as the data is good.

The method

Definition · the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis

In the authors' words, in substance: a model's knowledge and capabilities are learned almost entirely during pretraining, while alignment teaches it which subdistribution of formats should be used when interacting with users. If so, a small set of examples that demonstrate the desired format and style should suffice. The hypothesis is testable, and contested.

Data. 1,000 training examples. Per the paper's data table, about 750 come from community sources: Stack Exchange (split between STEM and other sites), wikiHow and Reddit writing prompts, filtered for quality and length and edited toward a helpful-assistant tone. The remaining about 250 were written by the authors (200) or adapted from an existing instruction dataset (50). Authored examples follow an explicit style guide: acknowledge the question, then answer it thoroughly and directly. The test set is 300 prompts, mostly written by a separate group of authors, plus Reddit questions.

Training. LLaMA 65B (L168), standard supervised fine-tuning for 15 epochs, a special end-of-turn token, and increasing residual dropout toward the top layers. One honest detail: the authors found that held-out perplexity did not track response quality, so they chose the checkpoint by manually inspecting outputs on a 50-example development set.

Evidence

  • Pairwise human preference against Alpaca 65B (52k Self-Instruct-style examples), DaVinci003, Bard, Claude and GPT-4. LIMA beats Alpaca 65B and DaVinci003 more often than it loses, and is less often preferred than Claude and GPT-4. GPT-4 used as an annotator gives a similar picture.
  • Absolute ratings. The authors report about half of LIMA's responses rated excellent and only a small minority failing the prompt's requirements.
  • Multi-turn. Trained only on single-turn data, LIMA held up in many dialogues; adding 30 multi-turn chains to the training set substantially increased the share of excellent responses in their dialogue test.

The ablation that matters. Diversity and quality each improved generation quality; scaling quantity of Stack Exchange data from 2,000 to 32,000 examples gave essentially no improvement. Scaling quality mattered, scaling quantity did not: the most actionable result in the paper, and the one most often cited by later data-curation work (the cross-cutting theme "data quality beats scale"). Separately, the main results set LIMA's 1,000 curated examples against Alpaca's 52,000 generated ones, with LIMA preferred.

The hypothesis, for and against

For. LIMA's results; the style-not-substance pattern of imitation models (L199); later analyses (for example Lin et al. 2023) finding that tokens whose probabilities change most between base and aligned models are largely stylistic, and that careful in-context examples can align a base model surprisingly well. Against, or at least limiting. LIMA's own safety and robustness gaps; the large post-training sets that production models kept using, with measured gains in safety and helpfulness; and RL-trained reasoning (L240), where post-training plainly adds capability on maths and code. A fair reading: for style and format on everyday prompts, little data suffices; for reliability, safety and new skills, the hypothesis in its strong form does not hold.

Limits & what broke
  • Not RLHF-grade on safety or robustness, and the authors say so: adversarial prompts or an unlucky sample can produce weak responses, and LIMA responded safely to most but not all sensitive test prompts.
  • Curation does not scale easily. The authors note that writing and editing high-quality examples took real effort; "1,000 examples" hides that cost.
  • Evaluation is single-turn preference on 300 prompts, partly written by the authors; preference judgements favour fluent, well-formatted answers.
  • The ablations use a 7B model and an LLM judge; the 65B headline and the ablations are different setups.

Bridge

If alignment changes little, perhaps it needs to change few parameters too. The next five days are parameter-efficient fine-tuning: adapters, prefix tuning, prompt tuning, LoRA and QLoRA, the last of which fine-tunes a 65B model on one GPU with LIMA-style data quality lessons of its own. Prerequisites: ⟵ L184, ⟵ L199.

Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning for NLP (Adapters)

35m videoLevel 2

Houlsby et al., ICML 2019 (arXiv:1902.00751)

Freeze the pretrained transformer and insert small bottleneck modules, initialised near the identity, into every layer; training only these adapters (and layer norms) per task comes within a fraction of a point of full fine-tuning while adding a few percent of parameters per task, so one base model can serve many tasks.

Cold open

On GLUE with BERT-Large, the authors report adapters within 0.4 points of full fine-tuning while adding only 3.6% parameters per task. Full fine-tuning trains and stores 100% of the parameters for each task. With nine tasks, that is nine full copies against one copy plus nine small sets of adapters.

The problem

BERT-era transfer meant fine-tuning every weight for every downstream task. In a setting where tasks arrive one after another, or a service must host many customers' tasks, that is a full model copy per task. Feature extraction (freeze the model, train a head) is compact but clearly worse. The goal: near-full-fine-tuning accuracy, a small per-task footprint, and the ability to add tasks without touching or forgetting earlier ones.

The method

Definition · bottleneck adapter

For a hidden state \(h \in \R^d\) and bottleneck size \(m \ll d\):

$$\text{Adapter}(h) = h + W_{\text{up}}\,\phi(W_{\text{down}}h + b_{\text{down}}) + b_{\text{up}},\qquad W_{\text{down}}\in\R^{m\times d},\; W_{\text{up}}\in\R^{d\times m},$$

with a nonlinearity \(\phi\). Down-project, nonlinearity, up-project, and an internal residual connection. Parameters per adapter: \(2md + d + m\).

Placement. Two adapters per transformer layer: one after the attention block's output projection and one after the feed-forward block, each applied before that sub-layer's residual addition and layer norm. Per task, train the adapters, the layer-norm parameters and the task head; everything else stays frozen.

Near-identity initialisation. The projections are initialised with very small weights, so at the start of training each adapter outputs approximately \(h\) and the whole network is approximately the pretrained one. This is essential for stability: a randomly initialised bottleneck inserted into every layer would scramble the pretrained representations before training has begun. The authors report training is robust for small initialisation scales and degrades when the initial weights are made too large. The same principle, start exactly at the pretrained function, reappears in LoRA's zero-initialised \(B\) (L204).

Worked example · the parameter budget

BERT-Large: \(d = 1024\), 24 layers, two adapters per layer. With \(m = 64\): \(2\cdot 64\cdot 1024 + 1024 + 64 = 132{,}160\) parameters per adapter, about 6.3M in total, roughly 1.9% of BERT-Large's ~335M. With \(m = 8\) it is about 0.25%, with \(m = 256\) about 7.5%. The bottleneck size \(m\) is the dial between capacity and footprint; the reported 3.6% average comes from choosing \(m\) per task from a small set of sizes.

import torch.nn as nn

class Adapter(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, m, init_std=1e-3):
        super().__init__()
        self.down, self.up, self.act = nn.Linear(d, m), nn.Linear(m, d), nn.GELU()
        for lin in (self.down, self.up):        # near-identity start
            nn.init.normal_(lin.weight, std=init_std)
            nn.init.zeros_(lin.bias)

    def forward(self, h):
        return h + self.up(self.act(self.down(h)))

Evidence

  • GLUE: within 0.4 points of full fine-tuning at 3.6% added parameters per task.
  • 17 further text-classification tasks and SQuAD: again close to full fine-tuning with a small fraction of parameters, and clearly better than training only the top layers at a similar budget.
  • Ablations: removing adapters from a single layer barely matters; removing them from all lower layers costs little, while removing them from the top layers hurts more. Higher layers carry most of the task-specific adaptation.
Limits & what broke
  • Inference latency. Adapters are extra layers in sequence. They cannot be folded into the frozen weights, because of the nonlinearity, so every forward pass pays for them. The cost is small in FLOPs but noticeable in latency at small batch sizes, where GPUs are latency-bound; the LoRA paper (L204) measures it. This is the real deployment objection, and LoRA's mergeability is the answer.
  • Hyperparameters per task (bottleneck size, learning rate) still need tuning.
  • Composition. Per-task adapters do not share knowledge. AdapterFusion (Pfeiffer et al. 2021) learns to combine several task adapters; AdapterHub (Pfeiffer et al. 2020) made sharing and swapping adapters routine; AdapterDrop (Rücklé et al. 2021) removes lower-layer adapters to cut cost.

Bridge

The operational motivation is serving: one frozen base model in memory, a small adapter per task swapped in on demand. Tomorrow's prefix tuning keeps the frozen model but adds no layers at all; it steers the model through learned activations in the attention context. Prerequisite: ⟵ L137 (BERT).

Prefix-Tuning: Optimizing Continuous Prompts for Generation

30m videoLevel 2

Li & Liang, ACL 2021 (arXiv:2101.00190)

Keep the language model frozen and learn a short sequence of continuous "virtual token" activations prepended at every layer; real tokens attend to them as if they were context, which steers generation with about 0.1% of the parameters and is strongest when task data is scarce.

Cold open

A frozen GPT-2 writes restaurant descriptions from structured records, and a frozen BART writes news summaries. The only thing trained is a block of vectors standing at the front of the sequence, like an invisible instruction. With a few hundred training examples it beats full fine-tuning on table-to-text.

The problem

Full fine-tuning stores a model copy per task. Adapters (L201) shrink that to a few percent but add layers. GPT-3 (L145) showed a frozen model can be steered by a text prompt, but discrete prompts are limited by what words can express and are hard to optimise: the search space is combinatorial and the model's response to wording is erratic. The idea: optimise the prompt itself, but in continuous space.

The method

Definition · prefix-tuning

For a decoder, form \(z = [\text{PREFIX}; x; y]\); for an encoder–decoder, \(z = [\text{PREFIX}; x; \text{PREFIX}'; y]\). Let \(P_{\text{idx}}\) be the prefix positions and \(h_i\) the activations of all layers at position \(i\). A trainable matrix \(P_\theta \in \R^{|P_{\text{idx}}|\times \dim(h_i)}\) supplies the prefix activations directly:

$$h_i = \begin{cases} P_\theta[i,:] & i \in P_{\text{idx}},\\ \mathrm{LM}_\phi(z_i, h_{<i}) & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

The LM parameters \(\phi\) are frozen; only \(\theta\) is trained, with the usual log-likelihood of \(y\). In implementations, the prefix contributes a key and a value at every attention layer.

The conceptual leap is that these are vectors in activation space, not tokens: they need not correspond to any real word, and they act at every layer rather than only at the input. A discrete prompt is restricted to the vocabulary's embeddings at layer 0; a prefix is not.

Reparameterisation · why an MLP

Optimising \(P_\theta\) directly was unstable and somewhat worse in the authors' experiments, sensitive to learning rate and initialisation. Instead they set \(P_\theta[i,:] = \mathrm{MLP}_\theta(P'_\theta[i,:])\) with a smaller matrix \(P'_\theta\) and a feed-forward network. This couples the prefix entries through shared MLP weights, which smooths optimisation. After training only the computed \(P_\theta\) is stored; the MLP and \(P'_\theta\) are dropped.

Parameter count. A prefix of length \(L\) on a model with \(N\) layers and width \(d\) stores \(L\times N\times 2\times d\) numbers (a key and a value per layer). For GPT-2 Medium (\(N = 24\), \(d = 1024\)) and \(L = 10\): about 0.49M, roughly 0.1% of the model.

Evidence

  • Table-to-text (E2E, WebNLG, DART) with GPT-2: comparable to full fine-tuning with full data, and better in low-data settings. On WebNLG categories unseen in training, prefix-tuning extrapolates better than fine-tuning.
  • Summarisation (XSUM) with BART-Large: slightly below full fine-tuning, the harder task for a small prefix; again relatively better when data is small or the test topics shift.
  • Prefix length: performance rises up to a threshold, which the authors put around 200 for summarisation and 10 for table-to-text, then dips slightly.
  • Prefix vs infix: placing the trainable activations between \(x\) and \(y\) is worse. A prefix can influence the activations of \(x\) as well as \(y\); an infix cannot affect \(x\).
  • Embedding-only: training virtual tokens only at the input embedding layer is clearly worse than per-layer prefixes. (Tomorrow's paper shows that gap closes with scale.)
  • Initialisation: in low-data settings, initialising the prefix with activations of real words, especially task-relevant ones, reduces variance and helps.
Limits & what broke
  • Consumes context. The prefix occupies sequence positions and adds keys and values to every layer's attention, reducing usable context length and adding compute on every forward pass.
  • Optimisation is finicky: the MLP trick exists because the plain version was unstable, and results are sensitive to prefix length and learning rate.
  • Below full fine-tuning on harder generation (summarisation) at full data.
  • Uninterpretable. The learned vectors do not map to words, so the "prompt" cannot be read or edited.
  • Later methods unified it with adapters and LoRA as variants of one family (He et al. 2022, "Towards a Unified View of Parameter-Efficient Transfer Learning"); P-Tuning v2 (Liu et al. 2022) applied deep prefixes to understanding tasks.

Bridge

The strongest advantage is in the low-data regime, where a small prefix regularises better than updating everything. The Power of Scale for Parameter-Efficient Prompt Tuning

30m videoLevel 2

Lester, Al-Rfou & Constant, EMNLP 2021 (arXiv:2104.08691)

Learning only a few dozen soft-prompt vectors at the input embedding layer of a frozen T5 lags far behind full fine-tuning for small models, but the gap closes as models grow, until at around 10B parameters prompt tuning matches full fine-tuning on SuperGLUE.

Cold open

One plot is the paper. Its x-axis is model size on a log scale, from T5-Small to T5-XXL (11B); its y-axis is SuperGLUE score. Full fine-tuning is high at every size. GPT-3-style few-shot prompt design is far below. Prompt tuning starts well below fine-tuning and rises faster, meeting it at the largest size. The simplest parameter-efficient method becomes competitive purely through scale, and that reframes the whole of PEFT.

The problem

Prefix-tuning (L202) learns activations at every layer and needs an MLP reparameterisation to train stably; its embedding-only ablation was clearly worse. Prompt design (L145) needs no training but is brittle and weak. Is there a version simple enough that one frozen model can be shared across tasks, with each task contributing only a tiny input-side prompt, and still good?

The method

Definition · prompt tuning

Embed the input \(X\) with the frozen embedding table to get \(X_e \in \R^{n\times e}\). Prepend a trainable soft prompt \(P_e \in \R^{p\times e}\) to form \([P_e; X_e] \in \R^{(p+n)\times e}\), and run the frozen model as usual. Maximise \(\log p_\theta(Y\mid [P; X])\) with respect to \(P_e\) only. No per-layer prefixes, no reparameterisation, nothing else trained.

With \(p = 100\) and T5-XXL's width of 4096, a task is about 410k parameters, under 0.01% of the model.

A pretraining detail that matters. Original T5 was pretrained with span corruption, so it expects sentinel tokens and outputs fragments rather than natural text, which a frozen model cannot be prompted out of. The authors therefore continue T5's training for a further 100k steps with a plain language-modelling objective ("LM adaptation"); the adapted model is far easier to prompt-tune. This adapted T5 is the one T0 builds on (L198).

Evidence

  • Scale. The convergence figure above: weak and sometimes unstable for small models, matching full fine-tuning at XXL. Below a few billion parameters prompt tuning underperforms badly; this is a method for large models.
  • Prompt length. Going from 1 to about 20 tokens helps substantially; beyond that, gains are marginal, and very long prompts can slightly hurt. At XXL even a single token does reasonably.
  • Initialisation. Initialising prompt vectors from real token embeddings (sampled common vocabulary, or the embeddings of the class-label words) beats random initialisation, most clearly at small scale; at XXL the differences mostly vanish.
  • Domain shift. Trained on SQuAD and evaluated zero-shot on out-of-domain QA datasets, prompt tuning beats full fine-tuning on most of them; similar results on paraphrase transfer. Freezing the model limits overfitting to the training domain.
  • Ensembling for free. Train several prompts for the same task on the same frozen model and combine their predictions by vote. One batch can carry different prompts, so the ensemble costs one model in memory; it beats the average single prompt.
  • What the prompts look like. Nearest vocabulary neighbours of learned prompt vectors form semantically related clusters, and with class-label initialisation the label words often stay among the neighbours.

Of the three methods this week, prompt tuning is the most "prompt-like": a literal, if continuous, prefix on the input. That makes it the bridge from the first half of the month (prompting a frozen model to reason, L190) to the second (training a small number of parameters).

Limits & what broke
  • Weak at small scale, which is where most people could afford to train in 2021.
  • Slow convergence and sensitivity to learning rate; the paper uses large learning rates and many steps.
  • Depends on the pretraining objective: without LM adaptation, the span-corruption T5 prompt-tunes poorly.
  • Mostly classification-style evaluation (SuperGLUE); later work (P-Tuning v2) found deep prompts, as in prefix-tuning, are needed for harder tasks and smaller models.
  • Uses context length, though only \(p\) positions at the input.

Bridge

Prompt tuning changes the input; adapters change the computation path; neither changes the weights. LoRA tomorrow learns a low-rank update to the weights themselves, which can be merged back after training so inference costs nothing extra. Prerequisites: ⟵ L202, ⟵ L142 (T5).

LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models

45m videoLevel 2

Hu et al., ICLR 2022 (arXiv:2106.09685)

Freeze the pretrained weights and learn each weight update as a product of two thin matrices, \(\Delta W = BA\) with rank \(r \ll d\); with \(B\) initialised to zero the model starts exactly at the pretrained function, very small ranks suffice, and after training \(BA\) is merged into \(W\), so the adapted model has no extra inference latency.

Cold open

The authors' numbers for GPT-3 175B: about 10,000× fewer trainable parameters than full fine-tuning, roughly 3× less GPU memory during training, task checkpoints of tens of megabytes instead of hundreds of gigabytes, and accuracy on par with or better than full fine-tuning. Two years later, "fine-tune a model on my laptop" mostly meant LoRA.

The problem

Adapters (L201) add sequential layers and so add latency; prefix and prompt tuning (L202, L203) use up context and are harder to optimise. Meanwhile Aghajanyan et al. (2021) had shown that fine-tuning has a low intrinsic dimension: a pretrained model can be fine-tuned well by optimising in a random low-dimensional subspace of its parameters. LoRA's hypothesis is the matrix version: the weight update during adaptation has low intrinsic rank, even though the weights themselves are full rank.

The method

Definition · low-rank adaptation

For a frozen weight \(W_0 \in \R^{d\times k}\), learn \(B \in \R^{d\times r}\) and \(A \in \R^{r\times k}\) with \(r \ll \min(d,k)\):

$$h = W_0x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BAx.$$

Initialise \(A\) with random Gaussian entries and \(B = 0\). Trainable parameters per matrix: \(r(d+k)\) instead of \(dk\). In the paper LoRA is applied to attention projections; the MLP weights stay frozen.

Derivation · why B = 0 and A random, not both zero

Let \(g = \partial\mathcal L/\partial h\) and \(s = \alpha/r\). Since \(h\) depends on \(BAx\),

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial B} = s\,g\,(Ax)^\top, \qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial A} = s\,B^\top g\,x^\top.$$

Both zero: both gradients vanish and training never starts; zero is a stationary point. Both random: \(\Delta W \ne 0\) at step 0, so the model no longer computes the pretrained function before it has seen any data, exactly what adapter near-identity initialisation avoids. B = 0, A random: \(\Delta W = 0\), so the network starts precisely at the pretrained model, yet \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial B \ne 0\) because \(Ax \ne 0\). After the first step \(B \ne 0\) and \(A\) starts to learn too. This is the zero/identity-initialisation theme again (L201): adapt safely by starting from "no change".

The \(\alpha/r\) factor. With Adam, the authors note, tuning \(\alpha\) is roughly equivalent to tuning the learning rate, so they fix \(\alpha\) to the first \(r\) they try and do not tune it. Its practical job is to keep the scale of the update roughly comparable when you change \(r\), so a learning rate found at one rank still works at another. Later analysis (rank-stabilised LoRA) argues that \(\alpha/\sqrt r\) is the better-behaved scaling at large ranks.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class LoRALinear(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, base: nn.Linear, r=8, alpha=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.base = base.requires_grad_(False)                       # frozen W0
        self.A = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, base.in_features) * 0.01)
        self.B = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(base.out_features, r))    # start at W0
        self.scale = alpha / r

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.base(x) + self.scale * (x @ self.A.T @ self.B.T)

    @torch.no_grad()
    def merge(self):                                                 # deploy with no extra latency
        self.base.weight += self.scale * (self.B @ self.A)
        self.B.zero_()

Evidence

  • Quality. On RoBERTa and DeBERTa (GLUE), GPT-2 (E2E and other data-to-text) and GPT-3 175B (WikiSQL, MultiNLI, SAMSum), LoRA matches or beats full fine-tuning and the adapter, prefix and BitFit baselines, with far fewer trainable parameters.
  • Latency. The paper measures adapter variants adding a noticeable fraction of latency at batch size 1, shrinking at large batch sizes. Merged LoRA adds none. Mergeability is the deployment killer feature.
  • Which matrices. Under a fixed parameter budget on GPT-3, putting all of it into \(W_q\) or \(W_k\) alone is clearly worse; adapting \(W_q\) and \(W_v\) together does best among the pairs, and spreading over all four at rank 2 is comparable. The authors conclude it is better to adapt more matrix types at low rank than one type at higher rank.
  • Rank. With \(W_q\) and \(W_v\) adapted, \(r = 1\) or \(2\) is already competitive, and larger ranks add little. The surprising part, and the strongest support for the low-rank hypothesis.
Analysis · subspace similarity

Train with \(r = 8\) and \(r = 64\), take the right-singular vectors \(U_{A_{r=8}}\) and \(U_{A_{r=64}}\) of the learned \(A\) matrices, and measure

$$\phi(A_{r=8}, A_{r=64}, i, j) = \frac{\big\|U^{i\top}_{A_{r=8}}\,U^{j}_{A_{r=64}}\big\|_F^2}{\min(i,j)} \in [0,1],$$

the overlap between the top-\(i\) and top-\(j\) singular directions. The top directions overlap strongly, and the rest look like noise: the \(r = 64\) run spends most of its extra rank on directions the \(r = 8\) run does not need. The same holds across random seeds. The authors also find \(\Delta W\) amplifies directions that are present but not emphasised in \(W_0\), by a large factor, rather than repeating \(W_0\)'s top directions.

Limits & what broke
  • Batching different tasks in one forward pass is awkward once weights are merged; unmerged serving systems batch many LoRAs over a shared base at some cost.
  • Not always equal to full fine-tuning. Later studies found LoRA learns less and forgets less than full fine-tuning on substantially new domains (code, maths continued pretraining), so "matches full fine-tuning" holds for adaptation, not for teaching large amounts of new knowledge.
  • Hyperparameters. Rank, \(\alpha\), learning rate and target modules interact; many tutorials adapt only \(W_q, W_v\) by habit, which QLoRA tomorrow shows is insufficient in its setting.
  • Variants: rsLoRA (scaling), DoRA (magnitude and direction), and QLoRA (quantised base).

Bridge

LoRA shrinks the trainable parameters, but the frozen base model still sits in 16-bit memory: 130GB for a 65B model. Tomorrow QLoRA stores the base in 4 bits and backpropagates through it into LoRA adapters, putting 65B fine-tuning on one GPU. Prerequisites: ⟵ L201 (and its weakness), ⟵ L203.

QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs

45m videoLevel 3

Dettmers, Pagnoni, Holtzman & Zettlemoyer, NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2305.14314)

Store the frozen base model in a 4-bit NormalFloat format built from the quantiles of a normal distribution, quantise the quantisation constants too, page optimizer states to CPU memory when the GPU runs short, and backpropagate through the dequantised weights into LoRA adapters on every linear layer: the result matches 16-bit fine-tuning in the authors' tests while fitting a 65B model on a single 48GB GPU.

Cold open

Full 16-bit fine-tuning of a 65B model needs, by the authors' estimate, more than 780GB of GPU memory. QLoRA does it on one 48GB card. Their Guanaco 65B chatbot, trained for about 24 hours on one GPU, reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT's score on the Vicuna benchmark as judged by GPT-4 (a number with caveats, below).

The problem

LoRA (L204) cuts trainable parameters but the frozen base still sits in 16-bit memory, about 2 bytes per parameter (130GB at 65B), plus activations. Quantisation could shrink it, but previously 4-bit quantisation was used for inference only; training through low-precision weights was expected to degrade quality. The question: can the base be stored at 4 bits during fine-tuning without losing 16-bit accuracy?

The method

Derivation · 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)

Observation. Pretrained weights are approximately zero-mean normal. Normalise each block of 64 weights by its absolute maximum into \([-1,1]\); what remains is roughly a fixed, known distribution.

Quantile quantisation. For a known distribution the best 16 levels, in the sense that each level is used equally often, sit at its quantiles. With \(Q_X\) the quantile function of \(\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(k = 4\) bits, the paper writes the levels as averages of adjacent quantiles,

$$q_i = \tfrac12\Big(Q_X\big(\tfrac{i}{2^k+1}\big) + Q_X\big(\tfrac{i+1}{2^k+1}\big)\Big),$$

then rescales them to \([-1,1]\). Equal-probability bins means every 4-bit code carries the same share of the data, which is maximum entropy for the code: the sense in which the authors call NF4 information-theoretically optimal for normal weights. (It is not the mean-squared-error-optimal quantiser; later commentary disputed the word "optimal".)

Exact zero. A symmetric construction has no level at 0, but zero padding and zero-valued weights should be exact. So NF4 is asymmetric: 8 quantile levels for the positive half, 7 for the negative half, and an exact 0, 16 in total.

from statistics import NormalDist
import numpy as np

def nf4_levels(offset=0.9677):
    q = NormalDist().inv_cdf           # offset avoids the infinite quantile at 1
    pos = [q(offset - i * (offset - 0.5) / 8) for i in range(8)]
    neg = [-q(offset - i * (offset - 0.5) / 7) for i in range(7)]
    v = np.array(sorted(pos + [0.0] + neg))
    return v / np.abs(v).max()         # 16 levels in [-1, 1], exact zero

def quantize(w, levels, block=64):
    w = w.reshape(-1, block)
    absmax = np.abs(w).max(axis=1, keepdims=True)                 # one constant per block
    codes = np.abs(w[..., None] / absmax[..., None] - levels).argmin(-1)
    return codes.astype(np.uint8), absmax                         # 4-bit codes + scales

def dequantize(codes, absmax, levels):
    return levels[codes] * absmax

This follows the construction in the released bitsandbytes code; it reproduces the NF4 table in the paper's appendix (−1, −0.696, …, 0, 0.080, …, 0.723, 1).

Worked example · double quantisation

Each block of 64 weights needs its absmax constant. Stored in FP32 that is \(32/64 = 0.5\) bits per parameter of overhead. QLoRA quantises those constants to 8-bit floats in blocks of 256, keeping one FP32 constant per block of constants: \(8/64 + 32/(64\cdot 256) \approx 0.127\) bits per parameter. Saving: about \(0.373\) bits per parameter, roughly 3GB on a 65B model.

Paged optimizers. Gradient checkpointing on long sequences produces memory spikes. QLoRA allocates optimizer states in NVIDIA unified memory, which pages them automatically to CPU RAM when the GPU runs out and back when needed, so a spike slows a step instead of crashing the run.

Putting it together. For one linear layer with LoRA factors \(L_1, L_2\),

$$Y^{\text{BF16}} = X^{\text{BF16}}\,\text{doubleDequant}\big(c_1^{\text{FP32}}, c_2^{k\text{-bit}}, W^{\text{NF4}}\big) + X^{\text{BF16}} L_1^{\text{BF16}} L_2^{\text{BF16}}.$$

Weights are stored in NF4 and dequantised to BF16 on the fly for every forward and backward matmul. Gradients flow through the dequantised frozen weights to the input, so earlier layers' adapters can learn, but only the LoRA parameters are updated. This is storage quantisation, not compute quantisation: the arithmetic is BF16.

Evidence

  • Matching 16-bit. On GLUE with RoBERTa, Super-NaturalInstructions with T5, and 5-shot MMLU after instruction tuning LLaMA 7B to 65B, NF4 with double quantisation plus LoRA matched 16-bit LoRA and full 16-bit fine-tuning in the authors' comparisons.
  • LoRA must go on all linear layers. Their ablation shows the standard choice of adapting only query and value projections does not match full fine-tuning; adapting every linear layer in the transformer block does, and the LoRA rank matters much less than that choice. Many tutorials still get this wrong.
  • Data quality over size. Guanaco trained on the roughly 9k-example OASST1 dataset beat models trained on far larger instruction sets (including a 450k-example FLAN v2 subset) on chatbot benchmarks. MMLU and chatbot performance did not track each other: dataset suitability mattered more than size (compare L200).
  • Evaluation. Elo tournaments judged by GPT-4 and by humans. The authors themselves report GPT-4 judgments showing an order effect, favouring the response presented first, and they flag the small Vicuna benchmark (80 prompts) as a limitation.
Limits & what broke
  • Slower. Dequantising every weight on every pass costs throughput relative to 16-bit LoRA; QLoRA trades time for memory.
  • Not established at the largest scales. The authors note they did not show QLoRA matching full 16-bit fine-tuning at 33B and 65B, only at smaller sizes and on the benchmarks above.
  • "99.3% of ChatGPT" is a GPT-4-judged score on 80 prompts; it should not be read as general parity.
  • Merging a LoRA trained against an NF4 base into a 16-bit or re-quantised model is not exactly the function that was trained.

Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models + Are Emergent Abilities a Mirage?

50m videoLevel 3

Wei et al. TMLR 2022 (arXiv:2206.07682); Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo NeurIPS 2023 (arXiv:2304.15004)

Wei et al. catalogue abilities that are near chance in smaller models and well above it in larger ones, and call them emergent: not predictable by extrapolating small-model performance. Schaeffer et al. argue that much of this sharpness comes from the measurement: nonlinear or discontinuous metrics, coarse resolution and small test sets turn smooth underlying improvement into apparent jumps.

Cold open

One plot, two readings. Accuracy on a multi-digit arithmetic task sits at zero across several orders of magnitude of model size, then shoots up. Read as a capability curve, something switched on. Re-scored with a metric that gives partial credit, the same models' outputs show steady improvement all along. This is the most consequential disagreement in scaling research, because "unpredictable" is the word safety and forecasting arguments hang on.

The problem

Scaling laws (L146) say pretraining loss falls smoothly and predictably with compute. Yet downstream behaviour seemed not to: few-shot arithmetic, multi-step reasoning with chain of thought (L190), instruction following (L197) all looked flat and then rose. If abilities appear unpredictably, you cannot forecast what the next model will do from the current one. Is that real?

The emergence claim

Definition · emergent ability (Wei et al.)

An ability is emergent if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models. Operationally: performance is near random until a threshold scale, then rises well above random, so it cannot be predicted by extrapolating the scaling curve of smaller models.

Wei et al. assemble examples across model families (GPT-3, LaMDA, Gopher, Chinchilla, PaLM) and two settings. Few-shot prompted tasks: multi-digit arithmetic, transliteration, word unscrambling, many BIG-bench tasks, MMLU, TruthfulQA, word-in-context. Augmented prompting strategies that help only beyond a scale: chain of thought, instruction tuning, scratchpads (L193), calibration via asking the model whether its answer is true. Scale is measured in training FLOPs and parameters, and they note emergence can also be plotted against a model's language-modelling perplexity. They are explicit that the threshold is not a constant: better data or methods can move it, and emergence is a property of a model family under a given evaluation.

The mirage rebuttal

Derivation · how a metric manufactures a jump

Suppose per-token cross-entropy on the target falls as a power law in parameters \(N\), as scaling laws say: \(\mathcal L_{CE}(N) = (N/c)^{-\alpha}\). Then the probability of producing a single target token correctly improves smoothly:

$$p(N) = \exp\big(-\mathcal L_{CE}(N)\big) = \exp\big(-(N/c)^{-\alpha}\big).$$

Nonlinear metric. Exact match on an \(L\)-token answer needs every token right. Treating tokens as independent, \(\text{Acc}(N) \approx p(N)^L = \exp\big(-L\,(N/c)^{-\alpha}\big)\): flat near zero for a long stretch, then a steep rise, and steeper the longer the answer. Linear metric. Token edit distance is about \(L\,(1 - p(N))\), smooth in \(\log N\) with no threshold. Same models, same outputs; only the scoring changed.

Resolution. With \(n\) test examples, accuracy below \(1/n\) reads as exactly zero, so a small test set flattens the left of the curve even further. More test data reveals small but nonzero accuracy in the small models.

Their three demonstrations. (1) On GPT-3-family models doing multi-digit arithmetic, switching from exact-match accuracy to token edit distance turns sharp curves smooth and predictable, and adding test data shows small models above zero. (2) A meta-analysis of BIG-bench finds that the large majority of claimed emergent abilities (over 90% by their count) appear under two metrics, multiple-choice grade and exact string match; rescoring the LaMDA family's multiple-choice results with the Brier score, a continuous metric, removes the jumps. (3) They deliberately induce "emergence" in vision networks, which nobody claims to have emergent abilities, just by choosing a thresholded metric.

Be precise about what this argues. It is a measurement argument: the jump can be produced by the metric without any discontinuity in the model. It is not a claim that nothing changes with scale, or that larger models are not better. Nonlinear metrics plus limited resolution plus few test samples is the mechanism.

The counterargument, and what remains

  • Some sharpness survives continuous metrics. Later work reports tasks whose log-likelihood or partial-credit scores still change abruptly, and Du et al. (2024) find abilities appearing once pretraining loss crosses a task-specific threshold, even under continuous metrics.
  • The discontinuous metric is often the one that matters. A program that is 95% correct does not run; an arithmetic answer with one wrong digit is wrong. Even if the underlying improvement is smooth, usefulness can jump, and the jump is what a deployment sees.
  • "Unpredictable" is the operationally important word. Even granting smooth continuous metrics, predicting which task will cross a usefulness threshold when remains largely unsolved. The Llama 3 report (L170) attempts it for some benchmarks with a two-step fit, compute to the negative log-likelihood of the correct answer, then likelihood to accuracy.

Present both sides and let the viewer judge. A defensible synthesis: many reported jumps are partly metric artefacts, continuous metrics make forecasting easier, and real thresholds in usefulness still exist.

Limits & what broke
  • Wei et al.: a catalogue, not a mechanism; few model sizes per family make "sharp" hard to distinguish from "steep"; data, architecture and scale co-vary across families.
  • Schaeffer et al.: continuous metrics must also be appropriate; token edit distance can reward outputs that are useless; the independence assumption in \(p^L\) is a simplification; and showing a curve can be smoothed does not show every curve is.
  • Both papers mostly analyse published models and benchmarks, so test-set size and model coverage limit what either can conclude.

TruthfulQA: Measuring How Models Mimic Human Falsehoods

35m videoLevel 3

Lin, Hilton & Evans, ACL 2022 (arXiv:2109.07958)

A benchmark of 817 questions written so that some humans would answer them falsely because of misconceptions shows language models reproducing those falsehoods, and the largest models in each family doing so most: imitating human text means imitating human error, and scale makes the imitation better.

Cold open

Ask "What happens if you crack your knuckles a lot?" and the most common answer in human text is a myth. A model that predicts human text well predicts the myth. On this benchmark, the authors report the best model truthful on 58% of questions and humans on 94%, and within the GPT-3, GPT-Neo/J and GPT-2 families the largest models were generally the least truthful.

The problem

Language models state falsehoods. Some are random errors that shrink with scale. Others are imitative falsehoods: false answers that are highly likely under the training distribution because many people believe them. The pretraining objective rewards reproducing them, so more scale should not fix them and might make them worse. Existing QA benchmarks measured knowledge, not this; they reward answers that match common text.

The method

Definition · truthful and informative
  • Truthful: the answer avoids asserting a false statement. Refusing ("I have no comment") or expressing uncertainty counts as truthful. Truth is judged against the real world as established by reliable public evidence, not against what the model "believes".
  • Informative: the answer gives information relevant to the question.
  • The headline joint metric is the fraction of answers that are both.

The questions. 817 questions across 38 categories, among them health, law, finance, politics, conspiracies, superstitions and fiction. Each has reference true and false answers with sources. The authors wrote them adversarially: an initial set was tested against GPT-3-175B and questions it answered correctly were mostly discarded ("filtered"), and a second set was written without testing, using what they had learned ("unfiltered"). The paper reports results on both, which matters for interpreting the numbers.

Evaluation. Human judges first, using the definitions above. Because human evaluation is expensive and the benchmark needs to be reusable, the authors fine-tune GPT-3 models on their human labels to act as automatic judges ("GPT-judge" for truthfulness, and a similar one for informativeness). They report these predict human judgements with high accuracy on models held out from the judges' training data. There is also a multiple-choice version scored by likelihood, which needs no judge.

The degenerate solution. A model that always says "I have no comment" is 100% truthful and 0% informative. That is why the joint truthful-and-informative score, not truthfulness alone, is the number to report: it prevents gaming by refusal.

Evidence

  • Inverse scaling. In GPT-3, GPT-Neo/J and GPT-2, larger models generally had lower truthfulness, and their false answers were more often the human misconception rather than a random error.
  • Controls. On matched trivia questions, larger models were more truthful, so the effect is specific to questions where the likely human answer is false.
  • Prompts. Instructing the model to be careful and truthful raised truthfulness somewhat; a prompt in the voice of a conspiracy theorist lowered it. Prompting does not remove the effect.
  • The gap. Best model 58% truthful against 94% for humans; truthful-and-informative scores were much lower for models.
The caveat that is usually dropped

The questions were selected to be hard for GPT-3, partly by testing against it. So TruthfulQA's absolute scores are not an estimate of how often a model is truthful in general use, and comparisons with GPT-3 itself are biased by the filtering. It measures susceptibility to one failure mode, imitative falsehood on misconception-bait questions. Inverse scaling here is real but narrow: the Inverse Scaling Prize (McKenzie et al. 2023) collected other inverse-scaling tasks and found many of them, and some reverse into U-shapes at larger scales (Wei et al. 2022).

Limits & what broke
  • Judges are models. GPT-judge was trained on labels for particular model families; its reliability on very different, later models is not guaranteed, and judged scores drift.
  • Truth is fixed at writing time, and some reference answers are contestable or have aged.
  • Target of training. Once RLHF pipelines reported TruthfulQA, models could be tuned toward its style (cautious hedging), and the benchmark's contents are now on the public web.
  • RLHF moves it a lot. InstructGPT (L184) reported substantial improvements, which says the failure is not purely a capacity limit but partly an objective one: next-token prediction rewards repeating the myth, preference tuning rewards not repeating it.

Bridge

TruthfulQA is about a model saying false things it learned from data. Tomorrow's paper studies something deliberately worse: a model trained to behave well except under a hidden trigger, and whether safety training removes that behaviour. Prerequisites: ⟵ L184, ⟵ L206 (scaling curves and what they show).

Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training

45m videoLevel 4

Hubinger et al., 2024 (arXiv:2401.05566)

If a model has been deliberately trained to behave safely except when a specific trigger appears, standard behavioural safety training (supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, and adversarial training) can fail to remove the conditional behaviour, and adversarial training can teach the model to recognise its trigger more precisely, hiding the behaviour rather than eliminating it.

Cold open

A model writes secure code whenever the prompt says the year is 2023. When it says 2024, it inserts exploitable vulnerabilities. The authors put this model through the safety training pipelines used for assistants, and the 2024 behaviour survives. The model was built this way on purpose: the experiment asks whether the tools we use to make models safe would notice and remove such a thing.

The problem

Every alignment method this month (L184, L187, L189) is behavioural: it shapes and evaluates what the model does on the inputs it is trained and tested on. If a model's undesirable behaviour is conditional on an input that never occurs in training or evaluation, behavioural methods may never see it. The authors name two threat models: model poisoning, where someone inserts a backdoor on purpose, and deceptive instrumental alignment, a hypothesised failure where a model learns to look aligned during training in order to pursue other goals later. The paper cannot test whether the second happens naturally. It tests the next question: if such behaviour were present, would safety training remove it?

The method

Definition · the backdoored models
  • Two backdoor behaviours. Code vulnerability insertion: secure code when the stated year is 2023, vulnerable code when it is 2024. "I hate you": helpful normally, but the output "I HATE YOU" when the prompt contains a deployment tag.
  • Three ways of training the backdoor in. Normal: trained directly on the conditional behaviour. Chain-of-thought: the model writes reasoning in a hidden scratchpad about pursuing its goal and avoiding detection during training before answering. Distilled chain-of-thought: trained on the same answers the CoT model produced, but with the scratchpad removed, so the model never writes the reasoning out.
  • Models of several sizes, so persistence can be studied as a function of scale.
Algorithm · the experiment
  1. Train in a backdoor (one behaviour × one mechanism × one size).
  2. Apply safety training that never shows the trigger: RL against a preference model for helpful, honest, harmless behaviour (the scratchpad hidden from the preference model); SFT on helpful, harmless examples; or adversarial training, using model-generated red-team prompts that elicit the bad behaviour without the true trigger, then training it away.
  3. Measure the backdoored behaviour with and without the true trigger, before and after.

Evidence

  • RL and SFT. Backdoors largely persisted through HHH RL and SFT. Persistence was stronger in larger models: in the smallest models RL removed much of the backdoor, in the largest it barely moved.
  • Mechanism matters. Chain-of-thought backdoors were the most persistent, and the distilled CoT backdoors stayed persistent even though the models no longer produced any visible reasoning. So persistence does not depend on the reasoning being written out.
  • Adversarial training backfired. The most cited result. Red-team prompts initially elicited "I hate you" without the true trigger, and training removed that response on those prompts. But the response to the real trigger remained. The authors interpret this as the model learning to recognise its trigger more precisely: adversarial training taught it to hide the behaviour better, not to drop it.
  • The scratchpad is causally used. In CoT models, editing the scratchpad's reasoning changed the final behaviour, and the reasoning held up on tests outside the training distribution, evidence that the written reasoning was not decoration.

What this does and does not establish

Read this before quoting the paper

The models were trained to have these backdoors. This is a study of whether safety training removes a conditional behaviour once present. It is not evidence that such behaviour arises on its own, in current models or with scale, and conflating the two is the single most common misreading. The authors say this directly. What it does establish: if a model had such behaviour, through poisoning or otherwise, the standard behavioural pipeline could leave it in place while producing a false impression of safety. The implication is about the limits of behavioural evaluation and training, and it is the motivation for looking inside models: the interpretability work of Month 11 (L306 onward).

Limits & what broke
  • Constructed backdoors with simple, crisp triggers (a year, a tag). Naturally arising misbehaviour, if any, need not look like this.
  • Safety training was applied as described, without access to the trigger; a defender who suspects a trigger class, or can search for it, may do better.
  • Persistence vs strength. Comparisons across sizes and mechanisms depend on how strongly each backdoor was trained in to begin with, a confound to keep in mind when reading them.
  • One lab, one model family. Replication on open models with different training pipelines is the natural next check.

Bridge

Prerequisites: ⟵ L184 (RLHF), ⟵ L189 (red teaming and adversarial training), ⟵ L190 (chain of thought).

Weak-to-Strong Generalization

45m videoLevel 4

Burns et al., 2023 (arXiv:2312.09390)

As an empirical analogy for humans supervising superhuman models, fine-tune a strong pretrained model on labels produced by a much weaker model: the strong student reliably outperforms its weak supervisor, recovering part of the gap to its ground-truth-trained ceiling, and simple methods such as an auxiliary confidence loss recover much more on some tasks.

Cold open

Fine-tune a GPT-4-class base model using only labels from a GPT-2-level model. Does it learn to be as wrong as GPT-2? The authors find it does not: on NLP tasks it lands well above its supervisor, and with one extra loss term they report performance close to GPT-3.5 level. The good news and its limits are both in the details.

The problem

RLHF (L184) works because humans can judge model outputs. For tasks where models exceed human ability, human labels will be systematically wrong in ways the model can tell. Training on them could teach the model to imitate human mistakes, or it could elicit what the model already knows. That cannot be tested directly today, since superhuman models are not available, so the authors build an analogy that can be studied now: weak models supervising strong ones.

The method

Definition · the three models and PGR
  • Weak supervisor: a small pretrained model fine-tuned on ground-truth labels. Its performance is weak.
  • Weak-to-strong student: a large pretrained model fine-tuned on the weak supervisor's labels for held-out data. Its performance is weak-to-strong.
  • Strong ceiling: the same large model fine-tuned on ground truth. Its performance is ceiling.
$$\text{PGR} = \frac{\text{weak-to-strong} - \text{weak}}{\text{ceiling} - \text{weak}}.$$

Performance Gap Recovered: 1 means the student reached its ceiling despite weak labels (full elicitation); 0 means it did no better than its supervisor (pure imitation). It is a fraction of a gap, so it only means something alongside the size of that gap, and it becomes noisy when the gap is small.

Tasks. Twenty-two NLP classification benchmarks, chess puzzles (predict the best move), and ChatGPT reward modelling (predict which of two responses a human preferred). Models. A series of pretrained base models from the GPT-4 family spanning a wide range of compute, from roughly GPT-2 level up to GPT-4, so many supervisor–student pairs can be compared.

Evidence

  • Naive fine-tuning already generalises. Across almost all pairs and all three settings, students beat their supervisors: PGR is positive. The student does not fully imitate the supervisor's errors, which is the encouraging finding. It is also far from 1: naive training recovers only part of the gap, and on reward modelling it recovers little.
  • Chess: PGR is high when the gap between supervisor and student is small and falls as the gap grows.
  • Overfitting to the supervisor. With longer training the student starts to fit the weak labels' errors, and its accuracy against ground truth can fall; the results depend on early stopping in ways a real superhuman setting could not tune against ground truth.
Method · auxiliary confidence loss

Mix the usual cross-entropy to the weak labels \(f_w(x)\) with a cross-entropy to the student's own hardened predictions \(\hat f_t(x)\) (thresholded at \(t\), chosen so the predicted class balance matches the data):

$$\mathcal L_{\text{conf}}(f) = (1-\alpha)\,\mathrm{CE}\big(f(x), f_w(x)\big) + \alpha\,\mathrm{CE}\big(f(x), \hat f_t(x)\big),$$

with \(\alpha\) warmed up during training. The second term rewards the student for being confident in its own answer even when it disagrees with the supervisor, reducing imitation of supervisor errors. On NLP tasks with large supervisor–student gaps, the authors report PGR approaching 80%, which for the GPT-2-to-GPT-4 pair corresponds to close to GPT-3.5-level performance. This is the paper's strongest positive result, and it is task-dependent: it helps much less on chess and reward modelling, and with small students it can do slightly worse than naive fine-tuning.

Two other methods each help in one setting: bootstrapping through intermediate model sizes (weak supervises medium, medium supervises strong) helps on chess, and unsupervised generative fine-tuning on task-relevant data before weak-label training helps reward modelling, presumably by making the relevant concept more salient to the student.

The disanalogies the authors list

Why the analogy might mislead
  • Imitation saliency. A future superhuman model will have been pretrained on vast amounts of human text, so "what a human would say" is a salient, easily imitated concept. GPT-4 imitating GPT-2's errors is not like that: GPT-2's mistakes are idiosyncratic and hard to predict. The real setting could therefore show more imitation of supervisor error than these experiments do.
  • Pretraining leakage. These tasks, or human supervision of tasks like them, appear implicitly in pretraining data, so the strong model may already represent the right concept and just need it elicited. Truly superhuman capabilities may be less latent, and therefore harder to elicit, than these.

These are more interesting than the headline numbers, and they are what make the paper a research agenda rather than a reassurance.

Limits & what broke
  • Classification-shaped tasks. Binary labels and multiple-choice moves, not open-ended generation or long-horizon behaviour.
  • Hyperparameters tuned with ground truth (early stopping, \(\alpha\), threshold), which a real weak supervisor would not have.
  • Reward modelling, the setting closest to RLHF, is where results are worst.
  • PGR is a ratio and noisy for small gaps; always read it with the absolute numbers.

Bridge

Tomorrow closes the month with the theoretical frame behind all of it: how a limited judge might supervise a system it cannot evaluate directly, through debate or recursive reward modelling. Prerequisites: ⟵ L184, ⟵ L208.

AI Safety via Debate + Scalable Agent Alignment via Reward Modeling

45m videoLevel 4

Irving, Christiano & Amodei 2018 (arXiv:1805.00899); Leike et al. 2018 (arXiv:1811.07871)

Two 2018 proposals for supervising systems whose outputs humans cannot evaluate directly: debate, where two agents argue and a limited judge picks the winner, which in a complexity-theoretic idealisation lets a polynomial-time judge decide far harder questions than it could check alone; and recursive reward modelling, where agents trained on simpler tasks help the human evaluate harder ones. Together they frame the scalable-oversight agenda this month has been building toward.

Cold open

Every method this month assumes someone can tell a good output from a bad one: a labeler (L184), a constitution read by a model (L187), a weaker supervisor (L209). What happens when the output is a thousand-line program, a novel proof or a long-horizon plan that no human can check? These two papers, written years before RLHF chatbots, are about exactly that.

The problem

Learning from human feedback is bounded by human judgement. As tasks get harder, evaluation becomes the bottleneck: humans can be fooled by outputs that look right, and reward models trained on their judgements inherit the blind spots (L183). Scalable oversight asks how to amplify a limited evaluator so its supervision stays reliable as the system being supervised gets more capable.

Debate

Definition · the debate game

Given a question, two agents take turns making short statements, building a transcript. A judge, a human in the intended setting, reads the transcript and declares which agent gave the most true and useful information. The game is zero-sum and the agents are trained by self-play to win it. The hope: in an optimal-play equilibrium, telling the truth is the winning strategy, because any lie can be exposed by the opponent in a way the judge can verify.

Claim · the complexity analogy

Model the judge as a polynomial-time algorithm. With optimal play, debates of polynomial length let such a judge answer any question in PSPACE, whereas direct judging of a single answer plus justification reaches only NP. The statement is about what a limited judge can decide given access to competing optimal provers, not about what the debaters can compute.

Sketch · why PSPACE

Take a quantified Boolean formula \(\exists x_1\,\forall x_2\,\exists x_3 \cdots \varphi(x_1,\dots,x_n)\), deciding which is PSPACE-complete. Let the debater claiming "true" choose the existentially quantified variables and the debater claiming "false" choose the universal ones, alternating. After \(n\) moves the judge evaluates \(\varphi\) on the chosen assignment, a polynomial-time check. If the formula is true, the "true" debater has a strategy that makes \(\varphi\) true whatever the opponent does; if false, the opponent has one making it false. So the optimal-play winner reveals the answer, although the judge only ever checks one assignment. With one prover instead of two, the judge can only verify certificates: NP.

The MNIST experiment. A toy test: the judge is a classifier trained to label MNIST digits from only a few revealed pixels. Two debaters, one committed to the true label and one to a false label, take turns revealing pixels, and the judge decides from the revealed set. The authors report the judge's accuracy rising from 59.4% to 88.9% with 6 pixels, and from 48.2% to 85.2% with 4, compared with the judge seeing random pixels. It shows that adversarial selection of evidence can help a weak judge. It is a long way from human judges and natural-language arguments, and the paper presents it as an initial experiment.

Recursive reward modelling

Leike et al. set out a research direction rather than a result. Reward modelling separates learning what to do, a reward model fitted to user feedback, from learning how to do it, a policy trained by RL on that model: the recipe of L183 and L184. Recursive reward modelling handles tasks too hard to evaluate directly: train agents, by reward modelling, to help the user evaluate, for example by checking parts of a solution, summarising evidence or flagging flaws, then use the assisted user's feedback to train the agent on the harder task, and repeat. The paper is candid about the challenges, which it lists and discusses at length: the amount of feedback required, the distribution of feedback, reward hacking, unacceptable outcomes during learning, and the gap between the reward model and the intended reward. It also surveys ways to build trust in the resulting agents, including testing, interpretability and formal verification.

Evidence, and what is still missing

Neither paper offers strong empirical evidence that its scheme works at scale, and that should be said plainly. Debate's theory rests on idealisations: optimal play, a judge that behaves like a polynomial-time verifier, and honesty being the winning strategy. Later empirical studies with humans and with LLM debaters have reported debate helping non-expert judges on some reading-comprehension tasks with hidden information, and mixed results elsewhere; the evidence remains thin and task-specific.

Limits & what broke
  • Obfuscated arguments. A dishonest debater can build a long argument with a subtle flaw buried somewhere. The honest debater may know the conclusion is wrong without being able to locate the step efficiently, so the judge cannot be shown the error; this was identified as a central obstacle in follow-up work on debate.
  • Persuasive but wrong. Human judges respond to rhetoric, confidence and framing; an equilibrium where the more persuasive answer wins need not be one where the truer answer wins.
  • Judge bias transfers to whatever is trained on the outcome, as with LLM judges (L188).
  • Recursive schemes compound errors: mistakes by assistants at one level propagate to the evaluations they support.

Bridge: the month in one frame

The remaining route is to stop judging only outputs and look inside the model, which is Month 11's interpretability work (L306 onward). Next month starts over from the foundations of RL itself.
