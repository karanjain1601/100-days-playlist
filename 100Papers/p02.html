Month 3 · Days 61–90 · Lessons 61–90

Computer Vision I: Backbones and Detection

From AlexNet in 2012 to DETR in 2020, vision papers invented most of the architectural vocabulary the rest of deep learning borrowed: residual connections, 1×1 bottlenecks, normalization placement, efficient convolutions, learned sampling grids and feature pyramids. The month follows the classification backbones from AlexNet and VGG through ResNet and its descendants to the mobile, scaled and modernized ConvNets, then object detection from R-CNN's two thousand forward passes per image through Faster R-CNN, the one-stage detectors and focal loss to FPN, Mask R-CNN and DETR. Treat it as architecture school, not a vision course: it leans on last month's initialization, normalization and optimization papers, and hands its backbones, pyramids and RoI machinery to next month's segmentation, video, 3D and vision-transformer papers.

By the end of this month you can

  • Compute the parameters and FLOPs of the standard blocks by hand: stacked 3×3 against 5×5 and 7×7 convolutions, Inception's 1×1 reductions, the ResNet bottleneck, grouped and depthwise separable convolutions (the \(1/N + 1/D_k^2\) ratio), and explain why FLOPs do not predict latency on real hardware.
  • Explain why deeper plain networks fail on training error, derive the gradient through an identity shortcut, and show why pre-activation ordering gives every residual block a clean additive path.
  • Place each backbone on the design axes it changes (depth, width, cardinality, addition versus concatenation, channel attention, compound scaling), and separate architecture from training recipe in a reported gain, using ConvNeXt's roadmap and RegNet's error distributions.
  • Implement bilinear sampling and use it to explain deformable convolution, spatial transformers and RoIAlign, and compute the receptive field of a stack of dilated convolutions.
  • Define IoU, mAP, anchors and the box-offset parameterization, and trace the detection lineage from R-CNN through Fast and Faster R-CNN to YOLO and SSD by the bottleneck each paper removed.
  • Diagnose class imbalance in dense detection, derive focal loss's down-weighting and the prior-probability bias initialization, and explain how FPN's top-down pathway makes every pyramid level semantically strong.
  • Explain how Mask R-CNN's decoupled per-class masks and RoIAlign produce pixel-accurate instances, and why DETR's one-to-one bipartite matching removes the need for anchors and non-maximum suppression.

L61 · DAY 61

ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks (AlexNet)

55m videoLevel 2

Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, NIPS 2012

A large convolutional network trained on 1.2 million labelled images, using GPUs, ReLUs, dropout and heavy augmentation, beat hand-engineered vision pipelines by a margin nobody could ignore. Almost every ingredient already existed. The scale did not.

Cold open

ILSVRC-2012, classification task. The team "SuperVision" reported a top-5 test error of 15.3%. The second-best entry reached 26.2%, a gap of almost 11 points. The paper describes that runner-up as an average of classifiers trained on Fisher vectors computed from several kinds of densely sampled, hand-designed local descriptors. That was the state of the art: SIFT-style features (Day 108), an encoding step, and a linear classifier. Within two years nearly every competitive entry was a convnet.

The problem

Convnets had worked since LeNet-5 (Day 33), but only on small, low-resolution datasets such as MNIST, NORB and CIFAR. Two objections kept them out of mainstream vision. First, a network big enough for 1000 classes of high-resolution photos would have tens of millions of parameters, and the field expected it to overfit. Second, training one on a CPU would take months. ImageNet (Day 111) answered the first objection with data, and a hand-written CUDA implementation answered the second with compute.

The method: eight layers on two GPUs

Five convolutional layers and three fully connected layers, about 60 million parameters and 650,000 neurons. Conv1 applies 96 kernels of 11×11×3 with stride 4; conv2 has 256 kernels of 5×5; conv3, conv4 and conv5 are 3×3 with 384, 384 and 256 kernels; then FC layers of 4096, 4096 and a 1000-way softmax. Max-pooling follows conv1, conv2 and conv5.

The accidental consequence is the paper's most famous figure. The 48 first-layer kernels on GPU 1 came out largely colour-agnostic (oriented edges, gratings) and the 48 on GPU 2 largely colour-specific (colour blobs). The authors report that this happens in every run, independent of initialization up to swapping the GPUs. The restricted connectivity forced a division of labour that nobody designed.

Derivation · where the 60M parameters live

With a 227×227 input (the arithmetic only works for 227, not the 224 the paper states), conv1 gives \((227-11)/4+1 = 55\), pooling 3/2 gives 27, conv2 keeps 27, pooling gives 13, conv3–5 keep 13, and the last pool gives 6×6×256. Weights per layer:

$$\underbrace{11^2\!\cdot\!3\!\cdot\!96}_{35\text{k}} + \underbrace{5^2\!\cdot\!48\!\cdot\!256}_{307\text{k}} + \underbrace{3^2\!\cdot\!256\!\cdot\!384}_{885\text{k}} + \underbrace{3^2\!\cdot\!192\!\cdot\!384}_{664\text{k}} + \underbrace{3^2\!\cdot\!192\!\cdot\!256}_{442\text{k}} \approx 2.3\text{M}$$ $$\underbrace{6\!\cdot\!6\!\cdot\!256\!\cdot\!4096}_{37.7\text{M}} + \underbrace{4096^2}_{16.8\text{M}} + \underbrace{4096\!\cdot\!1000}_{4.1\text{M}} \approx 58.6\text{M}.$$

About 96% of the parameters sit in the FC layers, while the conv layers do most of the arithmetic. Day 63 deletes those FC layers.

The method: four ingredients, attributed properly

  • ReLU, credited to Nair & Hinton (2010) (Day 45). The paper's evidence is a four-layer net on CIFAR-10 that reaches 25% training error about six times faster with ReLU than with tanh. Non-saturating units keep gradients alive.
  • GPUs. Two GTX 580s, five to six days of training. GPU convnets were not new (Cireşan et al. had them in 2011); a fast, careful implementation at ImageNet scale was.
  • Dropout (Hinton et al. 2012, Day 46) with \(p = 0.5\) in the first two FC layers, which is where the parameters are. It roughly doubled the iterations needed to converge; without it the network overfit badly.
  • Augmentation. Random 224×224 crops of the 256×256 images plus horizontal flips; at test time, average over ten patches (four corners, centre, and their flips). Then PCA colour augmentation: to every pixel add
$$I_{xy} \leftarrow I_{xy} + [\mathbf p_1, \mathbf p_2, \mathbf p_3]\,[\alpha_1\lambda_1,\ \alpha_2\lambda_2,\ \alpha_3\lambda_3]^\top,\qquad \alpha_i \sim \mathcal N(0, 0.1^2),$$

where \(\mathbf p_i, \lambda_i\) are eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the 3×3 covariance of RGB values over the training set, and the \(\alpha_i\) are redrawn each time an image is used. It perturbs illumination colour and intensity along the directions natural images actually vary. The authors report it cuts top-1 error by over 1%.

Two smaller pieces. Overlapping pooling (3×3 windows, stride 2) gave a few tenths of a point over non-overlapping 2×2. Local Response Normalization, applied after ReLU in conv1 and conv2, divides each activation by the energy of its neighbours across \(n\) adjacent channels:

$$b^i_{x,y} = a^i_{x,y} \Big/ \Big(k + \alpha \!\!\sum_{j=\max(0,\,i-n/2)}^{\min(N-1,\,i+n/2)}\!\! \big(a^j_{x,y}\big)^2\Big)^{\beta},\qquad k=2,\ n=5,\ \alpha=10^{-4},\ \beta=0.75.$$

Inspired by lateral inhibition, it was reported to cut top-1/top-5 error by 1.4%/1.2%. It died quickly: VGG (Day 64) found it gave no gain, and Batch Normalization (Day 47) took over the job of controlling activation scale.

Evidence

On ILSVRC-2010, where test labels were public, the network reports 37.5% top-1 and 17.0% top-5 error, more than ten points better top-5 than the best 2010 competition entry. For 2012 the paper's table separates the pieces: one CNN gives about 18.2% top-5 on validation, an average of five gives 16.4%, and the 15.3% test entry adds two networks pre-trained on the full 15-million-image Fall 2011 ImageNet release. Removing any middle convolutional layer costs about 2% top-1, the first hint that depth itself matters. Qualitatively, images whose 4096-d fc7 vectors are close are semantically similar even when their pixels are far apart.

What the evidence does not show: there is no full-scale ablation of ReLU, dropout and augmentation one at a time, and the headline number is an ensemble with extra pre-training data, not a single model.

Limits & what broke
  • Nothing here was conceptually new except scale. Convolution, pooling, backprop and SGD are LeNet-5 (1998). ReLU, dropout and GPU convnets each had prior papers. The contribution was showing that the combination works when you have enough data and compute, which is a real contribution of a different kind.
  • The first layer aliased. 11×11 kernels at stride 4 left dead and high-frequency-only filters; ZFNet diagnosed and fixed it the next year (Day 62).
  • LRN turned out to be useless; the GPU split disappeared once memory grew; the FC head was 96% of the parameters and was replaced by global average pooling (Day 63).
  • Reproduction details matter. The 224/227 mismatch, biases initialized to 1 in some layers, and ten-crop testing all affect reported numbers. Comparing a "single-crop AlexNet" with the paper's numbers is a classic apples-to-oranges error.

Visualizing and Understanding Convolutional Networks (ZFNet)

45m videoLevel 2

Zeiler & Fergus, ECCV 2014 (arXiv:1311.2901)

Zeiler and Fergus built a probe that projects any single activation back into pixel space, used it to see what each layer of an AlexNet-style network responds to, and then used what they saw to fix the architecture. Debugging a network by looking at it is the real contribution.

Cold open

One figure carries the paper: for layers 1 to 5, the nine strongest activations of a feature map across the validation set, each projected back to pixels next to the image patch that caused it. Layer 1 is edges and colour blobs. Layer 2 is corners and edge–colour conjunctions. Layer 3 is textures and repeated patterns such as meshes and text. Layer 4 is class-specific parts: dog faces, bird legs. Layer 5 is whole objects in varied poses. The hierarchy everyone had assumed was now visible. Then came the twist: the same tool showed that AlexNet's first two layers were damaged, and fixing that improved the model.

The problem

By late 2013 AlexNet (Day 61) worked and nobody could say why. The existing visualization tools were weak above the first layer. You could show the first-layer filters directly, since they live in pixel space. For deeper units you could list the image patches that activate them most, which tells you which inputs fire a unit but not what part of them does, or optimize an input to maximize a unit by gradient ascent, which at the time gave hard-to-read images. Without a way to see deeper features, architecture design was trial and error.

The method: a deconvnet attached to every layer

A deconvnet runs a convnet's operations in reverse, using the convnet's own weights and learning nothing new. To examine one activation, present an image, run the forward pass, set every other activation in that layer to zero, and pass the resulting maps down through the attached deconvnet layers to pixel space. Each layer is undone by three steps, in reverse order of the forward pass.

  • Unpooling. Max pooling discards which input won. During the forward pass the deconvnet records the location of each maximum in "switch" variables; unpooling places the signal at those locations and zeros elsewhere.
  • Rectification. The unpooled signal passes through a ReLU, keeping the reconstruction positive as the forward features are.
  • Filtering. Convolve with the transposed filters (each kernel flipped horizontally and vertically), the same operation backprop uses to send gradients to a layer's input.
Why this is not an inverse

The switches come from this image's forward pass, so the reconstruction is conditioned on the input: it shows which structure in this image excited the unit, not the unit's preferred stimulus in general. Change the image and the same unit gives a different picture. Nothing about it is generative. Simonyan et al. later showed (Day 301) that the deconvnet is almost the gradient of the activation with respect to the input. The two differ only at ReLUs. With \(f_l\) the forward input to a ReLU and \(R_{l+1}\) the signal arriving from above:

$$\text{backprop: } R_l = \mathbb 1[f_l > 0]\, R_{l+1},\qquad \text{deconvnet: } R_l = \mathbb 1[R_{l+1} > 0]\, R_{l+1},\qquad \text{guided backprop: } R_l = \mathbb 1[f_l > 0]\,\mathbb 1[R_{l+1} > 0]\, R_{l+1}.$$

Guided backprop (Springenberg et al. 2015) combines the two masks. All three methods descend from this paper.

The method: looking, then fixing

Feature evolution. Projecting the strongest activation of each feature at several checkpoints during training shows the lower layers converging within a few epochs, while the upper layers only take shape after many tens of epochs (the paper puts it at 40–50). The practical lesson: don't stop early and conclude that the top layers learned nothing.

Architecture selection. Visualizing AlexNet's first layer showed a mix of very high- and very low-frequency filters with little in between, plus some dead filters. The second layer showed aliasing artifacts, which the authors attribute to the stride of 4 in the first layer. Their fix: 7×7 first-layer filters at stride 2. They also renormalized any convolutional filter whose RMS exceeded a fixed radius, so that a few filters could not dominate. The new first and second layers kept more distinctive features with fewer dead units. A 7×7 stride-2 stem survives in GoogLeNet and ResNet.

Occlusion sensitivity. Is the network recognizing the object, or the context around it? Slide a grey square across the image and record the probability of the correct class at each position. The probability drops sharply when the square covers the object (a dog's face, say) and barely moves when it covers the background, so the model localizes what it classifies. Unlike the deconvnet pictures, this is a causal test: you intervene on the input and measure the output.

import numpy as np

def occlusion_map(img, predict, target, patch=32, stride=16, fill=0.5):
    """img: H x W x 3 in [0, 1]; predict: batch of images -> class probabilities."""
    H, W, _ = img.shape
    rows = range(0, H - patch + 1, stride)
    cols = range(0, W - patch + 1, stride)
    heat = np.zeros((len(rows), len(cols)))
    for i, y in enumerate(rows):
        batch = []
        for x in cols:
            occluded = img.copy()
            occluded[y:y + patch, x:x + patch] = fill      # grey square
            batch.append(occluded)
        heat[i] = predict(np.stack(batch))[:, target]
    return heat                                            # low = the prediction depends on it

Evidence

The authors first replicate AlexNet's single-model ImageNet-2012 error, then show that their modified architecture beats the replication by more than a point of top-5 error, and that an ensemble of their models edges below AlexNet's 15.3% test error. An ablation table removes layers: dropping the two middle FC layers, or two middle convolutional layers, changes error only modestly, while dropping both hurts badly, so overall depth matters more than any single layer. Widening the middle conv layers helps; widening the FC layers as well starts to overfit.

Transfer is the other half of the evidence. Freezing layers 1–7 and training only a new softmax on Caltech-101 and Caltech-256 beat the previous best results, on Caltech-256 by a wide margin and with only a handful of training images per class. On PASCAL VOC 2012, whose images contain several objects each, the frozen features did less well and fell short of the leading result overall, which the authors attribute to dataset bias. A descendant of this model, entered by Zeiler's startup Clarifai, won the ILSVRC 2013 classification task.

Limits & what broke
  • The pictures are anecdotes. Top-9 patches for hand-picked features are not a measurement. There is no test that a visualization is faithful to the model's computation.
  • The descendants failed sanity checks. Adebayo et al. (2018, Day 305) found that guided backprop gives nearly the same maps after the model's weights are randomized, so it behaves partly like an edge detector on the input. That should make you cautious about reading too much into deconvnet images.
  • The aliasing diagnosis was visual. The fix improved accuracy, but the paper does not isolate how much of the gain comes from the stride change versus the filter size or the renormalization.
  • What lasted: occlusion sensitivity, which is causal and model-agnostic, and the habit of inspecting a model before redesigning it.

Network In Network

30m videoLevel 2

Lin, Chen & Yan, ICLR 2014 (arXiv:1312.4400)

Network In Network replaced each linear convolution filter with a tiny MLP slid across the image, which is just a stack of 1×1 convolutions, and replaced the fully connected classifier with global average pooling. Both ideas became standard parts of nearly every CNN that followed.

Cold open

About 58.6 of AlexNet's roughly 61 million parameters sit in its three fully connected layers (Day 61), well over 90% of the model. Network In Network deletes them. It has no fully connected layers at all: the last convolutional layer emits one feature map per class, each map is averaged to a single number, and those numbers go straight into the softmax. With that head and a stack of 1×1 convolutions, the paper reports state-of-the-art error on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 at the time.

The problem

The authors' argument starts from what a convolution filter is: a generalized linear model applied to a local patch, followed by a nonlinearity. That is a good detector only if the concept it looks for is linearly separable in patch space. For anything else the network needs many filters in one layer and has to combine them in the next. Maxout networks (Goodfellow et al. 2013), the main baseline, made the local unit piecewise linear. NIN asks for a general function approximator instead: a small MLP over each patch.

The second complaint is the classifier. FC layers on top of conv features are where overfitting lives (hence dropout, Day 46), and they are a black box between spatial feature maps and class scores.

The method: mlpconv is a 1×1 convolution

An mlpconv layer computes, at every position \((i, j)\),

$$f^{1}_{i,j,k} = \max\!\big(\mathbf w^{1\top}_{k}\,\mathbf x_{i,j} + b^1_{k},\ 0\big),\qquad f^{n}_{i,j,k} = \max\!\big(\mathbf w^{n\top}_{k}\,\mathbf f^{\,n-1}_{i,j} + b^n_{k},\ 0\big),$$

where \(\mathbf x_{i,j}\) is the input patch centred at \((i,j)\). The first layer is an ordinary convolution. Every layer after it takes the channel vector at one position and applies the same weights at every position. That is exactly a 1×1 convolution, which the paper calls cross-channel parametric pooling.

The equivalence is literal, and five lines of NumPy show it:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
H, W, C_in, C_out = 8, 8, 64, 16
x = rng.normal(size=(H, W, C_in))
Wt, b = rng.normal(size=(C_out, C_in)), rng.normal(size=C_out)

y_conv = np.empty((H, W, C_out))                      # a 1x1 convolution, pixel by pixel
for i in range(H):
    for j in range(W):
        y_conv[i, j] = Wt @ x[i, j] + b

y_fc = (x.reshape(-1, C_in) @ Wt.T + b).reshape(H, W, C_out)   # one FC layer on every pixel
assert np.allclose(y_conv, y_fc)
Definition · cost of a 1×1 convolution

On an \(H \times W \times C_\text{in}\) input producing \(C_\text{out}\) channels: \(C_\text{in} C_\text{out}\) weights and \(HW\,C_\text{in} C_\text{out}\) multiply-adds. A 3×3 convolution with the same channels costs 9× more. Followed by a ReLU, a 1×1 layer adds a nonlinearity and can change the channel count, while leaving the receptive field unchanged. It is the cheapest expressivity you can buy, and changing channel counts cheaply is exactly what Inception's reductions (Day 65) and ResNet's bottlenecks (Day 67) need.

The method: global average pooling as the head

The last mlpconv layer outputs \(K\) maps for \(K\) classes. Global average pooling (GAP) replaces each map with its spatial mean, and the softmax acts on those \(K\) numbers. Three consequences, as the authors argue them: no parameters to overfit in the head; robustness to spatial translation, because position is averaged away; and a structural regularizer, because map \(k\) is forced to act as a confidence map for class \(k\), which ties channels to classes.

Derivation · why GAP gives you localization for free

Modern networks put one linear layer after GAP. With final maps \(f_k(i,j)\) and class weights \(w_{ck}\), linearity lets the average and the sum swap:

$$s_c = \sum_k w_{ck}\,\frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j} f_k(i,j) = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j}\underbrace{\sum_k w_{ck}\, f_k(i,j)}_{M_c(i,j)}.$$

The class score is the spatial average of a class activation map \(M_c\), which shows where the evidence for class \(c\) sits. That is CAM (Zhou et al. 2016), and Grad-CAM (Day 302) generalizes it to networks without a GAP head by using gradients as the weights. The same swap shows that "1×1 conv to \(K\) channels, then GAP" and "GAP, then a linear layer" are the same function.

Evidence

The models are three stacked mlpconv layers with dropout between them, evaluated on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN and MNIST. The paper reports state-of-the-art test error on both CIFAR datasets at the time (about 10.4% on CIFAR-10 without augmentation and 8.8% with it) and competitive results on SVHN and MNIST. The cleanest experiment holds the mlpconv layers fixed and swaps only the head: GAP gives lower CIFAR-10 error than an FC head with dropout, which in turn beats an FC head without dropout. A visualization of the final maps shows the strongest responses on the region containing the ground-truth object, evidence that the class-map interpretation is real.

What it doesn't show: whether "micro-network abstraction" explains anything beyond the extra depth and nonlinearity the 1×1 layers add. The paper has no ImageNet-scale headline result.

Limits & what broke
  • The framing faded; the parts survived. Nobody says "mlpconv" today. Everybody uses the 1×1 convolution.
  • Pure GAP heads were replaced by GAP plus one linear layer, which decouples the last conv width from the number of classes. ResNet-50's head is a 2048×1000 linear layer after GAP: about 2M of its roughly 25M parameters.
  • GAP throws away layout. Counting, relative position and anything dense need a different head (Month 4).
  • "Cheap" is about FLOPs. A 1×1 convolution is a matrix multiply and runs well on hardware, but stacks of thin layers become memory-bound, a theme that returns on Days 73–75.

L64 · DAY 64

Very Deep Convolutional Networks (VGG)

40m videoLevel 2

Simonyan & Zisserman, ICLR 2015 (arXiv:1409.1556)

VGG fixed every design choice except depth, built networks from 11 to 19 layers using only 3×3 convolutions, and showed that depth alone buys accuracy. Stacking small filters, which gives the same receptive field with fewer parameters and more nonlinearities, became the default way to build a CNN.

Cold open

In ILSVRC 2014 VGG came second in classification, behind GoogLeNet (Day 65), and first in localization. It still became the more widely used network, because anyone could write it down from memory: blocks of 3×3 convolutions, 2×2 max-pooling, double the channels after each pool, three FC layers. A decade later its intermediate features remain a standard backbone for perceptual and style losses.

The problem

After AlexNet (Day 61), improvements came from tweaks to the first layer (smaller window and stride, as in ZFNet, Day 62) and from training and testing over multiple scales (OverFeat). Depth had not been studied on its own. VGG holds everything else fixed and varies depth.

The method: one rule, five configurations

Every convolution is 3×3, stride 1, padding 1, so spatial size changes only at the five 2×2 max-pools. Widths go 64 → 128 → 256 → 512 → 512, then FC layers of 4096, 4096 and 1000. The configurations differ only in how many conv layers each block has.

Derivation · the 3×3 arithmetic

A stride-1 \(k \times k\) layer grows the receptive field by \(k - 1\). Starting from one pixel, \(n\) layers of 3×3 give \(1 + 2n\): 5 for two layers, 7 for three. With \(C\) input and \(C\) output channels, each 3×3 layer has \(3^2C^2 = 9C^2\) weights, so

$$\underbrace{2\cdot 3^2 C^2 = 18C^2}_{\text{two } 3\times3} \;<\; \underbrace{5^2C^2 = 25C^2}_{\text{one } 5\times5},\qquad \underbrace{3\cdot 3^2C^2 = 27C^2}_{\text{three } 3\times3} \;<\; \underbrace{7^2C^2 = 49C^2}_{\text{one } 7\times7}.$$

The 7×7 needs 81% more parameters than the stack. The stack also has three ReLUs instead of one, which the authors argue makes the decision function more discriminative, and they describe the factorization as a kind of regularization on the 7×7 filters. FLOPs scale the same way, since every weight is applied once per output position.

Derivation · where VGG-16's 138M parameters are

The thirteen conv layers hold about 14.7M weights. The head is \(7\cdot7\cdot512\cdot4096 \approx 102.8\)M (FC6) \(+\ 4096^2 \approx 16.8\)M (FC7) \(+\ 4096\cdot1000 \approx 4.1\)M (FC8), about 123.6M. So the FC layers hold roughly 89% of the parameters, and FC6 alone about three quarters. The compute is the reverse: about 15.5 billion multiply-adds per 224×224 image, more than 99% of it in the convolutions.

The method: training and testing

SGD with momentum 0.9, batch 256, weight decay \(5\cdot10^{-4}\), dropout 0.5 on the first two FC layers, learning rate \(10^{-2}\) divided by 10 three times. Depth made initialization a real problem. Configuration A was shallow enough to train from random weights. For the deeper networks the authors initialized the first four conv layers and the three FC layers from the trained A and drew the middle layers at random. They note that after submission they found the Glorot & Bengio initialization (Day 43) works without this pre-training. Staged initialization was a workaround for a missing theory of initialization, which He et al. supplied months later (Day 44).

Scale handling: training images are rescaled so their shorter side is \(S\), either fixed (256 or 384) or randomly drawn from \([256, 512]\) per image ("scale jittering"), and then a 224×224 crop is taken. At test time the FC layers are turned into convolutions (FC6 becomes a 7×7 conv, FC7 and FC8 become 1×1), so the network runs on a whole image of any size and the class-score map is averaged. Dense evaluation and multi-crop evaluation are combined for the final numbers.

Evidence

With everything else fixed, error falls steadily from A to E and saturates at 19 layers on this data. A-LRN is no better than A, so LRN is dropped (Day 61). C, with 1×1 layers, beats B but loses to D: extra nonlinearity helps, but so does spatial context. The authors also replace each pair of 3×3 layers in B with a single 5×5 and report roughly 7% higher top-1 error, direct evidence for the stacking argument. Scale jittering helps even when testing at a single scale. The ILSVRC 2014 submission, an ensemble of seven networks, reached 7.3% top-5 test error; after the competition a two-model ensemble reached 6.8%.

Limits & what broke
  • Expensive. 138M parameters and about 15.5 GMACs, against GoogLeNet's ~5M parameters and ~1.5 GMACs the same year. The authors report two to three weeks to train one network on four GPUs.
  • Depth saturated at 19. The paper does not explain why going deeper stopped helping. ResNet (Day 67) shows deeper plain networks get worse, even on training error.
  • The head was the parameter problem. Replacing it with GAP (Day 63) removes almost 90% of the weights, as GoogLeNet and ResNet did.
  • Staged initialization made the results fragile to reproduce until better initialization schemes and BatchNorm (Day 47) arrived.

Going Deeper with Convolutions (GoogLeNet / Inception v1)

45m videoLevel 2

Szegedy et al., CVPR 2015 (arXiv:1409.4842)

GoogLeNet made a 22-layer network cheap. It runs 1×1, 3×3 and 5×5 convolutions and pooling side by side, and puts 1×1 convolutions in front of the expensive branches to cut the channel count first. The result won ILSVRC 2014 with roughly 5 million parameters.

Cold open

ILSVRC 2014 classification: 6.67% top-5 error, first place, from a network 22 layers deep that was designed to stay within a budget of about 1.5 billion multiply-adds per image. The authors stress that it uses about 12 times fewer parameters than AlexNet (Day 61) while being far more accurate. VGG (Day 64), second that year, needed 138M parameters and about ten times the compute.

The problem

The obvious way to improve a network is to make it bigger, but uniformly widening two chained conv layers increases their compute quadratically, and much of that extra capacity may be wasted if many weights end up near zero. Sparse connectivity would be the principled fix. The authors cite Arora et al. (2014): if a dataset's distribution can be represented by a large, very sparse deep network, the optimal topology can be built layer by layer by clustering units whose activations are highly correlated, the Hebbian "fire together, wire together" principle. But hardware is fast at dense matrix arithmetic and slow at irregular sparse structures. Inception is the compromise: approximate a locally sparse structure with dense, off-the-shelf parts.

The method: the Inception module

The intuition: correlated units cluster in local regions (a 1×1 convolution covers those), while a smaller number of more spread-out clusters need 3×3 and 5×5 windows. So run all of them in parallel, add a pooling branch because pooling was known to help, and concatenate the outputs along channels. That naive module has a fatal cost problem: the 3×3 and 5×5 convolutions act on all the input channels, and the pooling branch passes every input channel through, so the output is always wider than the input and the width snowballs from stage to stage.

The fix is the 1×1 convolution of Day 63, used for dimensionality reduction. A 1×1 layer with a ReLU shrinks the channels before each 3×3 and 5×5, and another one projects the pooling branch down.

Derivation · the number that is the episode

Count multiply-adds for inception (3a): input 28×28×192, so \(HW = 784\). A \(k\times k\) convolution from \(C_\text{in}\) to \(C_\text{out}\) costs \(HW\,k^2 C_\text{in} C_\text{out}\).

The method: the full network

A conventional stem (7×7 stride-2 conv, max-pool, LRN, 1×1 and 3×3 convs, LRN, max-pool), then nine Inception modules (3a–3b, 4a–4e, 5a–5b) with max-pools between stages, then global average pooling over a 7×7 map, 40% dropout and a single linear layer to the softmax. That is 22 layers with parameters, 27 counting pooling. The GAP head (Day 63) is why the parameter count stays near 5M. The training augmentation is worth knowing because it outlived the architecture: random crops covering 8% to 100% of the image area with aspect ratios between 3/4 and 4/3, resized to the input size. That is the default RandomResizedCrop in torchvision today.

Auxiliary classifiers. Two small heads sit on top of modules 4a and 4d: 5×5 average pooling with stride 3, a 1×1 conv with 128 filters, an FC layer of 1024, 70% dropout, and a softmax. Their losses are added with weight 0.3 during training, and they are discarded at inference. The stated intent was to push gradient into the middle of a deep network and to make intermediate features discriminative.

Evidence

The 6.67% headline combines seven models and 144 crops per image (4 scales × 3 squares × 6 crops × 2 flips). The paper's own breakdown shows how much of it is test-time machinery: a single model on a single crop is around 10% top-5 on validation. Detection used the same network inside an R-CNN-style pipeline (Day 82) and also took first place. There is no ablation of the module design itself: no comparison against a VGG-style stack at equal FLOPs, and no test of the Arora-et-al. motivation.

Limits & what broke
  • The auxiliary heads did not do what was intended. The Inception-v3 paper (Day 66) reports that they did not speed up convergence early in training, that removing the lower one made no difference, and that they help slightly at the end as regularizers, not gradient highways: the main classifier did better when the side branch had batch-norm or dropout.
  • Candour about the design. The authors say the topology came out of extensive manual experimentation, and that it remains questionable whether its quality can be attributed to the guiding principles. "We tried architectures until one worked" is an honest description, and rare in print.
  • Hard to modify. Hand-tuned per-module channel counts make the network hard to scale or adapt, a complaint Inception-v3 makes explicitly.
  • FLOPs are not speed. Many small parallel branches fragment the computation and run below their FLOP count on GPUs, as ShuffleNetV2's guidelines quantify (Day 75).
  • Superseded within a year by ResNet (Day 67), which went far deeper with a much simpler block.

L66 · DAY 66

Rethinking the Inception Architecture (Inception-v2/v3)

40m videoLevel 2

Szegedy et al., CVPR 2016 (arXiv:1512.00567)

Inception-v3 replaced GoogLeNet's ad hoc module with stated design principles, factorized large convolutions into small and asymmetric ones, redesigned downsampling so it creates no bottleneck, and introduced label smoothing, which has since become a default in classifier training.

Cold open

Section 2 of this paper does something architecture papers almost never do: it states, in four numbered points, the rules the authors design by. They admit the rules are speculative and only partly validated, and then use them to justify every block in the network. Read them verbatim on screen. They are the closest thing this era has to a written theory of CNN design.

The problem

GoogLeNet (Day 65) was efficient but brittle. Its per-module channel counts were hand-tuned, so scaling it up naively threw away the computational savings, and nobody could say which parts mattered. VGG (Day 64) was easy to modify but cost ten times as much. The goal was a family that scales up efficiently, with reasons for each choice.

The method: four principles, paraphrased

  1. Avoid representational bottlenecks, especially early. The representation size should shrink gently from input to output. Dimensionality is only a rough proxy for information content, but sharp drops lose information that later layers cannot recover.
  2. Higher-dimensional representations are easier to process locally. More activations per spatial tile give more disentangled features and faster training.
  3. Spatial aggregation can be done over lower-dimensional embeddings with little loss, because neighbouring units are strongly correlated. This is the justification for reducing channels before a 3×3.
  4. Balance width and depth. For a fixed budget, increase both together.

The method: factorizing convolutions

Smaller kernels. As in VGG, a 5×5 is replaced by two 3×3 layers: same receptive field, \(18/25\), a 28% saving. The authors check whether the first of the pair should be linear (it is a factorization, after all) and find ReLU in both positions always better.

Asymmetric kernels. Go further: a 3×3 becomes a 1×3 followed by a 3×1, which covers the same 3×3 window for 6 weights instead of 9, a 33% saving. (Splitting into two 2×2 layers would save only 11%.) In general an \(n\times n\) becomes \(1\times n\) then \(n\times1\), turning \(n^2\) weights into \(2n\). The detail most summaries drop: the authors report that this works poorly in early layers and very well on medium grid sizes, between 12×12 and 20×20, and they use 1×7 and 7×1 layers on the 17×17 grid.

Downsampling without a bottleneck. Pooling first and then convolving creates a bottleneck (principle 1); convolving at full resolution and then pooling costs four times as much, since the convolution runs on a grid with four times the positions. The fix is the parallel block above: a stride-2 conv branch and a stride-2 pooling branch, concatenated.

The method: label smoothing

Derivation · what label smoothing does to the logits

With softmax \(p_k = e^{z_k}/\sum_i e^{z_i}\) and target distribution \(q\), the cross-entropy is \(\ell = -\sum_k q_k \log p_k\) and its gradient is \(\partial\ell/\partial z_k = p_k - q_k\). With a one-hot \(q\), the loss keeps falling as \(z_y - z_k \to \infty\), so the model is rewarded for ever-larger logits. The authors name two costs: overfitting, and reduced adaptivity, since huge logit gaps combined with a bounded gradient make the network slow to change its mind. Label smoothing mixes the target with the uniform distribution \(u_k = 1/K\):

$$q'_k = (1-\varepsilon)\,\delta_{k,y} + \frac{\varepsilon}{K},\qquad H(q', p) = (1-\varepsilon)\,H(q, p) + \varepsilon\, H(u, p).$$

The second term penalizes predictions far from uniform, since \(H(u,p) = \KL(u\,\|\,p) + H(u)\). Cross-entropy is minimized at \(p = q'\) (Gibbs' inequality), so the optimal logits have a finite gap, the same for every wrong class:

$$z_y - z_k = \log\frac{1-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/K}{\varepsilon/K} = \log\Big(1 + \frac{K(1-\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}\Big) \approx \log 9001 \approx 9.1\quad(\varepsilon = 0.1,\ K = 1000).$$

The paper uses \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) on ImageNet and reports a gain of about 0.2 points in both top-1 and top-5 error. Small, but it costs nothing, and the Transformer (Day 129) and most modern image classifiers use the same value. Its lasting effect is on calibration: a smoothed model is less overconfident, which connects to Guo et al.'s calibration paper (Day 326).

BN-auxiliary. One auxiliary head is kept, with batch normalization in its FC layer, which gives a small further gain. This is the evidence, cited yesterday, that auxiliary heads act as regularizers.

Evidence

Table 3 is cumulative: starting from the factorized "Inception-v2" network, each row adds one change (RMSProp, label smoothing, a factorized 7×7 stem, BN-auxiliary), and the last row is what the paper calls Inception-v3. The abstract reports 21.2% top-1 and 5.6% top-5 for a single model on a single crop, and 3.5% top-5 on validation for an ensemble of four models with multi-crop evaluation. A side experiment holds compute fixed and lowers the input resolution: much lower-resolution inputs reach nearly the same accuracy when the early strides are adjusted, which matters for classifying small regions in detectors.

Naming trap

In this paper "Inception-v2" is the factorized architecture and "Inception-v3" is v2 plus the training changes above. Elsewhere, the BatchNorm paper's network (Day 47) is often called Inception-v2 too. Check which one a citation means.

Limits & what broke
  • The principles are heuristics. The authors call them speculative. None is tested in isolation, and the cumulative ablation table cannot tell you what removing any one change would do.
  • Asymmetric factorization is not universal: it hurts early in the network, by the authors' own account.
  • Label smoothing has a cost. Müller, Kornblith & Hinton (2019) show it improves calibration but erases information from the logits about how similar the wrong classes are, so teachers trained with it distil worse.
  • Still hand-crafted. Every grid size has its own module type, and ResNet's single repeated block (Day 67) proved far easier to scale. Later work automated these choices with search (Day 76).

L67 · DAY 67

Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition (ResNet)

60m videoLevel 2

He, Zhang, Ren & Sun, CVPR 2016 (arXiv:1512.03385)

Deeper plain networks reach higher training error than shallower ones, which is an optimization failure, not overfitting. Letting each block learn a residual on top of an identity shortcut, \(y = F(x) + x\), makes networks of 100+ layers trainable, and the resulting block became the most copied unit in deep learning.

Cold open

Figure 1 of the paper: two plain convolutional networks on CIFAR-10, one with 20 layers and one with 56. The 56-layer network is worse on test error, which nobody would find surprising, and also worse on training error throughout training, which should be impossible if depth only added capacity. The whole paper hinges on that one curve, and it is still constantly misreported as overfitting.

The problem

Why can't the deeper net simply match the shallower one? The authors give a construction. Take a trained 20-layer network and add 36 layers that compute the identity. The resulting 56-layer network has exactly the shallow net's training error, so a solution at least that good exists. The optimizer just doesn't find it. Their conclusion is that stacks of nonlinear layers have trouble even approximating the identity.

The authors are careful about the cause. Their plain networks use BatchNorm (Day 47) and He initialization (Day 44), and they check that forward activations have healthy variance and backward gradients healthy norms, so they argue that vanishing gradients in the classic sense (Day 42) are unlikely to be the cause. They conjecture that deep plain networks have exponentially slow convergence. Highway networks (Srivastava et al. 2015) had already added gated shortcuts, but their gates can close, and they had not shown accuracy gains from extreme depth.

The method: learn the residual

Let \(H(x)\) be the mapping a few stacked layers should learn. Instead, make them learn \(F(x) = H(x) - x\) and add the input back:

$$y = F(x, \{W_i\}) + x,\qquad F = W_2\,\sigma(W_1 x)\ \ (\text{BN omitted}),\qquad \text{output } \sigma(y).$$

The identity-is-easy argument: if the identity is optimal for a block, the optimizer only has to drive \(F\)'s weights to zero, which is much easier than fitting the identity with a stack of nonlinear layers. The identity is rarely optimal, but if the target is closer to the identity than to zero, learning a small perturbation is the easier problem. The paper backs this up: the responses of the residual branches have small standard deviation, and smaller still in deeper ResNets. When the shapes differ, the shortcut becomes a projection \(W_s x\).

Derivation · gradient flow through the shortcut

For \(y = F(x) + x\) (ignore the ReLU after the addition for now), the chain rule gives

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\Big(I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\Big) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}.$$

The first term passes the upstream gradient through untouched, however small \(\partial F/\partial x\) is. Stack \(n\) blocks and the end-to-end Jacobian is \(\prod_k (I + J_k)\), whose expansion always contains the identity. A plain stack gives \(\prod_k J_k\), which can shrink or grow exponentially with depth. That "+1" is why the signal survives. In this paper it is not perfectly clean, because a ReLU sits on the path after each addition; tomorrow's paper (Day 68) removes it and makes the argument exact.

The method: shortcuts, bottlenecks, the full network

Options A, B, C. When a stage doubles the channels, the shortcut must change shape. (A) zero-pad the extra channels, so every shortcut is parameter-free; (B) use 1×1 projections only where the shape changes; (C) use projections everywhere. All three beat the plain network by a wide margin. B is slightly better than A, since the zero-padded channels get no residual learning, and C is marginally better than B, which the authors attribute to the extra parameters. Their conclusion: projections are not essential. Identity shortcuts matter most for bottleneck networks, where a projection between the two 256-d ends would double the block's cost.

Derivation · why the bottleneck is cheap

Bottleneck at 256 channels: \(256\cdot64 + 9\cdot64\cdot64 + 64\cdot256 = 69{,}632\) weights. A basic block at 256 channels: \(2\cdot9\cdot256^2 = 1{,}179{,}648\), seventeen times more. A basic block at 64 channels: \(2\cdot9\cdot64^2 = 73{,}728\), about the same cost as the bottleneck. As a result ResNet-50 needs about 3.8 billion FLOPs (the paper counts multiply-adds), against 3.6 billion for ResNet-34, and even ResNet-152 at 11.3 billion stays below VGG-16 and VGG-19 at 15.3 and 19.6 billion.

The network: a 7×7 stride-2 stem (ZFNet's fix, Day 62) and max-pool, four stages at widths 64/128/256/512 (×4 at the bottleneck outputs) with stride-2 downsampling at the start of stages 3–5, then global average pooling (Day 63) and one FC layer. Blocks per stage: [3, 4, 6, 3] for ResNet-34 and -50, [3, 4, 23, 3] for 101, [3, 8, 36, 3] for 152. BN after every convolution, no dropout, He initialization.

import torch.nn as nn

class Bottleneck(nn.Module):
    """1x1 reduce -> 3x3 -> 1x1 expand, plus shortcut, then ReLU (ResNet v1)."""
    expansion = 4

    def __init__(self, c_in, width, stride=1):
        super().__init__()
        c_out = width * self.expansion
        self.f = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(c_in, width, 1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(width), nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            # stride on the 3x3 as in torchvision ("v1.5"); the paper strides the first 1x1
            nn.Conv2d(width, width, 3, stride, 1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(width), nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
            nn.Conv2d(width, c_out, 1, bias=False), nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out),
        )
        self.shortcut = nn.Identity()
        if stride != 1 or c_in != c_out:                  # option B: project only when shapes change
            self.shortcut = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(c_in, c_out, 1, stride, bias=False),
                                          nn.BatchNorm2d(c_out))
        self.relu = nn.ReLU(inplace=True)

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.relu(self.f(x) + self.shortcut(x))    # y = ReLU(F(x) + x)

Evidence

The reversal. On ImageNet, plain-34 has higher training and validation error than plain-18. With shortcuts and nothing else changed, ResNet-34 beats ResNet-18 and has lower training error. The paper's Table 2 reports 10-crop top-1 validation error of 27.94% vs 28.54% for plain-18 vs plain-34, and 27.88% vs 25.03% for ResNet-18 vs ResNet-34. At 18 layers the shortcut barely changes the final accuracy but makes convergence faster. Depth keeps paying: a single ResNet-152 reaches about 4.5% top-5 on validation, and an ensemble won ILSVRC 2015 with 3.57% top-5 test error. The same backbones won the ImageNet detection and localization tasks and the COCO detection and segmentation tasks, and the authors report a 28% relative improvement on COCO detection from swapping VGG-16 for ResNet-101 in Faster R-CNN (Day 84).

The 1202-layer net. On CIFAR-10 a 1202-layer ResNet trains without difficulty, to under 0.1% training error, but its test error (about 7.9%) is worse than the 110-layer network's (about 6.4%). The authors attribute this to overfitting a small dataset with 19.4M parameters and no dropout or maxout. Note the contrast: at 1202 layers the failure finally is overfitting, and the optimization problem is gone.

Another reading · an ensemble of shallow paths

Veit, Wilber & Belongie (2016) unroll \(n\) residual blocks into \(2^n\) paths of different lengths. Deleting a single block from a trained ResNet at test time barely hurts (except for the downsampling blocks), while deleting a layer from VGG is catastrophic, and accuracy degrades smoothly as more blocks are removed, like an ensemble losing members. They also find that most of the gradient during training flows through paths much shorter than the full depth. Tomorrow's derivation makes this view exact.

Limits & what broke
  • Degradation is a training problem. Explaining ResNet as "it prevents overfitting" gets the paper backwards. Explaining it as "it fixes vanishing gradients" is closer, and matches the gradient algebra, but the authors themselves argue that BN-equipped plain nets do not have vanishing gradients and leave the true cause as a conjecture.
  • The post-addition ReLU and BN placement were not optimal; pre-activation ResNets (Day 68) train 1000-layer networks this version cannot.
  • The headline numbers mix architecture with training. Later papers (He et al. 2019, "Bag of Tricks"; Wightman et al. 2021, "ResNet Strikes Back") push the same ResNet-50 several points higher with better recipes, a lesson ConvNeXt (Day 78) builds on.

Identity Mappings in Deep Residual Networks (pre-activation ResNet)

35m videoLevel 2

He et al., ECCV 2016 (arXiv:1603.05027)

If both the shortcut and the operation after the addition are pure identities, signal moves between any two residual blocks additively, forwards and backwards. Moving BatchNorm and ReLU in front of each convolution ("pre-activation") achieves exactly that, and lets a 1001-layer network train and generalize better than the original design.

Cold open

The abstract's headline: a 1001-layer ResNet reaching 4.62% error on CIFAR-10. With the original block from Day 67, a network that deep trains noticeably worse. The fix moves two operations and adds no parameters.

The problem

Day 67's block computes \(x_{l+1} = \mathrm{ReLU}(x_l + F(x_l))\). The ReLU after the addition means the shortcut is not really an identity path to the next block, and the "+1 in the gradient" argument holds only approximately. Meanwhile highway networks put gates on the shortcut, and Day 67's option C showed that 1×1 projections help slightly. Does anything on the shortcut help, and what is the cleanest path?

The method: the clean path, derived

Write a block generally as \(y_l = h(x_l) + F(x_l, W_l)\), \(x_{l+1} = f(y_l)\). Suppose both \(h\) and \(f\) are identities, so \(x_{l+1} = x_l + F(x_l, W_l)\).

Derivation · forward and backward through identity shortcuts

Apply the recursion repeatedly. For any deeper block \(L\) and shallower block \(l\):

$$x_L = x_l + \sum_{i=l}^{L-1} F(x_i, W_i).$$

Any deep feature is a shallow feature plus a sum of residuals, rather than a product of weight matrices as in a plain network. Differentiate with the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal E}{\partial x_l} = \frac{\partial \mathcal E}{\partial x_L}\,\frac{\partial x_L}{\partial x_l} = \frac{\partial \mathcal E}{\partial x_L}\Big(1 + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_l}\sum_{i=l}^{L-1} F(x_i, W_i)\Big).$$

The first term delivers the gradient from block \(L\) directly to block \(l\) without passing through any weight layer. For the total to vanish, the second term would have to equal \(-1\) for every sample in the minibatch, which is very unlikely. So the gradient cannot vanish even if the residual weights are tiny.

Now put a scale on the shortcut, \(h(x_i) = \lambda_i x_i\). The same steps give \(x_L = \big(\prod_{i=l}^{L-1}\lambda_i\big)\,x_l + \sum_i \hat F(x_i)\) (the residuals absorb the scales) and

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal E}{\partial x_l} = \frac{\partial \mathcal E}{\partial x_L}\Big(\prod_{i=l}^{L-1}\lambda_i + \frac{\partial}{\partial x_l}\sum_{i=l}^{L-1}\hat F(x_i, W_i)\Big).$$

With \(\lambda_i < 1\) the direct term decays exponentially with depth; with \(\lambda_i > 1\) it explodes. Gates and 1×1 convolutions do the same thing, with a product of Jacobians in place of \(\prod\lambda_i\). The sum form of \(x_L\) is also what makes yesterday's "ensemble of paths" view precise.

The method: five shortcut variants, then the activation

The shortcut ablation (ResNet-110 on CIFAR-10): constant scaling of the shortcut by 0.5; exclusive gating, \(g(x)\) on the residual and \(1-g(x)\) on the shortcut; shortcut-only gating; a 1×1 convolution on the shortcut; and dropout on the shortcut. Every variant has higher test error than the plain identity, some dramatically. The telling case is the 1×1 convolution: it can represent the identity and has strictly more capacity, yet it is worse, and its training error is higher too. The problem is optimization, not representational power.

Where BN and ReLU go. With the shortcut settled, \(f\) has to become an identity. The authors compare: BN after the addition (worse, since BN now alters the shortcut signal); ReLU moved before the addition (worse, since the residual becomes non-negative and the signal can only grow); ReLU-only pre-activation (about the same as the original); and full pre-activation, BN → ReLU → conv → BN → ReLU → conv, then add. The last is best. Seen across blocks, the activation after one addition has simply been moved to become the first operation of the next block's residual branch.

import torch.nn as nn

class PreActBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, c):
        super().__init__()
        self.f = nn.Sequential(nn.BatchNorm2d(c), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False),
                               nn.BatchNorm2d(c), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(c, c, 3, padding=1, bias=False))

    def forward(self, x):
        return x + self.f(x)          # nothing after the addition
# the full network needs one extra BN + ReLU after the last block, before pooling

Evidence

The authors identify two separate effects. Easier optimization: with the original block, the 1001-layer network's training loss falls slowly at the start of training, which they attribute to the post-addition ReLU truncating signal; the pre-activation version trains smoothly. At 164 layers the truncation matters less. Less overfitting: pre-activation ResNet-164 ends with slightly higher training loss but lower test error. Their explanation is that in the original block the BN-normalized residual is added to an unnormalized shortcut, so the next weight layer sees an unnormalized input, while with pre-activation every weight layer's input is normalized. On ImageNet, a pre-activation ResNet-200 beats the original-block ResNet-200.

Limits & what broke
  • "Any scaling hurts" is conditional. It was shown for this architecture with standard initialization. DeepNet (Wang et al. 2022) trains 1000-layer Transformers by up-scaling the shortcut before a post-addition LayerNorm, and Fixup, SkipInit and ReZero scale the residual branch down at initialization, even to zero, to train without normalization. That is the identity-initialization theme in another form.
  • The residual stream grows. In \(x_L = x_l + \sum F\) the magnitude of \(x\) tends to grow with depth, so late blocks change it proportionally less. Pre-LN Transformers inherit this and need a final normalization layer.
  • Small gains at moderate depth. For networks of tens of layers, pre-activation is roughly a wash. Its value appears at hundreds of layers.

Wide Residual Networks

30m videoLevel 2

Zagoruyko & Komodakis, BMVC 2016 (arXiv:1605.07146)

Making residual networks wider instead of deeper gives equal or better accuracy with a small fraction of the layers, and trains several times faster on a GPU, because width parallelizes and depth is sequential. Depth is one axis of a trade-off, not the only goal.

Cold open

A 28-layer network against a 1001-layer one. WRN-28-10 has about 36.5M parameters, more than three times the 10.2M of the pre-activation ResNet-1001 (Day 68), yet it reaches lower error on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 and trains much faster. The abstract goes further: even a 16-layer wide network outperforms all previous residual networks, thousand-layer ones included, in both accuracy and efficiency.

The problem

After ResNet, depth became the dial everyone turned: 152 layers on ImageNet, 1001 on CIFAR. Each extra fraction of a point cost roughly doubling the depth, and training got slow. The authors point to a problem they call diminishing feature reuse: nothing forces the gradient through a residual block's weights, so in a very deep, thin network many blocks may learn little, or contribute only a small amount each. Stochastic depth (Huang et al. 2016), which randomly drops whole blocks during training, is a symptom of the same worry. If most blocks contribute little, why have a thousand of them?

The method: a widening factor

Take the pre-activation CIFAR ResNet (BN → ReLU → conv) and multiply the channel count of every residual block by a factor \(k\). The network has a 16-channel stem conv and then three groups of \(N\) basic blocks at widths \(16k\), \(32k\), \(64k\), followed by average pooling and a linear layer. Depth counts conv layers: \(n = 6N + 4\). WRN-\(n\)-\(k\) names the depth and width, so WRN-28-10 has four blocks per group at widths 160, 320 and 640. The original thin ResNet is \(k = 1\).

The authors also try other block shapes, such as B(1,3,1) bottlenecks, B(3,1,3) and blocks with more convolutions, and conclude that the plain two-3×3 block is about as good as anything. Width, not block design, is the lever.

Derivation · why width is cheap on a GPU

A 3×3 convolution from \(C\) to \(C\) channels on an \(H\times W\) map costs \(9HWC^2\) multiply-adds. Widening by \(k\) multiplies that by \(k^2\) but keeps the number of layers, and so the number of sequential kernel launches, normalization passes and activation round-trips to memory, the same. Deepening multiplies the layer count. At \(C = 16\) on a 32×32 map, one layer is about 2.4M multiply-adds, far too little to fill a GPU, so the per-layer fixed costs dominate. At \(C = 160\) it is about 236M, and the arithmetic dominates. At equal FLOPs, a wide network therefore runs faster than a deep one. This is a hardware argument, not a learning-theory one, which is why it is underrated. "The hardware is part of the algorithm" returns with MobileNet and ShuffleNet (Days 73–75).

The method: dropout inside the block

Wide blocks have many parameters each, so the authors add dropout inside the residual branch, between the two convolutions: BN → ReLU → conv → dropout → BN → ReLU → conv. They use a rate of 0.3 on CIFAR. This works where earlier attempts did not. Dropout on the shortcut breaks the identity path, as Day 68's dropout-shortcut variant showed, and in BN-era networks dropout had largely been abandoned. Inside the branch it regularizes the block's weights while the identity path stays clean. The paper reports consistent gains from it on CIFAR and SVHN.

Evidence

Widening improves accuracy at every depth tested, from 16 to 40 layers, and wide networks with 16–28 layers match or beat thousand-layer thin ones on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. On computational efficiency, the authors time training and report that wide networks with accuracy comparable to ResNet-1001 run several times faster, with speed-ups of up to about 8× quoted. On ImageNet they widen the inner layers of bottleneck blocks and report that a wide 50-layer network (WRN-50-2) outperforms ResNet-152 with a third of the layers and faster training. Torchvision ships it as wide_resnet50_2. The parameter cost is real: growing both depth and width keeps helping only until the parameter count becomes so large that stronger regularization is needed.

Limits & what broke
  • More parameters, more FLOPs. WRN-28-10 is over three times larger than ResNet-1001 in parameters. "Faster" refers to GPU wall-clock time, not FLOPs, memory or model size, and the size of the speed-up depends on the GPU and batch size.
  • Mostly CIFAR. The strongest claims are at 32×32 resolution, where thin layers are especially inefficient. The ImageNet evidence is thinner.
  • Diminishing feature reuse is a hypothesis. The paper motivates width with it but does not measure it directly.
  • Neither extreme is optimal. The depth–width question did not end here: EfficientNet scales depth, width and resolution together (Day 76), and RegNet finds good depth saturating around 20 blocks (Day 77).

Densely Connected Convolutional Networks (DenseNet)

35m videoLevel 2

Huang, Liu, van der Maaten & Weinberger, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1608.06993)

DenseNet connects every layer in a block to every earlier layer by concatenation rather than addition, so features are reused explicitly. With very narrow layers this matches ResNet accuracy with far fewer parameters, and pays for it in activation memory.

Cold open

One equation per architecture, and they differ in a single symbol:

$$\text{ResNet: } x_\ell = H_\ell(x_{\ell-1}) + x_{\ell-1}\qquad\qquad \text{DenseNet: } x_\ell = H_\ell\big([x_0, x_1, \dots, x_{\ell-1}]\big).$$

Add versus concatenate is the single conceptual axis of this episode. The paper won the CVPR 2017 best paper award.

The problem

ResNets (Day 67), highway networks, stochastic depth and FractalNets share one trick: short paths from early layers to later ones. Stochastic depth also showed that many layers of a ResNet can be dropped at random during training, so many layers contribute little, which is the redundancy WRN (Day 69) answered with width. DenseNet answers it with connectivity. The authors argue that summation combines the identity and the block's output in a way that may impede information flow, and propose to keep every feature map separate and let every later layer read it.

The method: dense blocks

Inside a dense block, layer \(\ell\) receives the channel-wise concatenation of the block input and every earlier layer's output. An \(L\)-layer block therefore has \(L(L+1)/2\) direct connections, against \(L\) in a chain. Each \(H_\ell\) is BN → ReLU → 3×3 conv producing only \(k\) feature maps, where \(k\), the growth rate, is small (12 to 32 is typical, up to 40 in the largest CIFAR model). Layer \(\ell\) therefore sees \(k_0 + k(\ell-1)\) input channels. The authors' picture is a shared global state: each layer adds \(k\) maps to a collective memory that every later layer can read.

Derivation · what addition loses and concatenation keeps

Suppose the next layer is linear, with weights \(W\). After addition it computes \(W(x + F(x)) = Wx + WF(x)\): the same weights must serve both the identity and the new features. After concatenation it computes \([W_1\ W_2]\,[x;\,F(x)] = W_1x + W_2F(x)\), which is strictly more general, with addition the special case \(W_1 = W_2\). Addition is also many-to-one: from \(x + F(x)\) alone you generally cannot recover \(x\), while from \([x, F(x)]\) you trivially can. That is the precise sense in which concatenation preserves information that addition destroys.

The price is width. Layer \(\ell\) costs \(9HW\,(k_0 + k(\ell-1))\,k\) multiply-adds, so a block of \(L\) layers costs about \(9HW\,(k_0kL + k^2L^2/2)\): quadratic in depth. The small \(k\) is what keeps that affordable.

The method: bottlenecks, transitions, compression

  • DenseNet-B: each \(H_\ell\) becomes BN → ReLU → 1×1 conv to \(4k\) channels → BN → ReLU → 3×3 conv to \(k\). The 1×1 caps the cost of the growing input (Day 63).
  • Transition layers between blocks: BN, 1×1 conv, 2×2 average pooling.
  • Compression \(\theta\): if a block outputs \(m\) maps, the transition emits \(\lfloor\theta m\rfloor\); with \(\theta = 0.5\) this is DenseNet-C. Both together give DenseNet-BC.

The ImageNet models (DenseNet-121, -169, -201, -264) use four dense blocks with \(k = 32\) after a 7×7 stem; the CIFAR models use three blocks.

Evidence

Parameter efficiency is real. On CIFAR, the authors report a DenseNet-BC with about 0.8M parameters reaching accuracy similar to the 1001-layer pre-activation ResNet (Day 68), which has 10.2M. On ImageNet, DenseNet-201 with about 20M parameters is reported to match ResNet-101 with more than 40M. DenseNets also overfit less when trained without augmentation.

Feature reuse, measured. The heatmap figure trains a CIFAR DenseNet and plots, for each layer, the average absolute weight it assigns to each earlier layer's maps. Layers spread their weight over many inputs, so early features really are used deep in the block. Layers in the second and third blocks assign the least weight to the transition layer's outputs, which suggests those outputs are redundant and justifies compression. The final classifier concentrates on the last few layers, suggesting high-level features keep being produced late.

Limits & what broke
  • Memory is worse than ResNet's, not better. A naive implementation allocates a new concatenated tensor (and a new BN output) for every layer and keeps them all for the backward pass, so activation memory grows quadratically with block depth. Fewer parameters did not mean less memory.
  • The fix was to recompute. Pleiss et al. (2017) put the concatenations and BN outputs into shared buffers and recompute them during the backward pass, which makes feature-map memory linear in depth for a modest compute overhead. It is the "recompute rather than store" theme of automatic differentiation (Day 32), later central to FlashAttention (Day 153).
  • FLOPs and parameters are not speed. Many thin layers and concatenations make DenseNets slower on GPUs than ResNets of similar accuracy, which is one reason ResNet-style backbones stayed the default.
  • What lasted: concatenation as a way to fuse features, as in U-Net's skip connections (Day 92), and partial dense connectivity in later detector backbones.

Aggregated Residual Transformations (ResNeXt)

30m videoLevel 2

Xie, Girshick, Dollár, Tu & He, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1611.05431)

ResNeXt replaces the single wide transformation in a bottleneck block with a sum of many identical narrow ones. The number of paths, the cardinality, is a new design dimension that improves accuracy more than extra depth or width at the same cost, and the whole block turns out to be one grouped convolution.

Cold open

Figure 3 of the paper is the paper: three diagrams that look like three different architectures and compute exactly the same function. Put it on screen and walk each one. Once you see why they are equal, grouped convolution stops being an implementation detail and becomes a design axis.

The problem

Two design schools had emerged. VGG and ResNet (Day 64, Day 67) stack identical blocks: few hyperparameters, little risk of over-tuning to one dataset. Inception (Day 65, Day 66) uses split–transform–merge: cheap and accurate, but every module has hand-set filter counts and sizes, and it is unclear how to adapt them to a new dataset. ResNeXt combines the two: VGG-style repetition with Inception-style branching, where every branch has the same shape.

The method: aggregated transformations

The authors start from a single neuron: \(\sum_i w_i x_i\) splits the input into coordinates, transforms each by a scale, and aggregates by summing. Replace the scalar transform with a small network \(\mathcal T_i\):

$$y = x + \sum_{i=1}^{C} \mathcal T_i(x),$$

where every \(\mathcal T_i\) has the same topology (1×1 to \(d\) channels → 3×3 at \(d\) → 1×1 back to 256) and \(C\) is the cardinality. Two template rules from VGG/ResNet fix everything else: blocks that output the same spatial size share hyperparameters, and each time the map is downsampled by 2 the width doubles, which keeps the FLOPs per block roughly constant. ResNeXt-50 (32×4d) uses \(C = 32\) paths of width \(d = 4\).

Proof · why the three forms are equal

(a) ≡ (b): path \(i\) ends with \(W_i z_i\), where \(z_i \in \R^4\) is its 3×3 output and \(W_i \in \R^{256\times4}\). Then \(\sum_i W_i z_i = [W_1 \cdots W_{32}]\,[z_1; \dots; z_{32}]\): one 1×1 conv on the 128-channel concatenation. (b) ≡ (c): the 32 first-layer projections \(U_i \in \R^{4\times256}\) stack into one \(U \in \R^{128\times256}\), and 32 independent 3×3 convs, each on its own 4 channels, are by definition a grouped convolution with 32 groups. The equivalence is only interesting for blocks of depth ≥ 3. With two layers the block collapses into one wide dense layer.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
C, d = 32, 4
grouped = nn.Conv2d(C * d, C * d, 3, padding=1, groups=C, bias=False)   # form (c)
paths = [nn.Conv2d(d, d, 3, padding=1, bias=False) for _ in range(C)]   # form (b), path by path
for i, p in enumerate(paths):
    p.weight.data = grouped.weight.data[i * d:(i + 1) * d].clone()     # group i's slice

x = torch.randn(1, C * d, 8, 8)
y_paths = torch.cat([p(x[:, i * d:(i + 1) * d]) for i, p in enumerate(paths)], dim=1)
assert torch.allclose(grouped(x), y_paths, atol=1e-5)
Derivation · trading width for cardinality at fixed cost

One block with \(C\) paths of width \(d\) on 256-d input has \(C\,(256d + 9d^2 + 256d) = C\,(512d + 9d^2)\) weights. The ResNet-50 bottleneck (\(C = 1\), \(d = 64\)) has \(512\cdot64 + 9\cdot64^2 = 69{,}632\). Holding that fixed: \(C = 2, d = 40\) gives 69,760; \(C = 4, d = 24\) gives 69,888; \(C = 8, d = 14\) gives 71,456; \(C = 32, d = 4\) gives 70,144. These are the paper's settings, and ResNeXt-50 (32×4d) ends up with about the same parameters and FLOPs as ResNet-50.

Evidence

On ImageNet-1K at equal complexity, error falls steadily as cardinality rises. The paper reports top-1 validation error of 23.9% for ResNet-50 and 22.2% for ResNeXt-50 (32×4d), with the intermediate settings in between, and 22.0% vs 21.2% at 101 layers. The gain is not regularization: ResNeXt's training error is also lower, so the representation is stronger. The cardinality-versus-width table also shows saturation once the width per group gets very small. When the budget is doubled, spending it on cardinality beats spending it on depth (ResNet-200) or width. A ResNeXt-based entry placed second in ILSVRC 2016 classification, and the backbone gives consistent gains for COCO detection.

Limits & what broke
  • Equal FLOPs, unequal speed. Grouped convolutions have lower arithmetic intensity and were poorly optimized in GPU libraries at the time, so ResNeXt runs slower than ResNet at the same FLOPs. ShuffleNetV2 later quantifies why heavy grouping raises memory-access cost (Day 75).
  • Cardinality saturates. The gains flatten as \(d\) shrinks, and the paper does not explain why 32 is a sweet spot.
  • Superseded, but absorbed. RegNet's search (Day 77) finds a shared group width across stages to be a good design rule, and ConvNeXt (Day 78) takes grouping to its limit with depthwise convolution.

Squeeze-and-Excitation Networks

30m videoLevel 2

Hu, Shen & Sun, CVPR 2018 (arXiv:1709.01507)

A Squeeze-and-Excitation block pools each channel over the whole image, passes the result through a tiny two-layer bottleneck, and uses the output as a per-channel gate. It is attention with a global receptive field inserted into a local architecture, costs well under 1% extra compute, and drops into any backbone.

Cold open

ILSVRC 2017, the last edition of the classification challenge: the winning entry, built on SE blocks, reached a top-5 error of about 2.25%, which the authors describe as roughly a 25% relative improvement over the 2016 winner. The block responsible adds two small fully connected layers per residual block.

The problem

A convolution mixes channels, but only inside its local window. Relationships between channels are learned implicitly and locally, and global context reaches a unit only near the top of the network, where the receptive field finally covers the image. Prior architecture work had improved the spatial side: multi-scale branches (Day 65), shortcuts (Day 67), cardinality (Day 71). SE asks for something different: let the network explicitly reweight its channels, using information from the whole image, at every stage.

The method: squeeze, excite, scale

Take the output \(U \in \R^{H\times W\times C}\) of any transformation (a residual branch, an Inception module).

Algorithm · SE block
  1. Squeeze: global average pooling (Day 63), \(z_c = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i=1}^{H}\sum_{j=1}^{W} u_c(i,j)\), one number per channel that summarizes the whole image.
  2. Excitation: \(s = \sigma\big(W_2\,\delta(W_1 z)\big)\), with \(W_1 \in \R^{\frac{C}{r}\times C}\), \(\delta\) = ReLU, \(W_2 \in \R^{C\times\frac{C}{r}}\), \(\sigma\) = sigmoid. The reduction ratio \(r\) defaults to 16.
  3. Scale: \(\tilde u_c = s_c\, u_c\). Each channel is multiplied by its gate.

The authors list two requirements for the excitation: it must be flexible enough to model nonlinear interactions between channels, and non-mutually-exclusive, so that several channels can be emphasized at once. That is why the gate is a sigmoid and not a softmax. A softmax would force the gates to sum to 1, so the channels would compete and the average gate would be \(1/C\), shrinking every feature. With sigmoids each gate is independent. The authors' ablation finds sigmoid clearly better than ReLU or tanh in that position, and average pooling slightly better than max pooling for the squeeze.

Derivation · what SE costs in ResNet-50

Each SE block adds \(2C^2/r\) weights (ignoring biases), so over stages with \(N_s\) blocks of output width \(C_s\) the total is \(\frac{2}{r}\sum_s N_s C_s^2\). For ResNet-50 with \(r = 16\):

$$\tfrac{2}{16}\big(3\cdot256^2 + 4\cdot512^2 + 6\cdot1024^2 + 3\cdot2048^2\big) \approx 0.02 + 0.13 + 0.79 + 1.57 = 2.5\text{M},$$

about 10% on top of ResNet-50's ~25M, matching the paper's figure, with almost two thirds in the last stage. The compute is tiny: a pooling pass and two matrix–vector products per block. The paper reports about 3.87 GFLOPs against 3.86 for ResNet-50, a 0.26% relative increase.

Evidence

The paper reports a single-crop top-5 validation error of 6.62% for SE-ResNet-50, against 7.48% for ResNet-50, approaching ResNet-101's 6.52% at about half its compute. The gains hold across ResNet, ResNeXt, Inception-ResNet, MobileNet and ShuffleNet, and transfer to scene classification and to COCO detection. Ablations show the method is robust to the reduction ratio over a wide range, with 16 a good balance of accuracy and parameters. Dropping the SE blocks from the last stage removes most of the extra parameters at a small accuracy cost.

The excitation analysis shows what the gates do. In early layers the gate distributions look much the same across classes, consistent with class-agnostic low-level features. Deeper, they become increasingly class-specific. In the final stage many gates sit close to 1, so those SE blocks do little, which is why removing them is cheap.

Limits & what broke
  • FLOPs undersell the latency. Global pooling forces the whole feature map to be computed before any channel can be scaled, which breaks layer fusion and pipelining. The authors themselves report a wall-clock overhead larger than the FLOP count suggests.
  • Channels only. SE can say "edges matter more here" but not "look at this region". CBAM (Woo et al. 2018) adds a spatial attention map after the channel gate, using both average- and max-pooled descriptors.
  • The squeeze is crude. One mean per channel discards everything about spatial distribution. Later variants replace the two FC layers with cheaper operations (for example, ECA-Net's 1D convolution across channels), which suggests the bottleneck MLP is not essential.

Bridge

Say it this way and the Transformer chapters land harder: an SE gate is a function of the entire image, available at every layer, but it reweights channels; self-attention (Day 129) reweights positions, using content-dependent pairwise weights. Because it is a drop-in block, SE turns up everywhere, including inside MobileNetV3 and EfficientNet's MBConv blocks (Day 74, Day 76). Tomorrow: building CNNs small enough to run on a phone.

MobileNets: Efficient CNNs for Mobile Vision

35m videoLevel 2

Howard et al., 2017 (arXiv:1704.04861)

MobileNets split every standard convolution into a depthwise convolution (one spatial filter per channel) and a 1×1 pointwise convolution (channel mixing). For 3×3 kernels that cuts compute by 8–9× at a small accuracy cost, and two multipliers, for width and for resolution, let you dial size against accuracy.

Cold open

The paper's comparison table: the baseline MobileNet reaches about 70.6% ImageNet top-1 with roughly 569 million multiply-adds and 4.2M parameters, while VGG-16 (Day 64) reaches 71.5% with about 15.3 billion multiply-adds and 138M parameters. That is about a point of accuracy for 27× less compute and 33× fewer parameters: deep learning on a phone.

The problem

Phones, robots and cars need low latency, small memory and low energy, and standard backbones need billions of operations per image. Earlier work mostly compressed a big trained network (pruning, quantization, hashing, distillation) or designed small networks with size, not speed, as the target. MobileNet's angle is a simple, regular family built around one cheap operation, plus two hyperparameters an engineer can turn without redesigning anything.

The method: depthwise separable convolution

A standard convolution with an \(D_K\times D_K\) kernel, \(M\) input and \(N\) output channels, on a \(D_F\times D_F\) output map, filters spatially and mixes channels in one step. Factor it into two steps: a depthwise convolution that applies one \(D_K\times D_K\) filter to each input channel separately, and a pointwise 1×1 convolution (Day 63) that mixes the \(M\) channels into \(N\). The depthwise step is a grouped convolution with groups equal to channels, the limit case of ResNeXt's cardinality (Day 71).

Derivation · the cost ratio

A standard layer applies \(N\) filters of size \(D_K\times D_K\times M\) at each of \(D_F^2\) positions: \(D_K^2 M N D_F^2\) multiply-adds. The depthwise step applies \(M\) filters of size \(D_K\times D_K\): \(D_K^2 M D_F^2\). The pointwise step applies \(N\) filters of size \(1\times1\times M\): \(MND_F^2\). Divide:

$$\frac{D_K^2 M D_F^2 + M N D_F^2}{D_K^2 M N D_F^2} = \frac{1}{N} + \frac{1}{D_K^2}.$$

For \(D_K = 3\) the second term is \(1/9\) and dominates once \(N\) is moderately large: \(N = 64\) gives 0.127 (7.9× cheaper), \(N = 256\) gives 0.115 (8.7×), and \(N = 1024\) gives 0.112 (8.9×). Hence the paper's "8 to 9 times less computation". The parameter ratio is the same expression. One consequence: the 1×1 layers now carry almost all the cost.

The network. A full 3×3 convolution at the input, then 13 depthwise-separable layers, with BN and ReLU after both the depthwise and the pointwise convolution. Downsampling uses stride-2 depthwise layers. Global average pooling and one FC layer finish it. The paper's Table 2 shows where the budget goes: the 1×1 convolutions account for about 95% of the multiply-adds and 75% of the parameters, with most of the remaining parameters in the FC layer. That suits hardware, because a 1×1 convolution is a plain matrix multiply (GEMM) that needs no im2col reordering. Training uses less regularization and augmentation than for large models, since small models overfit less, and little or no weight decay on the depthwise filters, which have very few parameters.

The method: two dials

The width multiplier \(\alpha\) thins every layer, \(M \to \alpha M\) and \(N \to \alpha N\). The resolution multiplier \(\rho\) shrinks the input, so every map shrinks by \(\rho\) on each side. One layer then costs

$$D_K^2\,\alpha M\,\rho^2 D_F^2 + \alpha M\,\alpha N\,\rho^2 D_F^2,$$

so compute falls roughly as \(\alpha^2\) and exactly as \(\rho^2\). The paper uses \(\alpha \in \{1, 0.75, 0.5, 0.25\}\) and input sizes 224, 192, 160 and 128.

Evidence

Separable against full convolutions in the same architecture: about 70.6% against 71.7% top-1, for roughly one-eighth of the multiply-adds and one-seventh of the parameters. At equal compute, the paper reports that making the network thinner is about 3% better than making it shallower. Accuracy falls smoothly as \(\alpha\) and \(\rho\) shrink, roughly log-linearly in multiply-adds, with a sharper drop at \(\alpha = 0.25\). The paper also shows MobileNet working as a backbone for COCO detection with SSD and Faster R-CNN (Day 86, Day 84), for fine-grained recognition, for geolocation, and for face attributes via distillation.

    with torch.no_grad():
        for _ in range(3):
            module(x)                                    # warm-up
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        for _ in range(reps):

                          nn.Conv2d(M, N, 1, bias=False))                          # pointwise

Limits & what broke
  • FLOPs ≠ latency. This is the most useful practical caveat in the episode. Count operations per byte moved. A 1×1 conv with \(M = N\) channels does about \(M/2\) multiply-adds per value read or written; a 3×3 depthwise conv does about \(9/2\), whatever the width. At 256 channels that is roughly 30× less arithmetic per byte, so depthwise layers are memory-bandwidth-bound: they take a few percent of the FLOPs and a much larger share of the run time, especially on GPUs. Run the snippet above and the measured speed-up will usually fall well short of 8.7×.
  • ReLU everywhere, including on thin layers, loses information. MobileNetV2 removes it from the narrow layers tomorrow.
  • Quantization. Depthwise filters vary a lot in range from channel to channel, so per-tensor 8-bit quantization of MobileNet loses noticeable accuracy; per-channel scales became the standard fix.

MobileNetV2: Inverted Residuals and Linear Bottlenecks

30m videoLevel 2

Sandler et al., CVPR 2018 (arXiv:1801.04381)

MobileNetV2's block expands a thin input with a 1×1 convolution, filters it with a depthwise 3×3, and projects it back to thin with a linear 1×1, and the shortcut joins the thin ends. Removing the ReLU from the thin output avoids destroying information, and keeping only thin tensors alive between blocks lowers peak memory, the real deployment constraint.

Cold open

The persuasive figure is a toy. Take a 2-D spiral, embed it in \(n\) dimensions with a random matrix \(T\), apply a ReLU, and map it back. For \(n = 2\) or 3 the spiral comes back collapsed, with whole arms flattened onto lines. For \(n = 15\) or 30 it comes back nearly intact. ReLU destroys information in low dimensions and is nearly harmless in high ones. Every design choice in the paper follows from that.

The problem

MobileNetV1 (Day 73) puts a ReLU after every layer, including the pointwise layers that the width multiplier makes very thin, and it has no residual connections. The authors argue that the information a layer needs to carry, its "manifold of interest", often lies in a low-dimensional subspace, so thin layers are fine in principle, provided you do not apply a nonlinearity that throws part of that subspace away.

The method: linear bottlenecks

The paper states two properties. First, if the manifold of interest keeps non-zero volume after a ReLU, then on it the ReLU acted as a linear map. Second, a ReLU can preserve complete information about its input manifold only if that manifold lies in a low-dimensional subspace of the input space.

Why ReLU needs room

Embed a \(d\)-dimensional signal \(x\) as \(Tx\) in \(n\) dimensions and apply \(\mathrm{ReLU}\). For a given \(x\) only the coordinates with \((Tx)_i > 0\) survive, so the output is \(T_{A(x)}\,x\), where \(T_{A(x)}\) keeps the active rows of \(T\). You can recover \(x\) linearly only if those active rows have rank \(d\). With \(n\) close to \(d\), many inputs leave fewer than \(d\) coordinates active, and those points are irrecoverably collapsed. With \(n \gg d\), almost every input keeps far more than \(d\) active rows, and the information survives. Conclusion: apply the nonlinearity in the expanded space, and make the projection back to the bottleneck linear.

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.linspace(0.5, 4 * np.pi, 400)
X = np.stack([t * np.cos(t), t * np.sin(t)], axis=1) / (4 * np.pi)   # 2-D spiral, radius up to 1

for n in [2, 3, 5, 15, 30]:
    T = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))                   # random embedding into n dimensions
    H = np.maximum(X @ T.T, 0)                    # ReLU in the n-dimensional space
    W, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(H, X, rcond=None)     # best linear map back to 2-D
    err = np.linalg.norm(H @ W - X, axis=1).mean()
    print(f"n={n:2d}  mean reconstruction error {err:.3f}")
# seed 0: about 0.2-0.3 for n = 2, 3, 5; essentially 0 for n = 15 and 30

The paper maps back with the pseudo-inverse of \(T\); fitting the best linear decoder, as here, makes the trend easier to read as a number.

The method: inverted residuals

A ResNet bottleneck (Day 67) goes wide → narrow → wide and puts the shortcut on the wide ends. MobileNetV2 inverts it. Given a thin \(h\times w\times k\) input: a 1×1 convolution expands it to \(tk\) channels with ReLU6, a 3×3 depthwise convolution (stride \(s\)) filters it with ReLU6, and a linear 1×1 convolution projects it to \(k'\) channels. The shortcut is used when \(s = 1\) and \(k = k'\). The expansion factor is \(t = 6\) in every block except the first. ReLU6, \(\min(\max(x, 0), 6)\), is used for robustness in low-precision arithmetic.

The block costs \(hw\,tk\,(k + 9 + k')\) multiply-adds: \(hw\,k\,tk\) to expand, \(9\,hw\,tk\) for the depthwise filter, and \(hw\,tk\,k'\) to project. The authors' interpretation is that the bottlenecks carry the information and the expansion is an implementation detail of the nonlinear transformation, which separates the network's capacity (the bottleneck width) from its expressiveness (the expansion).

The method: memory-efficient inference

At inference, what limits a phone or microcontroller is often not FLOPs but peak memory: the largest set of tensors that must exist at once. Between blocks only the thin bottleneck tensors are alive. Inside a block, the depthwise layer works channel by channel, so the expanded tensor can be computed in \(t\) chunks: expand a slice of channels, filter it, project it, and accumulate into the output, without ever materializing the full \(tk\)-channel tensor. The authors' comparison of the largest tensors each architecture must hold shows MobileNetV2 well below MobileNetV1 and ShuffleNet. That memory argument, more than the FLOP count, is why the block was so widely deployed.

Evidence

On ImageNet the paper reports 72.0% top-1 at about 300M multiply-adds and 3.4M parameters, against MobileNetV1's 70.6% at about 575M and 4.2M, with lower measured latency on a phone CPU. Two ablations support the design: a linear bottleneck beats one with ReLU6, and shortcuts between the bottlenecks beat shortcuts between the expansion layers. For detection, SSDLite (SSD, Day 86, with separable convolutions in the prediction heads) on a MobileNetV2 backbone reaches accuracy comparable to much larger real-time detectors at a fraction of the compute. A mobile DeepLabv3 variant does the same for segmentation (Day 93).

Limits & what broke
  • The manifold argument is intuition, not a theorem about trained networks. The spiral uses a random \(T\). The real evidence is the linear-vs-ReLU6 ablation.
  • The memory saving needs runtime support. Chunked evaluation of the expansion only helps if the inference engine implements it; a naive framework still allocates the full expanded tensor.
  • The expanded 1×1 layers dominate compute again, and the depthwise layers stay memory-bound (Day 73).
  • MobileNetV3 (Howard et al. 2019), in one minute: platform-aware architecture search over these blocks, then NetAdapt to trim per-layer widths, SE modules (Day 72) inside the blocks, the cheap h-swish activation \(x\cdot\mathrm{ReLU6}(x+3)/6\), and a redesigned, cheaper final stage. The search optimizes measured phone latency, not FLOPs.

ShuffleNet: An Extremely Efficient CNN for Mobile Devices

25m videoLevel 2

Zhang, Zhou, Lin & Sun, CVPR 2018 (arXiv:1707.01083)

ShuffleNet makes the expensive 1×1 convolutions cheap by grouping them, and repairs the side effect, groups that never exchange information, with a channel shuffle that has no parameters and costs almost nothing. Its successor's guidelines teach the bigger lesson: memory access and parallelism, not FLOPs, decide speed.

Cold open

Stack two grouped convolutions and something quietly breaks: every output channel depends only on the input channels of its own group. The network becomes several thin networks running side by side that never talk to each other. The fix is a reshape, a transpose and another reshape.

The problem

In MobileNet (Day 73) and in ResNeXt (Day 71) the 1×1 convolutions carry almost all the cost. The authors note that in ResNeXt with cardinality 32 the pointwise layers take about 93% of the multiply-adds. In very small networks, with budgets of tens of MFLOPs, that cost caps how wide the feature maps can be. Grouping the 1×1 convolutions divides their cost by the group count \(g\), but it creates the isolation problem.

The method: channel shuffle

If a grouped convolution outputs \(g\times n\) channels (\(g\) groups of \(n\)), reshape the channel axis to \((g, n)\), transpose it to \((n, g)\), and flatten. Each consecutive block of channels now contains one channel from every original group. It is a fixed permutation, so it has no parameters, and its gradient is just the inverse permutation, so it trains end to end like any other layer.

import numpy as np

def channel_shuffle(x, groups):
    """x: (N, C, H, W) with C = groups * n. Reshape, transpose, flatten."""
    N, C, H, W = x.shape
    x = x.reshape(N, groups, C // groups, H, W)
    x = x.transpose(0, 2, 1, 3, 4)                 # (N, n, groups, H, W)
    return x.reshape(N, C, H, W)

x = np.arange(9).reshape(1, 9, 1, 1)               # channels 0..8 = three groups of three
print(channel_shuffle(x, 3).ravel())               # [0 3 6 1 4 7 2 5 8]

The same three calls work on PyTorch tensors (use permute for the transpose). The ShuffleNet unit starts from a ResNet bottleneck (Day 67): 1×1 group conv → BN, ReLU → channel shuffle → 3×3 depthwise conv → BN → 1×1 group conv → BN → add the shortcut → ReLU. For downsampling, the depthwise conv has stride 2, the shortcut becomes a 3×3 average pool with stride 2, and the two branches are concatenated instead of added, which widens the output at almost no cost.

Derivation · why grouping buys width

For an \(h\times w\times c\) input and bottleneck width \(m\), count multiply-adds for the two 1×1 layers and the 3×3 layer:

$$\text{ResNet: } hw\,(2cm + 9m^2),\qquad \text{ResNeXt: } hw\,(2cm + 9m^2/g),\qquad \text{ShuffleNet: } hw\,(2cm/g + 9m).$$

Only ShuffleNet divides the dominant \(2cm\) term by \(g\). At a fixed budget it can therefore afford feature maps about \(g\) times wider, which is what helps very small networks, whose accuracy is limited by width.

Evidence

With the architecture fixed, adding the shuffle consistently lowers ImageNet error, and the gain is larger for larger \(g\), where isolation is worse. Smaller networks benefit most from more groups. Against MobileNet at matched complexity, the abstract reports an absolute top-1 error reduction of 7.8% at a budget of 40 MFLOPs, and roughly a 13× actual speed-up over AlexNet on an ARM phone at comparable accuracy. The authors also note that in their implementation a 4× reduction in theoretical complexity typically gives only about a 2.6× real speed-up, because of memory access and other overheads. That remark sets up the sequel.

The method, revised: ShuffleNetV2's four guidelines

Ma, Zhang, Zheng & Sun (ECCV 2018) argue that FLOPs are an indirect metric, and that networks should be judged by measured speed on the target platform. FLOPs miss memory access cost (MAC) and the degree of parallelism. From benchmarks on a GPU and an ARM CPU they derive four guidelines, which are more useful than the original paper:

  1. G1: equal channel widths minimize memory access cost.
  2. G2: excessive group convolution increases MAC.
  3. G3: network fragmentation (many small parallel branches) reduces parallelism.
  4. G4: element-wise operations (ReLU, tensor addition, bias) are not negligible: few FLOPs, high MAC.
Derivation · G1 and G2

A 1×1 conv from \(c_1\) to \(c_2\) channels on \(h\times w\) does \(B = hwc_1c_2\) multiply-adds and touches \(\mathrm{MAC} = hw(c_1 + c_2) + c_1c_2\) values (input, output, weights). Since \(c_1 + c_2 \ge 2\sqrt{c_1c_2}\),

$$\mathrm{MAC} \ge 2hw\sqrt{c_1c_2} + c_1c_2 = 2\sqrt{hwB} + \frac{B}{hw},$$

with equality exactly when \(c_1 = c_2\) (G1). With \(g\) groups, \(B = hwc_1c_2/g\) and \(\mathrm{MAC} = hw(c_1 + c_2) + c_1c_2/g = hwc_1 + \frac{Bg}{c_1} + \frac{B}{hw}\). For fixed \(B\) and \(c_1\), memory traffic grows linearly with \(g\) (G2).

The V2 unit follows the guidelines directly. It splits the channels into two halves, passes one through unchanged, and runs the other through a 1×1 conv, a 3×3 depthwise conv and another 1×1 conv, all with equal input and output widths and no grouping (G1, G2). The halves are concatenated rather than added (G4), and the concatenation is followed by a channel shuffle. V1's grouped pointwise convolutions violate G2, and V2 drops them.

Limits & what broke
  • V1 broke its own successor's rules. Grouped 1×1 convolutions save FLOPs and spend memory traffic, and the shuffle is a real memory copy unless the runtime fuses it.
  • The guidelines are empirical and platform-specific, measured on two devices of 2018. They are reasons to measure, not substitutes for measuring.
  • FLOP-matched comparisons, the currency of most efficiency papers this month, systematically favour grouped and depthwise designs over what actually runs fastest. The lesson is to measure latency on the target device.

EfficientNet: Rethinking Model Scaling

40m videoLevel 2

Tan & Le, ICML 2019 (arXiv:1905.11946)

EfficientNet scales depth, width and input resolution together with fixed ratios, \(d = \alpha^\phi\), \(w = \beta^\phi\), \(r = \gamma^\phi\) with \(\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2 \approx 2\), so that each step of \(\phi\) roughly doubles the FLOPs. Applied to a small searched baseline, this one-coefficient rule produced a family that dominated the ImageNet accuracy-per-parameter frontier.

Cold open

The abstract: EfficientNet-B7 reaches 84.3% ImageNet top-1, while being 8.4× smaller and 6.1× faster at inference than the best existing ConvNet of the time (GPipe). The whole family B0–B7 comes from one small network and one scaling rule.

The problem

Scaling up was ad hoc. ResNets grew in depth (Day 67), Wide ResNets in width (Day 69), and large models were trained at higher input resolution, usually one dimension at a time, tuned by hand. The paper makes two observations. First, scaling any single dimension improves accuracy, but the gain saturates as the model grows. Second, the dimensions interact: a higher-resolution input needs more layers to cover it with a larger receptive field, and more channels to capture its finer patterns. So they should be scaled together.

The method: compound scaling

The method has two steps.

Algorithm · compound scaling
  1. Fix \(\phi = 1\), meaning twice the baseline's resources, and run a small grid search for \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma \ge 1\) subject to \(\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2 \approx 2\). For B0 the result is \(\alpha = 1.2\), \(\beta = 1.1\), \(\gamma = 1.15\).
  2. Freeze \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) and scale the baseline with larger \(\phi\): depth multiplier \(\alpha^\phi\), width multiplier \(\beta^\phi\), resolution multiplier \(\gamma^\phi\). This gives B1 to B7.

That is the actual method: the search is done once, at the smallest scale, and extrapolated. The authors note that searching \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) directly on a large model might do better, but is too expensive.

Derivation · why the constraint keeps FLOPs near \(2^\phi\)

A conv layer's cost is \(H W C_\text{in} C_\text{out} k^2\). Multiplying depth by \(d\) repeats layers, so cost scales by \(d\). Multiplying width by \(w\) scales both \(C_\text{in}\) and \(C_\text{out}\), so cost scales by \(w^2\). Multiplying resolution by \(r\) scales both \(H\) and \(W\): \(r^2\). Total:

$$\text{FLOPs} \propto d\,w^2\,r^2 = \alpha^\phi\,\beta^{2\phi}\,\gamma^{2\phi} = (\alpha\beta^2\gamma^2)^\phi \approx 2^\phi.$$

For the B0 values, \(1.2 \times 1.1^2 \times 1.15^2 = 1.2 \times 1.21 \times 1.3225 \approx 1.92\). Squaring in the constraint is the whole point: width and resolution are quadratically expensive, depth only linearly.

alpha, beta, gamma = 1.2, 1.1, 1.15               # grid search at phi = 1
print("alpha*beta^2*gamma^2 =", round(alpha * beta**2 * gamma**2, 3))   # 1.92
for phi in range(8):
    d, w, r = alpha**phi, beta**phi, gamma**phi
    print(f"phi={phi}  depth x{d:.2f}  width x{w:.2f}  input {224 * r:3.0f}px  FLOPs x{d * w**2 * r**2:5.1f}")
# phi=7: depth x3.58, width x1.95, ~596 px, FLOPs x96

B0 is about 0.39 billion FLOPs and B7 about 37 billion, a ratio close to the \(1.92^7 \approx 96\) the rule predicts. The released B1–B7 use rounded coefficients that follow the formula only approximately (B7 runs at 600×600).

The method: the baseline

Compound scaling only redistributes resources, so the baseline matters. EfficientNet-B0 comes from a multi-objective architecture search in the MnasNet search space, maximizing \(\text{ACC}(m)\times[\text{FLOPS}(m)/T]^{w}\) with \(T = 400\)M and \(w = -0.07\). It optimizes FLOPs rather than latency because it targets no particular device. Its main block is MobileNetV2's inverted bottleneck (Day 74) with a squeeze-and-excitation module (Day 72), called MBConv. B0 has about 5.3M parameters and 0.39 billion FLOPs.

Evidence

The headline figure plots ImageNet accuracy against parameters: B0–B7 lie above every earlier network at every size, and B7 reaches 84.3% with about 66M parameters and 37 billion FLOPs. The cleaner experiment applies compound scaling to existing networks: scaled-up MobileNets and ResNets do better than the same networks scaled along a single dimension to the same FLOPs. Class activation maps (Day 63) of compound-scaled models focus on more relevant regions with more object detail than single-dimension ones. The models also transfer well, with the abstract reporting state-of-the-art results on CIFAR-100, Flowers and three other transfer datasets with far fewer parameters.

Limits & what broke
  • "Fewer FLOPs, slower in practice" is a legitimate critique. Depthwise convolutions are memory-bound (Day 73), SE blocks serialize computation, and high resolution inflates activation memory at every layer. Training is slow and needs small batches, and the efficiency is measured in FLOPs and parameters, not wall-clock time.
  • EfficientNetV2 concedes it. Tan & Le (2021) identify three problems: training with very large images is slow; depthwise convolutions are slow in early layers, so they replace them there with Fused-MBConv (one regular 3×3 instead of 1×1 expansion plus depthwise 3×3); and scaling every stage equally is sub-optimal. They add training-aware search and progressive learning, which grows the image size during training and strengthens regularization along with it.
  • Resolution is the expensive dial. It contributes substantially to accuracy, and it drives most of the memory cost, because activations grow as \(r^2\) at every layer. Bello et al. (2021, "Revisiting ResNets") recommend increasing resolution more slowly than EfficientNet does, and scaling depth when overfitting is possible and width otherwise. With a modern training recipe and that scaling strategy, their ResNets match EfficientNets on the speed–accuracy curve: tune the baseline before you believe the delta.
  • The ratios are extrapolated. \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) are found at \(\phi = 1\) and assumed optimal at \(\phi = 7\), which is not tested.

Designing Network Design Spaces (RegNet)

35m videoLevel 3

Radosavovic, Kosaraju, Girshick, He & Dollár, CVPR 2020 (arXiv:2003.13678)

RegNet designs design spaces instead of single networks: sample and train hundreds of models from a space, compare spaces by their error distributions, and simplify the space one constraint at a time. The space it ends with, where block widths grow roughly linearly with depth, is described by a handful of human-readable rules.

Cold open

Neural architecture search answers "what is the best network?" with one point, found at great cost, that comes with no explanation. This paper asks a different question: which rules make populations of networks good? It starts from a space with on the order of \(10^{18}\) configurations and ends with a few rules, such as a shared bottleneck ratio, a shared group width, and widths and depths that increase from stage to stage, each of which can be checked and reused.

The problem

Hand design (Days 64–71) produced insight slowly, one network at a time. NAS, including the search behind EfficientNet's baseline (Day 76), produced strong single networks tuned to one setting, with little insight into why they work or whether they transfer to other compute regimes. The authors want the strength of both: automated, data-driven exploration whose output is interpretable principles.

The method: the error EDF

The analysis tool is unfamiliar but excellent. Sample \(n\) models from a design space (the paper typically uses \(n = 500\)), train each in a cheap regime (a fixed small FLOP budget, 10 epochs on ImageNet), and record their errors \(e_i\). The empirical distribution function of errors is

$$F(e) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb 1[e_i < e],$$

the fraction of models in the space with error below \(e\). Plot \(F\) for two spaces on the same axes. A curve that lies higher and further left belongs to the better space: at any error threshold, a larger fraction of its models beat it.

import numpy as np

def edf(errors, grid):
    """Fraction of sampled models whose error is below each value in grid."""
    errors = np.sort(np.asarray(errors))
    return np.searchsorted(errors, grid, side="left") / len(errors)

The method: AnyNet → RegNet, one constraint at a time

AnyNet fixes a stem (a stride-2 3×3 conv), a head (pooling and FC) and a body of four stages. Each stage \(i\) has a depth \(d_i\), width \(w_i\), bottleneck ratio \(b_i\) and group width \(g_i\), and is built from "X blocks": ResNeXt-style residual bottlenecks with group convolution (Day 71). That is 16 degrees of freedom. The simplification then adds one constraint at a time and checks that the EDF does not get worse:

  1. Shared bottleneck ratio, \(b_i = b\): EDF essentially unchanged.
  2. Shared group width, \(g_i = g\): unchanged again, with the space orders of magnitude smaller.
  3. Increasing widths, \(w_{i+1} \ge w_i\): the EDF improves noticeably.
  4. Increasing depths, \(d_{i+1} \ge d_i\): improves again.

Looking at the best models that remain, the authors find that per-block width grows roughly linearly with block index. That becomes the RegNet parameterization.

Derivation · the quantized linear width rule

For blocks \(0 \le j < d\), start from linear widths \(u_j = w_0 + w_a\cdot j\) with \(w_0 > 0\), \(w_a > 0\). Stages need piecewise-constant widths, so pick a multiplier \(w_m > 0\), solve \(u_j = w_0\, w_m^{s_j}\) for \(s_j\), round it, and set

$$w_j = w_0\, w_m^{\lfloor s_j \rceil},\qquad s_j = \log_{w_m}\!\big(u_j / w_0\big),$$

then round widths to multiples of 8. Consecutive blocks with the same \(w_j\) form a stage. A whole network is now six numbers: \(d, w_0, w_a, w_m\) plus the shared \(b\) and \(g\).

Example: \(w_0 = 48\), \(w_a = 36\), \(w_m = 2.5\), \(d = 13\). The linear widths run 48, 84, …, 480. Rounding in log base 2.5 gives exponents 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, … , 2, 3, so the stages have widths 48, 120, 304 and 752 with depths 1, 3, 8 and 1.

def regnet_widths(w0, wa, wm, d, q=8):
    u = w0 + wa * np.arange(d)                       # linear per-block widths
    s = np.round(np.log(u / w0) / np.log(wm))        # quantize in log space
    return (np.round(w0 * wm ** s / q) * q).astype(int)

print(regnet_widths(48, 36, 2.5, 13))   # [ 48 120 120 120 304 304 304 304 304 304 304 304 752]

Evidence: the rules, and how they compare

The discovered rules are the point, because they are human-readable:

  • Good depth saturates around 20 blocks (about 60 layers), and stays there across compute regimes, against the habit of going deeper for bigger budgets.
  • The best models use a bottleneck ratio of 1, which removes the bottleneck, and a width multiplier \(w_m\) around 2.5, close to the familiar "double the width each stage".
  • Group width, \(w_0\) and \(w_a\) grow with compute.
  • Inverted bottlenecks and depthwise convolution (Day 74, Day 73) make the EDF worse in this setting, and the best input resolution stays at 224 even at higher compute, unlike EfficientNet's compound scaling.
  • GPU run time tracks activations (the total size of the conv output tensors) much more closely than FLOPs, and the best models grow activations only with roughly the square root of FLOPs.

Adding SE blocks (Day 72) gives RegNetY. Under comparable training settings the abstract reports RegNet models outperforming EfficientNets at similar FLOPs while being up to 5× faster on GPUs.

Limits & what broke
  • A cheap proxy. EDFs come from 10-epoch, low-budget training. The authors check that conclusions hold under longer training for some settings, but rankings under a proxy can differ from rankings at full scale.
  • The "comparable settings" caveat cuts both ways. For the EfficientNet comparison the authors retrain EfficientNet under their own, simpler training setup, which gives lower EfficientNet numbers than the original paper reports; with strong augmentation and long schedules the picture can shift. Tune the baseline before you believe the delta, in both directions.
  • The principles live inside one family. They are rules for X-block ResNeXt-style networks, not laws of vision architectures. Depthwise and inverted designs lose here partly because of how the space and training were set up.
  • Honest contrast with Day 76: NAS found a better point for a FLOP budget; RegNet found a better space and the reasons behind it. The second is more reusable, and the first is what most people deployed.

A ConvNet for the 2020s (ConvNeXt)

45m videoLevel 2

Liu, Mao, Wu, Feichtenhofer, Darrell & Xie, CVPR 2022 (arXiv:2201.03545)

ConvNeXt modernizes a standard ResNet-50 one change at a time toward the design choices of a Swin Transformer, while staying purely convolutional, and ends up more accurate than Swin-T. Most of the gap between ViT-era models and ConvNets turned out to come from the training recipe and macro and micro design, not from self-attention.

Cold open

The paper's Figure 2 is the paper: a ladder of thirteen rungs, each a single change, each with its ImageNet-1K top-1 accuracy at roughly constant FLOPs. It starts at 76.1% (ResNet-50 with its original recipe) and ends at 82.0%, above Swin-T's 81.3%. Read every rung aloud. The first rung, which touches no architecture at all, is worth 2.7 points.

The problem

By 2021 the Vision Transformer (Day 115) and the hierarchical Swin Transformer had overtaken ConvNets on ImageNet and on detection and segmentation, and the common reading was that attention is simply a better inductive bias. But the transformer papers had also changed nearly everything else: 300-epoch training with AdamW and heavy augmentation, patch-based stems, different stage layouts, LayerNorm, GELU. Attention was confounded with all of it. The authors' experiment is to start from ResNet-50, match Swin-T's compute (about 4.5 GFLOPs), apply the transformer-era changes one by one, and measure each.

The method: the roadmap, rung by rung

1. Training recipe (+2.7). 300 epochs instead of 90, AdamW (Day 38), Mixup (Day 112), CutMix and RandAugment (Day 113), Random Erasing, stochastic depth and label smoothing (Day 66), close to the DeiT and Swin recipes. ResNet-50 goes from 76.1% to 78.8% before a single layer changes. That 2.7 points is the most important number this month: a large part of what looked like an architecture gap was a training gap.

2. Macro design. Change the blocks per stage from ResNet-50's (3, 4, 6, 3) to (3, 3, 9, 3), Swin-T's 1:1:3:1 ratio: 79.4%. Replace the 7×7 stride-2 stem and max-pool with a "patchify" stem, a 4×4 convolution with stride 4 that cuts the image into non-overlapping patches like ViT: 79.5%.

3. ResNeXt-ify. Make the 3×3 convolution depthwise (groups = channels, Day 71, Day 73), which cuts FLOPs, then spend them on width, going from 64 to Swin-T's 96 channels: 80.5%. The authors point out that a depthwise convolution is a per-channel weighted sum over space, much like self-attention's weighted sum, so the block now separates spatial mixing from channel mixing, as a Transformer does.

4. Inverted bottleneck. A Transformer's MLP expands by 4×, so make the block narrow → wide → narrow (Day 74): 80.6%, with fewer FLOPs because of the cheaper downsampling shortcuts.

5. Large kernels. Move the depthwise convolution to the start of the block, as attention comes before the MLP, so the expensive wide layers are 1×1: 79.9% temporarily, at lower FLOPs. Then grow the kernel to 7×7: 80.6%. Larger kernels give no further gain.

6. Micro design. GELU instead of ReLU: no change. Keep only one activation per block, between the two 1×1 layers, as in a Transformer MLP: 81.3%. Keep only one normalization: 81.4%. Replace BatchNorm with LayerNorm: 81.5%. Use separate downsampling layers (a 2×2 stride-2 convolution between stages, with LayerNorm added where needed for stability) instead of striding inside the first block: 82.0%.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class ConvNeXtBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, layer_scale=1e-6):
        super().__init__()
        self.dwconv = nn.Conv2d(dim, dim, 7, padding=3, groups=dim)   # spatial mixing, per channel
        self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(dim)                                  # the only norm in the block
        self.pw1 = nn.Linear(dim, 4 * dim)                             # 1x1 conv, expand 4x
        self.act = nn.GELU()                                           # the only activation
        self.pw2 = nn.Linear(4 * dim, dim)
        self.gamma = nn.Parameter(layer_scale * torch.ones(dim))       # layer scale

    def forward(self, x):                                  # x: (N, C, H, W)
        y = self.dwconv(x).permute(0, 2, 3, 1)             # channels last for LayerNorm and Linear
        y = self.pw2(self.act(self.pw1(self.norm(y))))
        return x + (self.gamma * y).permute(0, 3, 1, 2)    # residual; stochastic depth omitted
Large-kernel depthwise conv as local attention

A depthwise 7×7 convolution computes, for channel \(c\) at position \(p\), \(y_c(p) = \sum_{q \in \mathcal N_7(p)} w_c(q - p)\, x_c(q)\). Window self-attention (Swin uses 7×7 windows) computes \(y(p) = \sum_{q \in \mathcal W(p)} a(p, q)\, v(q)\) with \(a(p, \cdot) = \mathrm{softmax}_q\big(\langle W_Q x(p), W_K x(q)\rangle/\sqrt{d}\big)\). Both are weighted sums over a local spatial window. The convolution's weights are static, depend only on the offset, and differ per channel; attention's weights depend on the content and are shared across the channels of a head. A large depthwise kernel is a cheap, input-independent approximation of local attention, and the ladder suggests that for ImageNet classification the content-dependence is not where the accuracy was.

Evidence

The final ConvNeXt-T matches or beats Swin-T at similar FLOPs, and the family scales: larger ConvNeXts, with and without ImageNet-22K pre-training, are competitive with Swin at every size the paper reports, and the same holds as backbones for COCO detection and ADE20K segmentation. The authors also report comparable or better inference throughput, since depthwise convolution has no attention-specific overheads such as window shifting. The conclusion, in their framing: the design choices are not new individually, but together they show that a pure ConvNet can compete, and that the training recipe and macro design explain much of what was attributed to attention.

Limits & what broke
  • One path through a large space. Changes are applied in one order, each kept if it helps. Interactions and alternative orders are not explored, so the ladder shows that this sequence works, not how much each idea is worth in isolation.
  • Noise. Single-run differences of 0.1 point are within run-to-run variation on ImageNet; the paper does not give error bars for the ladder.
  • "Pure ConvNet" with transformer parts. LayerNorm, GELU, inverted MLPs, patchify stems and layer scale all came from the transformer line. The finding is about attention specifically, not about convolution versus transformers as whole design traditions.
  • Practical costs. LayerNorm cannot be folded into the preceding convolution at inference as BatchNorm can, and 7×7 depthwise layers are memory-bound (Day 73), so real speed depends heavily on the implementation.
  • Successors. ConvNeXt V2 (Woo et al. 2023) adds masked-autoencoder pre-training and a global response normalization layer. Wightman et al.'s "ResNet strikes back" (2021) makes the recipe point independently by pushing a plain ResNet-50 above 80%.

Deformable Convolutional Networks

30m videoLevel 2

Dai et al., ICCV 2017 (arXiv:1703.06211)

Deformable convolution adds a learned 2-D offset to every sampling location of a kernel, predicted from the input by a parallel convolution. The offsets are fractional, so values are read by bilinear interpolation, which keeps everything differentiable. The network learns where to look, and its receptive fields bend to the shape and scale of the object.

Cold open

The money figure: stack three deformable 3×3 layers and trace the \(9^3 = 729\) input locations that feed one output unit. On a small object they huddle around it. On a large object they spread out to cover it. On background they spread wide. A regular CNN would show the same fixed square in all three cases.

The problem

A CNN handles geometric variation (scale, pose, deformation) only through data augmentation and the brute force of capacity. Every unit in a layer has the same receptive field shape, and pooling and RoI pooling use fixed grids, though objects at different positions have different scales and shapes. Spatial Transformer Networks (Day 81) learn a warp, but a single global parametric one per input; dilated convolutions (Day 80) enlarge the grid, but by a fixed pattern. Deformable convolution makes the sampling grid dense, local and learned.

The method: offsets on the sampling grid

A regular 3×3 convolution samples a fixed grid \(\mathcal R = \{(-1,-1), (-1,0), \dots, (1,1)\}\) around each output position \(p_0\):

$$y(p_0) = \sum_{p_n \in \mathcal R} w(p_n)\, x(p_0 + p_n)\qquad\longrightarrow\qquad y(p_0) = \sum_{p_n \in \mathcal R} w(p_n)\, x(p_0 + p_n + \Delta p_n).$$

The offsets \(\Delta p_n\) come from a parallel convolution branch applied to the same input, with the same kernel size and dilation, outputting \(2N\) channels: an \((x, y)\) offset for each of the \(N = 9\) kernel taps at every output position. That branch is initialized to output zero, so training starts from a regular convolution. Because the offsets are real-valued, \(p = p_0 + p_n + \Delta p_n\) is fractional and \(x(p)\) has to be interpolated.

The method: bilinear interpolation is the mechanism

Spend time here, because this is what makes the idea trainable. The value at a fractional location is

$$x(p) = \sum_q G(q, p)\, x(q),\qquad G(q, p) = g(q_x, p_x)\, g(q_y, p_y),\qquad g(a, b) = \max(0,\ 1 - |a - b|),$$

where \(q\) ranges over integer locations and only the four neighbours of \(p\) have non-zero weight.

Derivation · the gradient with respect to an offset

By the chain rule,

$$\frac{\partial y(p_0)}{\partial \Delta p_n} = \sum_q w(p_n)\,\frac{\partial G(q,\, p_0 + p_n + \Delta p_n)}{\partial \Delta p_n}\, x(q).$$

In 1-D it becomes transparent. Write \(p = q_0 + f\) with \(q_0 = \lfloor p\rfloor\) and \(0 \le f < 1\). Then \(x(p) = (1 - f)\,x(q_0) + f\,x(q_0 + 1)\) and \(\partial x(p)/\partial p = x(q_0 + 1) - x(q_0)\): the gradient with respect to the offset is a local finite difference of the feature map. An offset moves in the direction that increases the response the loss wants. In 2-D, \(\partial x(p)/\partial p_x = (1 - f_y)\,[x(q_{00} + e_x) - x(q_{00})] + f_y\,[x(q_{00} + e_x + e_y) - x(q_{00} + e_y)]\). The derivative is undefined exactly at integer positions (the kink of \(g\)), where implementations pick a one-sided value, and it is zero for any neighbour more than one pixel away, so offsets learn only from local evidence.

import numpy as np

def bilinear(x, py, px):
    """Sample 2-D array x at fractional (py, px) using G(q, p) = g(qy, py) * g(qx, px)."""
    y0, x0 = int(np.floor(py)), int(np.floor(px))
    val = 0.0
    for qy in (y0, y0 + 1):
        for qx in (x0, x0 + 1):
            if 0 <= qy < x.shape[0] and 0 <= qx < x.shape[1]:
                val += max(0, 1 - abs(qy - py)) * max(0, 1 - abs(qx - px)) * x[qy, qx]
    return val

x = np.arange(16.0).reshape(4, 4) ** 1.5

The method: deformable RoI pooling

RoI pooling (Day 83) divides a region into \(k\times k\) bins and pools each. The deformable version shifts each bin by its own offset \(\Delta p_{ij}\). The offsets come from an FC layer applied to the regularly pooled features, and are predicted in normalized form and scaled by the RoI size, \(\Delta p_{ij} = \gamma\,\Delta\hat p_{ij}\circ(w, h)\) with \(\gamma = 0.1\), so the same prediction means the same relative shift for small and large boxes. A position-sensitive variant serves R-FCN-style detectors.

Evidence

The authors replace the last few 3×3 convolutions of a ResNet-101 backbone (three layers is their usual setting) and test on semantic segmentation (DeepLab, Day 93) and detection (Faster R-CNN, Day 84, and R-FCN) on PASCAL VOC, Cityscapes and COCO. The gains are consistent across tasks, with COCO detection improving by a few points of mAP, and deformable RoI pooling adds more. The extra parameters and FLOPs are small. The analysis measures an "effective dilation", the mean distance between adjacent sampling locations, and finds it grows with object size and is largest on background, which is the quantitative version of the money figure. The method generalizes dilation, which is the special case of a fixed, uniform offset pattern.

Limits & what broke
  • Speed in practice. Data-dependent, irregular memory reads do not map onto standard convolution kernels. Deformable convolution needs custom CUDA code, runs well below its FLOP count, and has historically been awkward to deploy through standard inference toolchains.
  • Support leaks. DCNv2 (Zhu, Hu, Lin & Dai 2019) found the v1 sampling locations often spread well beyond the object. It adds a learned modulation scalar \(\Delta m_k \in [0, 1]\) per tap, \(y(p) = \sum_k w_k\, x(p + p_k + \Delta p_k)\,\Delta m_k\), uses deformable layers throughout the later stages, and adds a feature-mimicking loss. Its effective receptive field visualizations show tighter, more object-focused support.
  • Mostly a detection and segmentation tool. The gains are smaller for image classification, where a global label tolerates fixed grids.

Bridge

This is spatial adaptivity without attention's quadratic cost: each output reads \(N\) learned locations, so the cost stays linear in the number of positions, while self-attention compares every position with every other. The idea returns almost verbatim in Deformable DETR (Day 90), whose attention reads a few learned sampling points around a reference location. PyTorch exposes the operation as torchvision.ops.deform_conv2d, with an optional mask for the v2 modulation. Next, two days on the ancestors it generalizes: dilated convolutions tomorrow, and on Day 81 the Spatial Transformer, which learns one global warp instead of many local offsets.

Multi-Scale Context Aggregation by Dilated Convolutions

30m videoLevel 2

Yu & Koltun, ICLR 2016 (arXiv:1511.07122)

A dilated convolution spaces its kernel taps apart. Stack 3×3 convolutions with dilation rates 1, 2, 4, 8, … and the receptive field grows exponentially with depth, while the resolution stays full and the parameters and FLOPs stay exactly those of ordinary 3×3 layers. Yu and Koltun use this to build a context module for dense prediction.

Cold open

Four 3×3 layers with dilations 1, 2, 4 and 8 see a 31×31 window, without a single pooling layer or stride. Four ordinary 3×3 layers see 9×9. The growth is exponential in depth, and it costs nothing extra per layer.

The problem

Semantic segmentation needs a label for every pixel, which requires two things at once: wide context (is this grey region road or wall?) and full resolution (where exactly is the boundary?). Classification networks get context by downsampling 32× with pooling and strides, and dense-prediction systems of the time, such as FCN (Day 91), then had to recover resolution with up-convolutions or by combining multi-scale inputs. The authors ask whether the downsampling was needed at all. Pooling is wrong for dense prediction in a specific sense: it throws away exactly the spatial detail the output requires, and upsampling cannot fully bring it back.

The method: the dilated convolution

Definition · dilated convolution

For a discrete input \(F\), kernel \(k\) and dilation factor \(l\),

$$(F *_l k)(\mathbf p) = \sum_{\mathbf s + l\mathbf t = \mathbf p} F(\mathbf s)\, k(\mathbf t).$$

With \(l = 1\) this is an ordinary convolution. With \(l = 2\), a 3×3 kernel's nine taps sit two pixels apart and cover a 5×5 window. The kernel still has nine weights and still does nine multiply-adds per output position and channel pair: dilation adds zero parameters and zero FLOPs relative to the same kernel size. (DeepLab calls the same operation atrous convolution.)

Derivation · the receptive field with rates 1, 2, 4, …

Let \(F_{i+1} = F_i *_{2^i} k_i\) for \(i = 0, 1, \dots\), with 3×3 kernels, and let \(\mathrm{RF}_i\) be the width of the window of \(F_0\) that one element of \(F_i\) depends on, so \(\mathrm{RF}_0 = 1\). A 3×3 kernel with dilation \(l\) reaches \(l\) further on each side, so

$$\mathrm{RF}_{i+1} = \mathrm{RF}_i + 2\cdot 2^i = 1 + 2\sum_{j=0}^{i} 2^j = 1 + 2\,(2^{i+1} - 1) = 2^{i+2} - 1.$$

The receptive field of \(F_{i+1}\) is \((2^{i+2}-1)\times(2^{i+2}-1)\): 3, 7, 15, 31 for rates 1, 2, 4, 8. Exponential in depth, while parameters grow linearly. Ordinary 3×3 layers give \(\mathrm{RF} = 2n + 1\), linear in depth.

The method: the context module and the front end

The context module takes \(C\) feature maps (one per class) and returns \(C\) maps of the same size, so it can be bolted onto any dense-prediction network. The basic version is seven 3×3 layers with dilations 1, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 1, each followed by a ReLU, then a final 1×1 layer, all with \(C\) channels: a 67×67 receptive field for \(64C^2\) parameters. A large version uses wider intermediate layers.

Identity initialization. The authors report that standard random initialization did not work well for the context module. Instead each layer starts as an identity map, \(k^b(\mathbf t, a) = \mathbb 1[\mathbf t = 0]\,\mathbb 1[a = b]\) for input channel \(a\) and output channel \(b\), so at initialization the module passes its input straight through and training only has to learn useful deviations. (The large module, whose layer widths change, uses a scaled identity plus small noise.) The idea that a new component should start as the identity, so that adding it cannot hurt, recurs through the rest of the course.

The front end is VGG-16 (Day 64) adapted for dense output: the last two pooling and striding layers are removed, and the convolutions after each removed pool are dilated (by 2, then 4) so their receptive fields are unchanged while the output stays at higher resolution.

Evidence

On PASCAL VOC 2012, the authors report that the simplified front end alone is more accurate than FCN-8s and DeepLab, which use more elaborate upsampling. Adding the context module improves it further, the large module more than the basic one, and adding a CRF-RNN on top improves it again, with the gains holding as the components are stacked. Results on CamVid, KITTI and Cityscapes follow the same pattern. What the evidence shows is that full-resolution context aggregation works; it does not compare against equally tuned encoder–decoder designs.

Limits & what broke
  • The gridding artifact is real and under-discussed. With repeated rates the taps never land on some inputs. Stack 3-tap layers with dilations \(r_1, r_2, \dots\): an output depends only on input offsets \(\sum_j \varepsilon_j r_j\) with \(\varepsilon_j \in \{-1, 0, 1\}\). If all rates share a factor \(g > 1\), only multiples of \(g\) are reachable, and in 2-D just \(1/g^2\) of the receptive field contributes, which shows up as checkerboard patterns in the output. Rates 1, 2, 4, 8 avoid it; rates 2, 2, 2, common in dilated backbones, do not.
  • Fixes. Wang et al. (2018) propose hybrid dilated convolution, choosing rates with no common factor, such as 1, 2, 5. Yu, Koltun & Funkhouser (2017, "Dilated Residual Networks") "degrid" by removing max-pooling, adding layers with decreasing dilation at the end, and removing residual connections in those layers.
  • Full resolution is expensive. Removing downsampling means every later layer runs on a larger map, so memory and compute grow with the square of the resolution kept. Encoder–decoder designs (Day 92) trade some of that back.

Spatial Transformer Networks

35m videoLevel 2

Jaderberg, Simonyan, Zisserman & Kavukcuoglu, NIPS 2015 (arXiv:1506.02025)

A spatial transformer is a differentiable module that predicts a geometric transformation from its own input, maps every output coordinate back to an input coordinate, and reads the input there with a bilinear kernel. Dropped into any network, it learns to crop, rotate, scale and straighten with no supervision beyond the task loss.

Cold open

Feed a CNN MNIST digits that have been rotated, scaled, translated and placed in clutter. Put a spatial transformer in front of it, train only on the classification loss, and look at what the transformer outputs: upright, centred, normalized digits. Nobody told it what "canonical" means. It found the pose that made classification easiest.

The problem

CNNs are only weakly spatially invariant. Max-pooling buys a little local invariance over small windows, and everything else has to be learned from augmented data, spending capacity on every pose. The authors want a module that actively transforms a feature map, conditioned on that feature map, inside the network, trained end to end with plain backprop.

The method: three parts

  1. Localisation network: any network, fully connected or convolutional, that regresses the transformation parameters \(\theta = f_\text{loc}(U)\) from the input feature map. For an affine transformation \(\theta\) has 6 numbers.
  2. Grid generator: for each location \(G_i = (x_i^t, y_i^t)\) on the regular output grid, compute the source location in the input. For the affine case, in normalized coordinates \([-1, 1]\),
$$\begin{pmatrix} x_i^s \\ y_i^s \end{pmatrix} = \mathcal T_\theta(G_i) = A_\theta \begin{pmatrix} x_i^t \\ y_i^t \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \theta_{11} & \theta_{12} & \theta_{13} \\ \theta_{21} & \theta_{22} & \theta_{23} \end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_i^t \\ y_i^t \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$
  1. Sampler: read the input at each \((x_i^s, y_i^s)\) with a sampling kernel. With the bilinear kernel, for every channel \(c\),
$$V_i^c = \sum_{n}\sum_{m} U_{nm}^c\,\max\big(0,\ 1 - |x_i^s - m|\big)\,\max\big(0,\ 1 - |y_i^s - n|\big).$$

The paper also uses projective, piecewise-affine and thin-plate-spline (TPS) transformations, the last defined by a small grid of control points, which can model smooth non-rigid warps. A transformation with \(A_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} s & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & s & t_y\end{bmatrix}\), \(s < 1\), is a crop-and-zoom, which is the attention-like use: the network learns where to look and at what scale.

The direction convention

The grid maps output coordinates back to input coordinates (inverse warping). This is deliberate: forward-mapping input pixels would scatter them to fractional output positions, leaving holes and collisions, while inverse mapping gives every output pixel exactly one well-defined read. Getting the direction backwards is the classic implementation bug. \(\theta\) is the inverse of the motion you see. In the snippet below, a "scale 0.5" matrix magnifies the content 2×, because each output pixel reads from a region half as large.

import numpy as np

def affine_grid(theta, H, W):
    """For each OUTPUT pixel, the INPUT location to read, in normalized [-1, 1] coordinates."""
    ys, xs = np.meshgrid(np.linspace(-1, 1, H), np.linspace(-1, 1, W), indexing="ij")
    tgt = np.stack([xs.ravel(), ys.ravel(), np.ones(H * W)])     # target (output) coordinates
    return (theta @ tgt).T.reshape(H, W, 2)                      # source (x_s, y_s) per output pixel

def sample(U, grid):
    """Bilinear sampler: V_i = sum_nm U_nm max(0, 1-|x_s-m|) max(0, 1-|y_s-n|)."""
    H, W = U.shape
    xs = (grid[..., 0] + 1) * (W - 1) / 2                         # normalized -> pixel coordinates
    ys = (grid[..., 1] + 1) * (H - 1) / 2
    x0, y0 = np.floor(xs).astype(int), np.floor(ys).astype(int)
    V = np.zeros(xs.shape)
    for n in (y0, y0 + 1):
        for m in (x0, x0 + 1):
            w = np.clip(1 - np.abs(xs - m), 0, None) * np.clip(1 - np.abs(ys - n), 0, None)
            inside = (m >= 0) & (m < W) & (n >= 0) & (n < H)
            V += np.where(inside, w * U[n.clip(0, H - 1), m.clip(0, W - 1)], 0.0)
    return V

This is what torch.nn.functional.affine_grid and grid_sample do, with the same convention. The pixel mapping here, \((x+1)(W-1)/2\), corresponds to align_corners=True; the two functions must agree on that flag.

The method: the gradient through the sampler

Derivation · ∂L/∂θ

The sampler is differentiable with respect to both its inputs. With respect to the feature map,

$$\frac{\partial V_i^c}{\partial U_{nm}^c} = \max\big(0, 1 - |x_i^s - m|\big)\,\max\big(0, 1 - |y_i^s - n|\big),$$

so gradients flow back to the four input pixels each output read. With respect to the sampling location,

$$\frac{\partial V_i^c}{\partial x_i^s} = \sum_n\sum_m U_{nm}^c\,\max\big(0, 1 - |y_i^s - n|\big)\cdot\begin{cases} 0 & |m - x_i^s| \ge 1 \\ 1 & m \ge x_i^s \\ -1 & m < x_i^s \end{cases}$$

and similarly for \(y_i^s\): a finite difference of the image across the cell containing the sample. Finally the grid is linear in \(\theta\): \(x_i^s = \theta_{11}x_i^t + \theta_{12}y_i^t + \theta_{13}\), so \(\partial x_i^s/\partial(\theta_{11}, \theta_{12}, \theta_{13}) = (x_i^t, y_i^t, 1)\), and likewise for the second row. Chaining,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_{1j}} = \sum_i \sum_c \frac{\partial L}{\partial V_i^c}\,\frac{\partial V_i^c}{\partial x_i^s}\,(x_i^t,\ y_i^t,\ 1)_j.$$

The kernel has kinks at integer positions, so this is a sub-gradient, which works fine in practice. This chain is the crux: it is what lets a classification loss teach a network where to look.

A common practice, used in PyTorch's own STN tutorial, is to initialize the localisation network's last layer to output the identity transform (zero weights, bias \([1, 0, 0, 0, 1, 0]\)), so the module starts as a no-op.

Evidence

On distorted MNIST variants (rotated; rotated, translated and scaled; projective; elastic), networks with spatial transformers have lower error than plain CNNs and fully connected nets, with the TPS transformer generally best. On Street View House Numbers, inserting transformers into a multi-digit recognition network gave state-of-the-art results at the time for a small speed cost. On CUB-200-2011 bird classification, two or four transformers run in parallel learned, without any part annotations, to attend to distinct parts such as the head and the body, and the combined model improved on the previous best. The paper also shows co-localisation and 3-D variants.

Limits & what broke
  • Hard to train well. The localisation net learns only through the downstream loss. It can drift into degenerate transforms, for example zooming out until it samples padding, and often needs a lower learning rate and identity initialization.
  • One global warp per transformer. An affine or TPS transform per image fits digits and birds, not cluttered scenes with many objects. Dense, local adaptivity came from deformable convolution (Day 79) and later from attention.
  • Now mostly a component, not a headline. The full module is rare in modern architectures, but its sampler is everywhere: grid_sample in PyTorch is literally this paper, used to warp images by optical flow (Day 107), to align images in registration networks, and to read features at fractional locations, as RoIAlign does (Day 89).

Bridge

Rich Feature Hierarchies for Accurate Object Detection (R-CNN)

45m videoLevel 2

Girshick, Donahue, Darrell & Malik, CVPR 2014 (arXiv:1311.2524)

R-CNN classifies category-independent region proposals using features from a CNN that is pre-trained on ImageNet classification and then fine-tuned on detection. It raised PASCAL VOC accuracy by more than 30% relative, and it was the founding demonstration that supervised pre-training followed by fine-tuning transfers a large CNN to a task with little labelled data.

Cold open

The abstract: mean average precision of 53.3% on VOC 2012, more than a 30% relative improvement over the previous best. Detection on PASCAL had been inching forward with HOG-based deformable part models (Day 109). A year after AlexNet (Day 61), this paper answered the question everyone was asking: does ImageNet classification performance carry over to detection? It does, if you are careful about how.

The problem

Two obstacles. Localization: a classifier outputs one label per image, while detection needs boxes. Framing it as regression worked poorly at the time, and sliding a CNN over every position and scale (as OverFeat did) was expensive with large receptive fields. Data: PASCAL has thousands of labelled boxes, far too few to train a 60M-parameter network from scratch.

The method: the pipeline

  1. Region proposals. Selective search (fast mode) gives about 2000 category-independent candidate boxes per image.
  2. Features. Each box is enlarged by 16 pixels of context, warped to 227×227 regardless of aspect ratio, and passed through the CNN; the 4096-d fc7 activation is the feature.
  3. Scoring. One linear SVM per class scores every box, followed by greedy non-maximum suppression per class.
  4. Localization fix. A class-specific linear regressor on pool5 features refines each box.

The method: pre-train, then fine-tune

The CNN is first trained on ILSVRC 2012 classification, with image-level labels only. Then the 1000-way layer is replaced by an \((N+1)\)-way one (20 VOC classes plus background) and the whole network is fine-tuned by SGD on warped proposals at a tenth of the original learning rate. Proposals with IoU ≥ 0.5 against a ground-truth box are positives for that box's class, the rest are background, and each minibatch of 128 holds 32 positives and 96 background windows. The authors' conclusion is the paper's most cited sentence in spirit: supervised pre-training on a large auxiliary dataset, followed by domain-specific fine-tuning on a small one, is an effective way to train high-capacity CNNs when data is scarce.

Why SVMs instead of the softmax? A good methodological story, told in Appendix B. The SVMs use a different labelling: only ground-truth boxes are positives, proposals with IoU below 0.3 to every ground truth of that class are negatives (the threshold was chosen by grid search; 0.5 or 0 cost several mAP points), and the rest are ignored, with standard hard-negative mining over the huge negative set. The authors explain that fine-tuning needed the looser IoU ≥ 0.5 positives to get roughly 30× more positive examples and avoid overfitting, but those jittered boxes are not precise, and the softmax is trained on random rather than hard negatives. Replacing the SVMs with the fine-tuned softmax dropped VOC 2007 mAP by about three points. They conjectured better fine-tuning could close the gap, and Fast R-CNN did (Day 83).

Derivation · the box regression targets

For a proposal \(P = (P_x, P_y, P_w, P_h)\) (centre, width, height) matched to ground truth \(G\), regress

$$t_x = \frac{G_x - P_x}{P_w},\qquad t_y = \frac{G_y - P_y}{P_h},\qquad t_w = \log\frac{G_w}{P_w},\qquad t_h = \log\frac{G_h}{P_h}.$$

Dividing the shifts by the box size makes the targets scale-invariant; taking logs of the size ratios makes them symmetric (doubling and halving have equal magnitude) and keeps predicted sizes positive, since \(\hat G_w = P_w\, e^{d_w(P)}\). Each \(d_\star(P) = \mathbf w_\star^\top \phi_5(P)\) is ridge regression on pool5 features (\(\lambda = 1000\)), trained only on proposals with IoU above 0.6, where the mapping is close to linear. The authors report it fixes a large number of mislocalized detections, worth 3–4 mAP points. Every later detector, including Faster R-CNN's anchors (Day 84), keeps this parameterization.

The method: the definitions you will reuse for ten episodes

Definition · IoU, matching, AP, mAP, NMS
  • IoU of boxes \(A, B\): \(|A\cap B| / |A\cup B|\), from 0 (disjoint) to 1 (identical).
  • Matching. Sort a class's detections by score. A detection is a true positive if its best-overlapping ground truth has IoU ≥ 0.5 and has not already been matched; otherwise it is a false positive. Duplicates are false positives.
  • AP. Walking down the sorted list gives precision \(= \text{TP}/(\text{TP}+\text{FP})\) and recall \(= \text{TP}/\#\text{ground truths}\) at every cut-off. AP is the area under the precision–recall curve, after replacing each precision by the maximum precision at any higher recall (VOC 2007 sampled 11 recall points; later VOC uses every point).
  • mAP is the mean of AP over classes. COCO's "AP" additionally averages over IoU thresholds 0.50, 0.55, …, 0.95, and reports AP50, AP75 and AP for small, medium and large objects.
  • NMS. Keep the highest-scoring box, delete every remaining box whose IoU with it exceeds a threshold, repeat.
def iou(a, b):
    """Boxes as (x1, y1, x2, y2)."""
    iw = max(0.0, min(a[2], b[2]) - max(a[0], b[0]))
    ih = max(0.0, min(a[3], b[3]) - max(a[1], b[1]))
    inter = iw * ih
    union = (a[2] - a[0]) * (a[3] - a[1]) + (b[2] - b[0]) * (b[3] - b[1]) - inter
    return inter / union

def nms(boxes, scores, thresh=0.5):
    """Greedy NMS: keep the best box, drop boxes overlapping a kept one by more than thresh."""
    keep = []
    for i in sorted(range(len(boxes)), key=lambda i: -scores[i]):
        if all(iou(boxes[i], boxes[j]) <= thresh for j in keep):
            keep.append(i)
    return keep

def average_precision(dets, gts, thr=0.5):
    """One class, one image. dets: [(score, box)], gts: [box]. VOC-style all-point AP."""
    used, hits = set(), []
    for _, box in sorted(dets, key=lambda d: -d[0]):
        g = max(range(len(gts)), key=lambda k: iou(box, gts[k]))   # best-overlapping ground truth
        hit = g not in used and iou(box, gts[g]) >= thr               # duplicates count as false
        hits.append(hit)
        used |= {g} if hit else set()
    ap, prev_recall, tp = 0.0, 0.0, 0
    prec = [sum(hits[:k + 1]) / (k + 1) for k in range(len(hits))]
    for k, hit in enumerate(hits):
        if hit:                                   # recall steps up: add a slab of the curve
            tp += 1
            ap += (tp / len(gts) - prev_recall) * max(prec[k:])   # interpolated precision
            prev_recall = tp / len(gts)
    return ap

dets = [(0.9, (12, 12, 48, 52)), (0.8, (11, 9, 50, 49)), (0.7, (58, 62, 99, 101)), (0.3, (0, 70, 20, 90))]

Fast R-CNN

35m videoLevel 2

Girshick, ICCV 2015 (arXiv:1504.08083)

Fast R-CNN runs the CNN once per image, pools a fixed-size feature for each proposal from the shared feature map (RoI pooling), and trains classification and box regression together with one multi-task loss, in a single stage. With VGG-16 it trains 9× faster than R-CNN, runs 213× faster at test time, and is more accurate.

Cold open

One forward pass instead of two thousand. R-CNN (Day 82) recomputed the convolutional features for every overlapping proposal, though most of each proposal's pixels had already been processed for its neighbours. Compute the feature map once and crop it instead, and test time with VGG-16 falls from about 47 seconds to about a fifth of a second per image.

The problem

R-CNN had three problems: a multi-stage training pipeline (fine-tune, SVMs, regressors), expensive training in time and disk space (features cached for every proposal), and slow detection. SPPnet (He et al. 2014) had already fixed the speed by sharing convolutions and pooling a spatial pyramid of features per proposal, but it kept the multi-stage training and, the author points out, could not effectively update the convolutional layers below its pooling layer. Backpropagating through pooled regions is very inefficient when every training region comes from a different image, because each region's receptive field covers most of its image.

The method: shared features and RoI pooling

Algorithm · Fast R-CNN forward pass
  1. Run the whole image through the convolutional layers once (for VGG-16, conv5_3 at stride 16).
  2. Project each of the ~2000 proposals onto the feature map and RoI-pool it to a fixed \(H\times W = 7\times7\) grid per channel.
  3. Run each pooled feature through two FC layers.
  4. Two sibling outputs: a softmax over \(K + 1\) classes and, for each of the \(K\) classes, four box-regression offsets.

RoI pooling divides an \(h\times w\) region of the feature map into an \(H\times W\) grid of sub-windows of size about \(h/H \times w/W\) and max-pools each into one output cell, independently per channel. It is SPP with a single pyramid level. To do that on a discrete feature map it quantizes twice: the RoI's coordinates are divided by the stride and rounded to cells, and then each bin's boundaries are rounded to cell edges.

import numpy as np

def roi_pool(fmap, roi, out=7, stride=16):
    """fmap: (C, H, W). roi: (x1, y1, x2, y2) in image pixels. Caffe-style RoI max pooling."""
    C, H, W = fmap.shape
    x1, y1, x2, y2 = (int(np.floor(v / stride + 0.5)) for v in roi)   # quantization 1: snap to cells
    h, w = max(y2 - y1 + 1, 1), max(x2 - x1 + 1, 1)
    y = np.zeros((C, out, out))
    for i in range(out):
        for j in range(out):
            hs = min(max(y1 + int(np.floor(i * h / out)), 0), H)         # quantization 2: bin edges
            he = min(max(y1 + int(np.ceil((i + 1) * h / out)), 0), H)
            ws = min(max(x1 + int(np.floor(j * w / out)), 0), W)
            we = min(max(x1 + int(np.ceil((j + 1) * w / out)), 0), W)
            if he > hs and we > ws:
                y[:, i, j] = fmap[:, hs:he, ws:we].max(axis=(1, 2))
    return y

Backprop through RoI pooling routes each output's gradient to the input cell that won the max, summed over all RoIs: \(\partial L/\partial x_i = \sum_r\sum_j [\,i = i^*(r, j)\,]\,\partial L/\partial y_{rj}\). Hierarchical sampling makes this efficient: each SGD minibatch takes \(N = 2\) images and 64 RoIs from each, so RoIs from the same image share the forward and backward computation. A quarter of the RoIs are foreground (IoU ≥ 0.5 with a ground truth); the rest are background with IoU in \([0.1, 0.5)\), the lower bound acting as a crude hard-negative heuristic.

The method: one multi-task loss

For an RoI with true class \(u\) (0 for background), predicted class distribution \(p\), predicted offsets \(t^u\) for class \(u\), and regression targets \(v\) (R-CNN's parameterization, normalized to zero mean and unit variance):

$$L(p, u, t^u, v) = -\log p_u + \lambda\,[u \ge 1]\sum_{i \in \{x, y, w, h\}} \mathrm{smooth}_{L_1}\big(t^u_i - v_i\big),\qquad \mathrm{smooth}_{L_1}(x) = \begin{cases} 0.5x^2 & |x| < 1 \\ |x| - 0.5 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

The bracket switches the box loss off for background RoIs, and \(\lambda = 1\). Everything, from the convolutional layers up, is trained together in one stage, replacing R-CNN's three.

Derivation · why smooth L1 and not L2

The gradient of \(\tfrac12 x^2\) is \(x\), unbounded: a proposal far from its ground truth produces a huge regression error and a huge gradient. The gradient of smooth L1 is \(\mathrm{clip}(x, -1, 1)\): linear near zero, where precise fitting matters, and constant beyond, so no single outlier dominates an update. The paper notes that with the L2 loss of R-CNN and SPPnet, the learning rate has to be tuned carefully to prevent exploding gradients when targets are unbounded. Smooth L1 is the Huber loss with threshold 1, the same loss DQN uses for TD errors, for the same reason.

Truncated SVD. With thousands of RoIs per image, the FC layers take nearly half of detection time. Approximating each FC weight matrix \(W \approx U\Sigma_tV^\top\) replaces one \(u\times v\) layer with two layers of \(t(u + v)\) total weights. The paper reports this cuts detection time by more than 30% for a 0.3-point mAP drop.

Evidence

With VGG-16, the paper reports training in 9.5 hours instead of R-CNN's 84, and testing at 0.32 s per image instead of 47 s (0.22 s, or 213× faster, with truncated SVD), with higher mAP on VOC 2007, 2010 and 2012. Against SPPnet it trains 3× faster, tests 10× faster and is more accurate. The ablations answer real design questions:

  • Fine-tuning the convolutional layers matters. Freezing them and training only the FC layers, as SPPnet effectively did, drops VOC 2007 mAP from 66.9% to 61.4%. For VGG-16 it suffices to fine-tune from conv3_1 up.
  • Multi-task training helps classification too, compared with training the classifier alone and adding regression afterwards.
  • Softmax is slightly better than post-hoc SVMs once training is one-stage, closing the question R-CNN left open.
  • Single-scale testing is nearly as good as an image pyramid, so deep networks learn much of the scale invariance directly.
  • More proposals are not better: beyond a point mAP falls slightly, and dense sliding windows do worse than sparse selective-search proposals.
Limits & what broke
  • The proposals are now the bottleneck. Selective search takes about 2 seconds per image on a CPU, roughly an order of magnitude more than the network itself. "Real time" excludes the step that dominates the run time.
  • Two quantizations misalign the pooled features with the RoI by up to about a cell, tens of image pixels at stride 16. Harmless for boxes, damaging for masks (Day 89).
  • Proposals are still not learned, so the detector cannot improve them.

Faster R-CNN: Towards Real-Time Object Detection with RPNs

45m videoLevel 2

Ren, He, Girshick & Sun, NIPS 2015 (arXiv:1506.01497)

Faster R-CNN replaces selective search with a Region Proposal Network that shares the detector's convolutional features. A small network slides over the feature map and, at every position, scores and refines \(k\) fixed reference boxes, called anchors, of several scales and aspect ratios. Proposals become learned and nearly free, and the result is the two-stage detector template that lasted five years.

Cold open

Make the proposals themselves a neural network. After Fast R-CNN (Day 83) the detector took about 0.3 s per image and selective search about 2 s on a CPU. With an RPN, the proposal step adds about 10 ms on top of the shared convolutions, and the whole VGG-16 system runs at about 5 frames per second, 0.2 s per image, including everything.

The problem

Region proposal methods were the test-time bottleneck. Selective search took about 2 s per image and EdgeBoxes about 0.2 s, still as long as the detection network itself. Re-implementing them on the GPU would not share any computation with the detector, and they could not learn from detection data. The authors' observation: the convolutional feature maps the detector already computes could be used to generate proposals.

The method: the Region Proposal Network

Slide a 3×3 convolution over the last shared feature map (stride 16), mapping each window to a 512-d feature for VGG-16 (256-d for ZF), followed by a ReLU. Two sibling 1×1 convolutions read that feature: a classification layer with \(2k\) outputs (object vs not, for each anchor) and a regression layer with \(4k\) outputs (box offsets, for each anchor).

The method: anchors, the key abstraction

An anchor is a fixed reference box: a regression target is always expressed relative to a specific anchor, so the network never predicts a box from scratch. With anchor \(a = (x_a, y_a, w_a, h_a)\), the network predicts \(t\) and is trained toward \(t^*\):

$$t_x = \frac{x - x_a}{w_a},\quad t_y = \frac{y - y_a}{h_a},\quad t_w = \log\frac{w}{w_a},\quad t_h = \log\frac{h}{h_a};\qquad t^*_x = \frac{x^* - x_a}{w_a},\ \dots,\ t^*_h = \log\frac{h^*}{h_a},$$

R-CNN's parameterization (Day 82) with the anchor as the reference box. Each of the \(k\) regressors is responsible for one anchor shape and they share no weights, so a fixed-size 3×3 feature can predict boxes of many sizes. The default is 3 scales (areas \(128^2, 256^2, 512^2\)) times 3 aspect ratios, \(k = 9\), giving about \(WHk\) ≈ 20,000 anchors on a typical feature map. The authors call this a pyramid of anchors: multi-scale prediction from one image scale and one filter size, much cheaper than an image pyramid. The RPN's output layers have about \(512\times(4 + 2)\times9 \approx 2.8\times10^4\) parameters.

import numpy as np

def make_anchors(fmap_hw=(38, 50), stride=16, scales=(128, 256, 512), ratios=(0.5, 1.0, 2.0)):
    """All anchors as (cx, cy, w, h): k = 9 shapes at every feature-map position."""
    shapes = np.array([(s / np.sqrt(r), s * np.sqrt(r)) for s in scales for r in ratios])  # w*h = s^2, h/w = r
    ys, xs = np.meshgrid(np.arange(fmap_hw[0]), np.arange(fmap_hw[1]), indexing="ij")
    centers = np.stack([(xs.ravel() + 0.5) * stride, (ys.ravel() + 0.5) * stride], axis=1)
    return np.concatenate([np.repeat(centers, len(shapes), axis=0),
                           np.tile(shapes, (len(centers), 1))], axis=1)

def encode(g, a):
    """Regression targets t* of box g relative to anchor a, both (cx, cy, w, h)."""
    return np.array([(g[0] - a[0]) / a[2], (g[1] - a[1]) / a[3], np.log(g[2] / a[2]), np.log(g[3] / a[3])])

def decode(t, a):
    return np.array([a[0] + t[0] * a[2], a[1] + t[1] * a[3], a[2] * np.exp(t[2]), a[3] * np.exp(t[3])])

A = make_anchors()                       # a 600x800 image at stride 16 gives a 38x50 map
print(A.shape)                           # (17100, 4): 1900 positions x 9 anchors

The method: labels, loss and training

Definition · anchor assignment

An anchor is positive if it has the highest IoU with some ground-truth box, or IoU above 0.7 with any ground-truth box. The first rule matters because occasionally no anchor reaches 0.7 for a box. It is negative if its IoU is below 0.3 with every ground-truth box. Everything in between, and anchors crossing the image boundary, are ignored during training.

The loss over a minibatch of 256 anchors sampled from one image, up to half of them positive, is

$$L(\{p_i\}, \{t_i\}) = \frac{1}{N_\text{cls}}\sum_i L_\text{cls}(p_i, p_i^*) + \lambda\,\frac{1}{N_\text{reg}}\sum_i p_i^*\, L_\text{reg}(t_i, t_i^*),$$

with log loss for classification, smooth L1 (Day 83) for regression, active only for positives (\(p_i^* = 1\)), \(N_\text{cls} = 256\), \(N_\text{reg}\) about 2400 (the number of anchor locations) and \(\lambda = 10\) so the two terms are roughly balanced. The authors report results insensitive to \(\lambda\) over a wide range.

Algorithm · 4-step alternating training
  1. Train the RPN, initialized from ImageNet.
  2. Train a separate Fast R-CNN detector, also ImageNet-initialized, on the step-1 proposals. No layers are shared yet.
  3. Initialize the RPN from the detector, freeze the shared conv layers, and fine-tune only the RPN-specific layers. Now the convolutions are shared.
  4. Keeping the shared layers frozen, fine-tune the detector-specific layers.

The paper also describes approximate joint training: one network, both losses summed, with the proposals treated as constants (the gradient with respect to proposal coordinates is ignored). It gives close accuracy with 25–50% less training time, and it is what the later public implementations use. Exact joint training would need RoI pooling to be differentiable with respect to the box coordinates, which bilinear RoIAlign (Day 89) later makes possible. At test time the ~20,000 scored anchors are reduced by NMS at IoU 0.7, and the top 300 proposals go to the detector.

Evidence

With VGG-16 on VOC 2007 (trained on 2007+2012), the paper reports 73.2% mAP using 300 RPN proposals, against 70.0% for Fast R-CNN with 2000 selective-search proposals, and 70.4% on VOC 2012. The full system runs at about 0.2 s per image. Recall-versus-IoU curves show why fewer proposals suffice: RPN recall drops only gracefully as the proposal count goes from 2000 to 300, while selective search and EdgeBoxes fall faster. Ablations show that sharing features helps, that the classification scores matter for ranking proposals, and that multiple anchors beat a single anchor shape, with scales contributing more than aspect ratios. With ResNet-101 backbones, Faster R-CNN was the basis of the winning ILSVRC and COCO 2015 detection entries (Day 67).

Limits & what broke
  • Anchors are hand-designed hyperparameters. Scales, ratios and the 0.7/0.3 thresholds must be retuned for new datasets with unusual object shapes, and most anchors are easy negatives, a class imbalance that RetinaNet confronts (Day 87).
  • One coarse feature map. Every anchor is predicted from stride-16 features, which is poor for small objects; FPN fixes that (Day 88).
  • "Real time" was aspirational. 5 fps is far from video rate, and the second stage still runs per RoI. Single-shot detectors take the opposite trade (Day 85, Day 86).
  • What lasted: nearly every detector until DETR (Day 90) regresses relative to anchors. Anchor-free detectors and DETR are defined by removing them.

You Only Look Once: Unified, Real-Time Object Detection (YOLO)

40m videoLevel 2

Redmon, Divvala, Girshick & Farhadi, CVPR 2016 (arXiv:1506.02640)

YOLO treats detection as a single regression from pixels to a fixed \(S\times S\times(B\cdot5 + C)\) tensor of box coordinates, confidences and class probabilities, computed by one network in one pass. It runs in real time and makes far fewer background false positives than Fast R-CNN, at the price of worse localization and trouble with small objects in groups.

Cold open

The paper reports 45 frames per second for the base model and 155 fps for Fast YOLO on PASCAL VOC 2007, against about 7 fps for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 (Day 84). No proposals, no per-region classifier, no separate box refinement: the image goes into one network and a tensor of detections comes out.

The problem

Detectors of the time were pipelines. Deformable part models slid classifiers over the image, and the R-CNN family generated proposals, classified each one, refined the boxes and then removed duplicates (Days 82–84). Each stage was trained separately, the pipelines were hard to optimize, and even the fastest accurate one was far from video rate. A region-based classifier also sees only a crop, never the whole image, which the authors argue is why it mistakes background patches for objects.

The method: the output tensor

Divide the image into an \(S\times S\) grid. The cell containing an object's centre is responsible for detecting it. Each cell predicts \(B\) boxes, each as \((x, y, w, h, \text{confidence})\): \(x, y\) give the centre as an offset within the cell, and \(w, h\) are fractions of the whole image. The confidence target is \(\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\mathrm{IoU}^\text{truth}_\text{pred}\): zero if the cell contains no object, otherwise the IoU between the predicted and true box. Each cell also predicts one set of \(C\) conditional class probabilities \(\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object})\), shared by its \(B\) boxes. At test time the two multiply into class-specific scores per box:

$$\Pr(\text{Class}_i \mid \text{Object})\cdot\Pr(\text{Object})\cdot\mathrm{IoU}^\text{truth}_\text{pred} = \Pr(\text{Class}_i)\cdot\mathrm{IoU}^\text{truth}_\text{pred}.$$

For VOC, \(S = 7\), \(B = 2\), \(C = 20\): a 7×7×30 output. The network has 24 convolutional layers, using 1×1 reductions followed by 3×3 convolutions in the spirit of GoogLeNet (Day 65), and two fully connected layers. Fast YOLO uses 9 convolutional layers. The first 20 layers are pre-trained on ImageNet at 224×224, and detection runs at 448×448, because detection needs finer detail.

import numpy as np

S, B, C = 7, 2, 20
out = np.random.default_rng(0).random((S, S, B * 5 + C))   # stand-in for the network's output

def decode(out, thresh=0.2):
    """S x S x (B*5 + C) tensor -> (class, score, cx, cy, w, h), all as fractions of the image."""
    dets = []
    for i in range(S):                                      # grid row
        for j in range(S):                                  # grid column
            p_cls = out[i, j, B * 5:]                       # Pr(class | object): one set per cell
            for b in range(B):
                x, y, sw, sh, conf = out[i, j, 5 * b:5 * b + 5]
                scores = p_cls * conf                       # class-specific confidence
                c = int(scores.argmax())
                if scores[c] > thresh:
                    cx, cy = (j + x) / S, (i + y) / S       # x, y are offsets inside the cell
                    dets.append((c, float(scores[c]), cx, cy, sw ** 2, sh ** 2))  # sqrt(w), sqrt(h) squared
    return dets                                             # then per-class NMS, as on Day 82

print(len(decode(out)), "boxes above threshold out of", S * S * B)

The method: the multi-part loss

Everything is sum-squared error, chosen because it is easy to optimize, and then reweighted, because plain sum-squared error weights the wrong things:

$$\begin{aligned} &\lambda_\text{coord}\sum_{i=0}^{S^2}\sum_{j=0}^{B}\mathbb 1^\text{obj}_{ij}\Big[(x_i-\hat x_i)^2+(y_i-\hat y_i)^2\Big] + \lambda_\text{coord}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\mathbb 1^\text{obj}_{ij}\Big[\big(\sqrt{w_i}-\sqrt{\hat w_i}\big)^2+\big(\sqrt{h_i}-\sqrt{\hat h_i}\big)^2\Big]\\ &+\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\mathbb 1^\text{obj}_{ij}\big(C_i-\hat C_i\big)^2 + \lambda_\text{noobj}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}\mathbb 1^\text{noobj}_{ij}\big(C_i-\hat C_i\big)^2 + \sum_{i}\mathbb 1^\text{obj}_{i}\sum_{c}\big(p_i(c)-\hat p_i(c)\big)^2, \end{aligned}$$

with \(\lambda_\text{coord} = 5\) and \(\lambda_\text{noobj} = 0.5\). Here \(\mathbb 1^\text{obj}_{ij}\) marks the predictor \(j\) in cell \(i\) that is "responsible" for an object, the one whose current box has the highest IoU with it. Assigning responsibility this way makes the two predictors in a cell specialize in different sizes and aspect ratios. Class error counts only in cells that contain an object.

Derivation · the two reweightings

The square root. Sum-squared error penalizes a 10-pixel error equally in a tiny box and a huge one, but it matters far more for the tiny box. For a small error \(\delta = \hat w - w\), a first-order expansion gives

$$\big(\sqrt{\hat w} - \sqrt{w}\big)^2 \approx \Big(\frac{\delta}{2\sqrt w}\Big)^2 = \frac{\delta^2}{4w},$$

so an absolute width error is weighted by \(1/(4w)\), more heavily for small boxes. The authors call it a partial fix; the weighting still is not the relative error that IoU implies.

\(\lambda_\text{noobj}\). Of the 98 predictors in a typical image only a handful are responsible for objects. Pushing the confidence of the other ninety-odd to zero would overpower the gradient from the few that matter and, the authors report, makes training unstable and diverge early. Multiplying their term by 0.5 (and boosting coordinates by 5) is a crude fix for this class imbalance. Day 87 solves exactly this problem properly.

Evidence

On VOC 2007 the paper reports 63.4% mAP at 45 fps for YOLO and 52.7% at 155 fps for Fast YOLO, against 70.0% for Fast R-CNN (at about half a frame per second) and 73.2% for Faster R-CNN with VGG-16 at 7 fps. An error analysis with the Hoiem et al. tool shows the trade clearly: YOLO's top detections contain many more localization errors than Fast R-CNN's, but far fewer background false positives (the paper gives under 5% for YOLO against over 13% for Fast R-CNN). Because the errors are complementary, using YOLO to rescore Fast R-CNN's detections raises Fast R-CNN's VOC 2007 mAP by about 3 points. On artwork datasets (Picasso, People-Art) YOLO's accuracy degrades much less than R-CNN's, which the authors attribute to reasoning about the whole image rather than local patches. NMS matters less than for proposal-based detectors, adding 2–3 points of mAP, because the grid already spreads predictions out.

Limits & what broke
  • Each grid cell predicts one class, and only two boxes. This is the main structural limitation: nearby objects, and small objects that appear in groups such as flocks of birds, collide in the same cell.
  • Coarse localization. Boxes are regressed from heavily downsampled features with no reference shapes, so unusual aspect ratios generalize poorly and localization error dominates.
  • Low recall. 98 candidate boxes per image, against thousands of proposals or anchors.
  • v2 and v3, in brief. YOLOv2 (Redmon & Farhadi 2017) adds batch normalization, a higher-resolution classifier stage, anchors back (with shapes chosen by k-means clustering of training boxes, using 1 − IoU as the distance), offsets bounded within each cell, a passthrough layer for finer features, multi-scale training and the Darknet-19 backbone. YOLOv3 (2018 tech report) predicts at three scales, as FPN does (Day 88), uses the residual Darknet-53 backbone, and replaces the softmax with independent logistic classifiers.

SSD: Single Shot MultiBox Detector

30m videoLevel 2

Liu et al., ECCV 2016 (arXiv:1512.02325)

SSD predicts class scores and box offsets for a fixed set of default boxes on several feature maps of decreasing resolution, all in one pass. It keeps YOLO's single-shot speed while matching or beating Faster R-CNN's accuracy, and its weakness on small objects shows that high-resolution features are not enough without deep semantics.

Cold open

The abstract of the final version: with a 300×300 input, SSD reaches 74.3% mAP on VOC 2007 test at 59 fps on a single GPU, and with 512×512 input 76.9%, above a comparable Faster R-CNN. Compare yesterday's YOLO at 63.4% and Faster R-CNN at 73.2% and 7 fps: YOLO's speed with anchor-based accuracy.

The problem

YOLO (Day 85) predicts from a single coarse 7×7 map through fully connected layers, with no reference boxes, and localizes poorly. Faster R-CNN (Day 84) is accurate but needs a second stage that resamples features for every proposal. The authors locate the speed in removing the proposal-and-resampling stage, and their accuracy in three choices: small convolutional predictors for categories and offsets, separate predictors for different aspect ratios, and predictions from multiple feature maps at different scales.

The method: predict from many feature maps

The backbone is VGG-16 (Day 64) truncated before its classifier, with fc6 and fc7 turned into convolutions (fc6 dilated, Day 80), followed by extra convolutional layers that shrink the map step by step. On each chosen \(m\times n\) map with \(p\) channels, a small 3×3×\(p\) convolution outputs, for each of the \(k\) default boxes at every location, \(c\) class scores (background included) and 4 offsets: \((c + 4)\,k\,mn\) outputs per map. Predicting from multiple maps is the core idea and the direct ancestor of FPN (Day 88).

The method: default boxes and matching

Derivation · the scale formula, computed

With \(m\) prediction maps, map \(k\) gets default-box scale

$$s_k = s_\text{min} + \frac{s_\text{max} - s_\text{min}}{m - 1}\,(k - 1),\qquad k = 1, \dots, m,\qquad s_\text{min} = 0.2,\ s_\text{max} = 0.9,$$

as a fraction of the image side. For \(m = 6\) the step is \(0.7/5 = 0.14\): scales 0.20, 0.34, 0.48, 0.62, 0.76, 0.90. Each location gets boxes of aspect ratio \(a_r \in \{1, 2, 3, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\}\) with \(w = s_k\sqrt{a_r}\), \(h = s_k/\sqrt{a_r}\) (so area stays \(s_k^2\)), plus one extra square of scale \(s'_k = \sqrt{s_k s_{k+1}}\): six boxes per location. Some maps drop the ratios 3 and 1/3 and keep four. For the first map: the 2:1 box is \(0.283 \times 0.141\) and the extra square is \(\sqrt{0.2\cdot0.34} \approx 0.26\) on a side. Summing over the SSD300 maps, \(38^2\cdot4 + 19^2\cdot6 + 10^2\cdot6 + 5^2\cdot6 + 3^2\cdot4 + 1^2\cdot4 = 8732\) default boxes.

import math

def ssd_default_boxes(fmaps=(38, 19, 10, 5, 3, 1), s_min=0.2, s_max=0.9,
                      ratios=((2,), (2, 3), (2, 3), (2, 3), (2,), (2,))):
    """Default boxes (cx, cy, w, h) as fractions of the image, SSD300 layout."""
    m = len(fmaps)
    s = [s_min + (s_max - s_min) * k / (m - 1) for k in range(m)] + [1.0]    # s_{m+1} = 1 by convention
    boxes = []
    for k, f in enumerate(fmaps):
        shapes = [(s[k], s[k]), (math.sqrt(s[k] * s[k + 1]),) * 2]          # ratio 1, plus the extra scale
        for r in ratios[k]:                                                 # ratio r and 1/r
            shapes += [(s[k] * math.sqrt(r), s[k] / math.sqrt(r)), (s[k] / math.sqrt(r), s[k] * math.sqrt(r))]
        boxes += [((j + 0.5) / f, (i + 0.5) / f, w, h) for i in range(f) for j in range(f) for w, h in shapes]
    return s[:m], boxes

Matching. Each ground-truth box is matched to the default box with the highest Jaccard overlap (IoU), and then every default box with IoU above 0.5 to any ground truth is also a positive. Allowing several positives per object, rather than only the best one, simplifies learning. The loss is \(L = \frac1N\big(L_\text{conf} + \alpha L_\text{loc}\big)\) over the \(N\) matched boxes, with softmax cross-entropy for classes, smooth L1 (Day 83) on Faster R-CNN-style offsets relative to each default box, and \(\alpha = 1\).

Hard negative mining at 3:1. After matching, almost all of the 8732 boxes are negatives. SSD sorts them by confidence loss and keeps only the hardest, so that negatives outnumber positives at most 3 to 1. The authors report faster optimization and more stable training. It works, but it discards most of the data and fixes the ratio by hand, which is what focal loss replaces tomorrow.

Augmentation is essential. Each training image is either used whole or replaced by a random crop, sampled so that it overlaps the objects with a minimum IoU of 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 or 0.9, or sampled at random, then resized and flipped, with photometric distortions. The final version adds a "zoom out" step that places the image on a larger canvas to create small training objects.

Evidence

In the ablation table, the sampling-based augmentation is the largest single contributor, worth several mAP points. Using more default-box shapes helps, as does predicting from more maps: restricting predictions to fewer, coarser layers costs accuracy, especially for small objects. The sensitivity analysis shows SSD strong on large objects and robust across aspect ratios, with fewer localization errors than a region-based detector because the box is regressed directly, but with much worse performance on small objects than on large ones, and more confusion between similar categories.

Limits & what broke
  • Small objects still fail, and the reason is subtle. conv4_3 is a 38×38 map at stride 8, fine enough for small boxes; the problem is that it sits only about ten convolutional layers into the network, so its features are semantically weak. SSD deliberately starts at conv4_3 and does not use even higher-resolution layers for that reason. Small-object failure is therefore a feature-semantics problem, not a resolution problem: no layer is both high-resolution and deep. Larger inputs (SSD512) help, and the zoom-out augmentation helps, but neither changes this. That distinction is what makes FPN's answer, bringing deep semantics down to high-resolution maps, non-obvious (Day 88).
  • Hand-set imbalance handling. The 3:1 ratio and the ignored easy negatives are a heuristic answer to an imbalance of thousands to one.
  • Hand-set default boxes: scales, ratios and the matching threshold all need retuning for new data.

Tomorrow's paper argues the reason was never the architecture. It was the extreme imbalance between a handful of objects and tens of thousands of easy background boxes, and it fixes that with one change to the loss.

Focal Loss for Dense Object Detection (RetinaNet)

40m videoLevel 2

Lin, Goyal, Girshick, He & Dollár, ICCV 2017 (arXiv:1708.02002)

One-stage detectors trailed two-stage ones because roughly 100,000 candidate boxes per image are overwhelmingly easy background, and their many small losses swamp the gradient. Focal loss, \(-(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t\), down-weights the examples the model already gets right, and with it a simple dense detector, RetinaNet, overtook the two-stage detectors.

Cold open

By 2017 the most accurate detectors were all two-stage (Day 84), and the fast one-stage ones (Day 85, Day 86) were noticeably less accurate. The usual explanation was architectural: the second stage re-examines each region. This paper argues that the gap came from the loss, keeps the architecture deliberately plain, changes one term, and reports a one-stage detector more accurate than every existing two-stage one on COCO.

The problem: class imbalance, diagnosed

A one-stage detector evaluates on the order of \(10^4\)–\(10^5\) candidate locations per image, and only a handful contain objects. Two-stage detectors avoid the worst of this: the proposal stage cuts candidates to one or two thousand, mostly removing easy background, and the second stage samples a fixed foreground:background ratio such as 1:3, or uses online hard example mining. One-stage detectors had tried the same heuristics, as SSD's 3:1 hard negative mining does, but they are inefficient when training is dominated by easy negatives. The authors identify two effects: training is inefficient, because most locations are easy negatives that carry no useful signal, and the easy negatives together can overwhelm training and produce degenerate models.

Derivation · why easy negatives dominate even when each is small

Write binary cross-entropy as \(\mathrm{CE} = -\log p_t\), where \(p_t = p\) for a positive and \(1 - p\) for a negative. Take one image with 100,000 easy negatives at \(p_t = 0.9\) and 10 hard positives at \(p_t = 0.1\):

$$\underbrace{10^5 \times (-\log 0.9)}_{\approx\,10{,}500} \quad\text{vs}\quad \underbrace{10 \times (-\log 0.1)}_{\approx\,23}.$$

The gradients tell the same story. For a sigmoid output with logit \(z\), \(\partial\,\mathrm{CE}/\partial z = p - y\): each easy negative contributes 0.1 and each hard positive 0.9 in magnitude, but \(10^5 \times 0.1 = 10^4\) against \(10 \times 0.9 = 9\). Each easy example is individually negligible, and together they set the direction of every update. With focal loss at \(\gamma = 2\) the easy negatives' loss shrinks by \((1 - 0.9)^2 = 0.01\) to about 105, while the hard positives keep \((0.9)^2 = 81\%\) of theirs, about 19: the ratio falls from over 450:1 to under 6:1.

The method: the focal loss

The first fix is α-balancing, \(-\alpha_t\log p_t\), with weight \(\alpha\) for positives and \(1-\alpha\) for negatives. It rebalances positives against negatives but not easy against hard. Focal loss adds a modulating factor:

$$\mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^\gamma\,\log(p_t),\qquad \text{in practice}\quad \mathrm{FL}(p_t) = -\alpha_t\,(1 - p_t)^\gamma\,\log(p_t).$$

When an example is misclassified, \(p_t\) is small and the factor is near 1, so its loss is barely changed. As \(p_t \to 1\) the factor goes to 0. With \(\gamma = 2\), an example at \(p_t = 0.9\) has 100× lower loss than under cross-entropy, and one at \(p_t \approx 0.968\) about 1000× lower.

α and γ are complementary, not alternatives. The best setting is \(\gamma = 2\) with \(\alpha = 0.25\), which gives positives less weight than negatives, the opposite of what α-balancing alone would suggest. Once the focal term has suppressed the easy negatives, the positives no longer need up-weighting. The authors also show that the exact functional form matters less than the shape: other functions that down-weight easy examples work similarly.

import numpy as np

def focal_loss(logits, y, gamma=2.0, alpha=0.25):
    """Sigmoid focal loss, summed over anchors. y in {0, 1}; log-sigmoid for stability."""
    p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-logits))
    log_p, log_1mp = -np.logaddexp(0, -logits), -np.logaddexp(0, logits)   # log p, log(1 - p)
    p_t = np.where(y == 1, p, 1 - p)
    log_pt = np.where(y == 1, log_p, log_1mp)
    alpha_t = np.where(y == 1, alpha, 1 - alpha)
    return -(alpha_t * (1 - p_t) ** gamma * log_pt).sum()

for pt in (0.9, 0.968):
    z = np.log(pt / (1 - pt))                                  # logit of a positive with this p_t
    ce = focal_loss(z, 1, gamma=0.0, alpha=1.0)
    print(pt, round(ce / focal_loss(z, 1, alpha=1.0)))          # 100, then about 1000

The method: the footnote that is the whole trick

Derivation · the prior-probability initialization

Initialize the bias of the final classification layer to \(b = -\log\big((1 - \pi)/\pi\big)\) with \(\pi = 0.01\), so every anchor starts with predicted foreground probability about 0.01 instead of 0.5. Why it matters: with \(p = 0.5\) everywhere, each of ~100,000 background anchors contributes \(-\log 0.5 \approx 0.69\), about 69,000 in total, to a loss normalized by the number of positive anchors, perhaps a hundred. At \(p = 0.01\) each contributes \(-\log 0.99 \approx 0.01\), about 1,000 in total. The authors report that their first attempt, standard cross-entropy with standard initialization, diverged quickly, and that this one change of initialization made training work. It also stabilizes focal loss. It is a "the paper's footnote is the whole trick" moment: without it the result does not reproduce.

RetinaNet is deliberately ordinary: a ResNet backbone with a Feature Pyramid Network (levels P3 to P7, tomorrow's paper, Day 88), 9 anchors per level (three scales times three aspect ratios), and two small fully convolutional heads shared across levels, one for classification (four 3×3 conv layers, then \(K\cdot A\) sigmoid outputs) and one for class-agnostic box regression. Anchors with IoU ≥ 0.5 to a ground truth are positives, below 0.4 negatives, and in between ignored. The focal loss is summed over all roughly 100,000 anchors, with no sampling, and normalized by the number of positive anchors.

Evidence

The ablations isolate the loss. With the same network, the best α-balanced cross-entropy is about 3 AP below focal loss with \(\gamma = 2\), and focal loss also beats the best online hard example mining configuration by about 3 AP. Plots of the cumulative loss over positives and negatives show the mechanism: for positives, changing \(\gamma\) barely changes the distribution, while for negatives at \(\gamma = 2\) nearly all of the loss comes from a small fraction of hard examples. The final RetinaNet with ResNet-101-FPN reports about 39 AP on COCO test-dev, above the two-stage detectors it compares against, including Faster R-CNN with FPN, and a ResNeXt backbone goes higher. Its speed–accuracy curve dominates the other detectors in the comparison.

Limits & what broke
  • Two new hyperparameters, \(\gamma\) and \(\alpha\), that interact and need tuning; and the anchor design and IoU thresholds are still hand-set.
  • Not a speed paper. The dense heads at high-resolution pyramid levels are expensive, and RetinaNet runs at speeds similar to two-stage FPN detectors. The contribution is accuracy for one-stage designs.
  • Easy is defined by the current model. Focal loss also down-weights examples that are easy because the labels are wrong or ambiguous, and it can down-weight rare classes once they are learned. Later variants (quality focal loss, varifocal loss) reshape it for detection quality scores.
  • What lasted: sigmoid focal loss and prior-probability bias initialization became default components of dense detectors, including anchor-free ones.

Bridge

Class imbalance is the unifying theme of this lineage: R-CNN's hard negative mining (Day 82), Faster R-CNN's 1:1 sampling (Day 84), YOLO's \(\lambda_\text{noobj} = 0.5\) (Day 85), SSD's 3:1 mining (Day 86), and now a loss that handles it without sampling. RetinaNet rested on a Feature Pyramid Network, which tomorrow's paper introduces: the fix for the small-object problem SSD left open.

Feature Pyramid Networks for Object Detection

35m videoLevel 2

Lin, Dollár, Girshick, He, Hariharan & Belongie, CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1612.03144)

A ConvNet already computes a pyramid of feature maps, but only its coarse top is semantically strong. FPN adds a top-down pathway that upsamples the strong coarse maps and adds them, through 1×1 lateral connections, to the finer maps below, so every level is both high-resolution and semantically strong, and one shared head runs on all of them.

Cold open

COCO objects range from a few pixels to the whole image, and a detector reading one stride-16 map must cover all of them with one set of features. The paper's first figure compares the four ways to handle scale. It is the fastest way to teach the method: walk all four panels before anything else.

The problem

(a) Image pyramids were the hand-engineered answer; the HOG detector of Day 109 slides over one. Every level is strong because every level passes through the whole feature extractor. Top ImageNet and COCO entries still tested on pyramids, the authors note, but inference time rises steeply and end-to-end training on a pyramid does not fit in memory, so pyramids were test-time only, inconsistent with training. Fast and Faster R-CNN (Day 83, Day 84) therefore default to (b).

(c) The network's own hierarchy is a pyramid for free, and SSD (Day 86) predicts from it. But its levels differ in depth as well as resolution, and the authors argue that the high-resolution maps hold low-level features with little capacity for recognition. SSD sidesteps this by starting at conv4_3 and adding layers on top, so it never uses the higher-resolution maps, which this paper shows matter for small objects. What small objects lack is semantics at high resolution, not resolution.

Top-down architectures with skip connections already existed for dense prediction (U-Net, Day 92; SharpMask; stacked hourglass networks), but they merge everything into one fine map and predict there. FPN uses the same shape as a pyramid, predicting independently at every level (the paper's Figure 2).

The method: bottom-up, top-down, lateral

The bottom-up pathway is the backbone's forward pass. For a ResNet it is the output of the last residual block of each stage, \(\{C_2, C_3, C_4, C_5\}\), at strides \(\{4, 8, 16, 32\}\); conv1 is left out because of its memory footprint.

Algorithm · Building the pyramid
  1. Apply a 1×1 convolution to \(C_5\), giving \(d = 256\) channels: the coarsest merged map.
  2. Upsample the current merged map by 2 with nearest-neighbour interpolation.
  3. Apply a 1×1 convolution, the lateral connection, to the bottom-up map of the same size, reducing it to \(d\) channels, and add the two element-wise.
  4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 down to \(C_2\).
  5. Apply a 3×3 convolution to every merged map, to reduce the aliasing of upsampling. The outputs \(\{P_2, \dots, P_5\}\) have the sizes of \(\{C_2, \dots, C_5\}\) and 256 channels each.

The extra layers have no non-linearities; the authors found they made little difference. Top-down maps are coarse but strong; lateral maps are weaker but more precisely localized, having been subsampled fewer times; addition gives each level both. The fixed width \(d\) is what lets one classifier and one box regressor be shared across all levels, just as one detector is shared across the scales of an image pyramid. More elaborate connection modules were only marginally better, the authors report.

The method: using the pyramid

In the RPN (Day 84), the same head slides over every level. Since the levels already cover scale, each level gets anchors of one area, \(\{32^2, 64^2, 128^2, 256^2, 512^2\}\) pixels on \(\{P_2, \dots, P_6\}\), at ratios 1:2, 1:1 and 2:1: 15 anchor types over the pyramid. \(P_6\), a stride-2 subsampling of \(P_5\), exists only for these largest anchors. Unshared heads gave similar accuracy, which the authors read as evidence that all levels share similar semantic levels.

In Fast R-CNN (Day 83), each RoI is pooled from one level into a 7×7 grid feeding two 1024-d fully connected layers, shared across levels and lighter than the conv5 head of the single-scale ResNet baseline.

Derivation · which level an RoI uses

An RoI of width \(w\) and height \(h\), in input pixels, goes to level

$$k = \Big\lfloor k_0 + \log_2\!\big(\sqrt{wh}\,/\,224\big)\Big\rfloor,\qquad k_0 = 4.$$

224 is the ImageNet pre-training size, and the single-scale ResNet detector pools from \(C_4\), so a 224×224 RoI goes to \(P_4\). Each level halves the resolution, so an RoI twice as large moves up one level: that is the \(\log_2\). On level \(k\) (stride \(2^k\)) an RoI of side \(s\) spans \(s/2^k\) cells: 14 at \(s = 224\), and because of the floor, between 14 and 28 for any \(56 \le s < 896\). So every RoI reaches its 7×7 pooling covering a similar number of cells. Examples: \(s = 112 \to 3\); \(s = 448 \to 5\); \(s = 64 \to \lfloor 2.19\rfloor = 2\); \(s = 32 \to \lfloor 1.19\rfloor = 1\), which implementations clamp to \(P_2\).

Evidence

Proposals. On COCO, replacing the single-scale RPN on \(C_4\) with FPN raises average recall at 1000 proposals by about 8 points, and by about 13 on small objects. The ablations isolate the parts. A bottom-up pyramid alone (panel c) is roughly on par with the baseline, which the authors attribute to the semantic gaps between levels. A top-down pyramid without laterals is strong at every level yet clearly worse than FPN: its features have been downsampled and upsampled several times, so their locations are imprecise. The finest level alone (\(P_2\), all anchor scales) beats the baseline but loses to FPN despite far more anchors: scanning a pyramid, not anchor count, makes the fixed-size head robust to scale.

Detection. The pyramid improves Fast and Faster R-CNN over strong single-scale baselines, most on small objects. The single-model Faster R-CNN with FPN and ResNet-101 reports about 36 AP on COCO test-dev, above every existing single-model entry including the 2016 winners, without their image pyramids, hard example mining or context modelling. The paper also reports that the ResNet-50 FPN detector is faster at inference than its single-scale baseline, because the light head replaces the conv5 head run on every RoI. What the evidence does not show is that this merge design is optimal; the paper says so, and the follow-ups found better ones.

Practical note · what marginal cost means

Our arithmetic, not the paper's. For ResNet-50 the neck adds about 3.3M weights to roughly 23.5M in the backbone, but its multiply-adds are not small: the 3×3 convolution on stride-4 \(P_2\) alone costs \(9\cdot256^2/16 \approx 37{,}000\) per input pixel, the whole neck about \(57{,}000\), the backbone about \(82{,}000\). "Marginal" holds against an image pyramid, which reruns the network per scale, and against the heavy RoI head it replaced, not against the backbone.

Limits & what broke
  • Information flows one way. Semantics travel down, but fine-level localization reaches the coarse levels only through the whole backbone. PANet (Liu et al., CVPR 2018) added a bottom-up path; NAS-FPN (Ghiasi et al., CVPR 2019) searched the topology; EfficientDet's BiFPN (Tan, Pang & Le, CVPR 2020) repeats weighted bidirectional merges.
  • Hard, hand-set level assignment. The \(\log_2\) rule uses size alone, and RoIs near a boundary jump levels. PANet pools every proposal from all levels; later anchor-free detectors learn the assignment.
  • \(P_2\) is expensive for dense heads. RetinaNet (Day 87) uses \(P_3\) to \(P_7\), dropping \(P_2\) for computational reasons.
  • Not the only route. ViTDet (Li, Mao, Girshick & He, 2022) found that a plain single-scale ViT with a simple pyramid built from its last map, without top-down and lateral connections, is competitive given strong pre-training.

Mask R-CNN

40m videoLevel 2

He, Gkioxari, Dollár & Girshick, ICCV 2017 (arXiv:1703.06870)

Mask R-CNN turns Faster R-CNN into an instance segmenter by adding one small fully convolutional branch that predicts a binary mask for every detected object, in parallel with classification and box regression. Two details make it work: RoIAlign, which removes RoI pooling's rounding so features line up with pixels, and per-class sigmoid masks, which decouple the mask from the class decision.

Cold open

Instance segmentation asks for a pixel mask per object, keeping two overlapping people apart. The leading 2016 systems, MNC and FCIS (the COCO 2016 winner), were elaborate cascades or joint position-sensitive designs. Mask R-CNN takes the detector of Day 84 with yesterday's pyramid, adds one branch, and outperforms every existing single-model entry on all three COCO tasks it tries (instance segmentation, boxes, person keypoints), the 2016 winners included. The lesson is how little has to change once the details are right.

The problem

Earlier methods either segmented first and classified the segments afterwards (DeepMask-style proposals), which the authors call slow and less accurate, or, like FCIS, predicted classes and masks together, which the paper shows gives systematic errors on overlapping instances and spurious edges. Mask R-CNN is instance-first: detect, then segment inside each box, predicting the mask in parallel with the class.

The obstacle is the feature extractor. RoI pooling (Day 83) rounds the box to cells and then rounds every bin edge: harmless for a class label, mostly harmless for a box, but a mask must line up with the object pixel for pixel.

The method: one more branch

The second stage gains a third output. For each RoI the loss is \(L = L_\text{cls} + L_\text{box} + L_\text{mask}\). The mask branch outputs \(K\) masks of \(m \times m\), one per class, with a per-pixel sigmoid; \(L_\text{mask}\) is the average binary cross-entropy over the mask of the RoI's ground-truth class \(k\) only, and the other \(K - 1\) masks receive no loss. It is defined on positive RoIs (IoU ≥ 0.5), and its target is the ground-truth mask cropped to the RoI.

Why per-class sigmoid, not softmax. A per-pixel softmax over classes, as in FCN-style semantic segmentation, makes classes compete at every pixel, so the mask branch must also decide what the object is. With independent sigmoids it only decides where the object of the already-classified category is; the class comes from the box branch. That is the decoupling.

Why a fully convolutional head. An FC layer flattens the RoI and discards its layout; convolutions keep the \(m\times m\) correspondence with the box. With FPN the head is RoIAlign to 14×14, four 3×3 convolutions, a 2× deconvolution to 28×28 and a 1×1 convolution to \(K\) outputs: a small FCN, the architecture of Day 91.

Algorithm · Mask R-CNN inference
  1. Backbone and FPN, then the RPN's proposals (1000 with FPN).
  2. Box branch on every proposal: class scores and class-specific box refinements; non-maximum suppression.
  3. Mask branch on only the 100 highest-scoring detections, which keeps the overhead small.
  4. For a detection of class \(k\), take the \(k\)-th \(m\times m\) mask, resize it to the box, and threshold at 0.5.

The method: RoIAlign

RoIAlign removes both roundings: the box is mapped to the feature map as \(x/16\), not \([x/16]\), and the bins keep their fractional edges. In each bin it samples four regularly spaced points, each bilinearly interpolated from the four nearest cells, and aggregates them by max or average. The paper notes the result is insensitive to where and how many points are sampled, as long as nothing is quantized; one sample per bin centre is nearly as good. It is the bilinear kernel of Day 79 and Day 81, so gradients reach the four neighbours with the same weights.

Derivation · why half a cell ruins a mask

Rounding a coordinate to the nearest cell moves it by up to half a cell, 8 pixels at stride 16, and the bin edges add their own rounding. If the features describe a region shifted by \(\delta\), the mask predicted from them and pasted into the box is shifted by about \(\delta\). For a \(w\times w\) object shifted along one axis,

$$\mathrm{IoU} = \frac{w - \delta}{w + \delta},\qquad\text{and along both axes}\qquad \mathrm{IoU} = \frac{(w - \delta)^2}{2w^2 - (w - \delta)^2}.$$

For a 64-pixel object, \(\delta = 8\) is \(w/8\): IoU \(= 7/9 \approx 0.78\) along one axis and \(0.766/1.234 \approx 0.62\) along both, below the 0.75 threshold of AP\(_{75}\) before the head has made any error of its own. The rounding error depends on where each box falls on the grid, so it is effectively random per RoI, and the head cannot learn to undo it. A class score barely notices.

import numpy as np

def bilinear(f, y, x):
    """f: (C, H, W); sample (i, j) sits at continuous position (i + 0.5, j + 0.5)."""
    C, H, W = f.shape
    y, x = min(max(y - 0.5, 0.0), H - 1.0), min(max(x - 0.5, 0.0), W - 1.0)
    y0, x0 = int(y), int(x)
    y1, x1 = min(y0 + 1, H - 1), min(x0 + 1, W - 1)
    dy, dx = y - y0, x - x0
    return ((1 - dy) * (1 - dx) * f[:, y0, x0] + (1 - dy) * dx * f[:, y0, x1]
            + dy * (1 - dx) * f[:, y1, x0] + dy * dx * f[:, y1, x1])

def roi_align(fmap, roi, out=7, stride=16, s=2):     # roi in image pixels; s x s samples per bin
    x1, y1, x2, y2 = (v / stride for v in roi)               # no rounding anywhere
    bh, bw = (y2 - y1) / out, (x2 - x1) / out
    y = np.zeros((fmap.shape[0], out, out))
    for i in range(out):
        for j in range(out):
            pts = [(y1 + (i + (a + 0.5) / s) * bh, x1 + (j + (b + 0.5) / s) * bw)
                   for a in range(s) for b in range(s)]
            y[:, i, j] = np.mean([bilinear(fmap, py, px) for py, px in pts], axis=0)
    return y

fmap = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(size=(256, 38, 50))     # the same map as Day 83's demo
a = roi_align(fmap, (100, 100, 300, 300))
b = roi_align(fmap, (103, 100, 303, 300))                       # box moved 3 pixels right
print(a.shape, np.array_equal(a, b))                            # (256, 7, 7) False

Limits & what broke
  • 28×28 masks are coarse. Large objects get blobby boundaries. PointRend (Kirillov, Wu, He & Girshick, CVPR 2020) refines masks at adaptively chosen points.
  • The mask lives inside the box. A box that cuts off part of an object truncates its mask, and the mask's confidence is the box classifier's score, not mask quality; Mask Scoring R-CNN (Huang et al., CVPR 2019) added a mask-IoU head.
  • RoIAlign itself was off by half a pixel. The original implementation did not subtract 0.5 when mapping coordinates to pixel indices; Detectron2's ROIAlign(aligned=True) fixes it, and its documentation says the difference does not matter when convolutions follow. A fixed offset can be learned; position-dependent rounding cannot.
  • What lasted: RoIAlign replaced RoI pooling everywhere, for boxes too, and Mask R-CNN stayed the default instance-segmentation baseline until query-based models such as Mask2Former (Day 96).

End-to-End Object Detection with Transformers (DETR)

45m videoLevel 3

Carion, Massa, Synnaeve, Usunier, Kirillov & Zagoruyko, ECCV 2020 (arXiv:2005.12872)

DETR predicts detections as a set: a CNN and a transformer encoder-decoder turn N learned object queries into N class-and-box predictions in parallel, and bipartite matching assigns each ground-truth object to exactly one prediction in the loss. With one-to-one training there is nothing to suppress, so anchors, assignment rules and non-maximum suppression disappear.

Cold open

Count the hand-designed parts in this month's detectors: anchor scales and ratios (Day 84), IoU thresholds for positives, sampling ratios or focal-loss constants (Day 87), level-assignment rules (Day 88), RoI operators (Day 89), and non-maximum suppression after all of them. DETR deletes every one and still matches a carefully tuned Faster R-CNN on COCO. The bill: a far longer training schedule and weak small objects.

The problem

Detectors need NMS because their training targets reward duplicates: every anchor above an IoU threshold is a positive, so neighbouring anchors all learn to fire on one object, and post-processing must delete the extras. Detection outputs a set, but the loss treats it as a dense grid of independent guesses. Bipartite-matching set losses had been tried (Stewart, Andriluka & Ng 2016, for people in crowds), but with autoregressive RNN decoders, on small datasets, and never against modern detectors.

The method: architecture

A ResNet's 2048-channel stride-32 map is reduced to \(d = 256\) by a 1×1 convolution and flattened into \(H\cdot W\) tokens. The encoder is six standard self-attention layers; attention ignores order, so fixed sine positional encodings are added at every attention layer. The decoder's six layers transform \(N = 100\) embeddings in parallel, not autoregressively, with self-attention among them and cross-attention to the image. Its inputs are the object queries, learned positional embeddings: in the reference code the decoder's content starts at zero and the queries are added at every attention layer, so they act as learned spatial priors. A shared MLP predicts a normalized box (centre, width, height), and a linear layer a softmax over \(K + 1\) classes. Attention is taught properly in Month 5 (Day 126, Day 129).

The method: the set prediction loss

Pad the ground truth \(y\) to \(N\) elements with ∅ and find the cheapest one-to-one assignment of ground truths to slots:

$$\hat\sigma = \argmin_{\sigma\in S_N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathcal L_\text{match}\big(y_i, \hat y_{\sigma(i)}\big),\qquad \mathcal L_\text{match} = [c_i \ne \varnothing]\Big(-\hat p_{\sigma(i)}(c_i) + \mathcal L_\text{box}\big(b_i, \hat b_{\sigma(i)}\big)\Big).$$

Padding elements cost nothing, so in effect the \(G\) real objects are assigned to \(G\) of the \(N\) slots. The matching uses the probability \(\hat p\), not its log, which the authors say makes the class term commensurable with the box term and works better. The loss is then computed on the matched pairs:

$$\mathcal L_\text{Hungarian} = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(-w_i\log\hat p_{\hat\sigma(i)}(c_i) + [c_i\ne\varnothing]\,\mathcal L_\text{box}\big(b_i, \hat b_{\hat\sigma(i)}\big)\Big),\qquad \mathcal L_\text{box} = \lambda_\text{iou}\,\mathcal L_\text{GIoU} + \lambda_{L1}\,\lVert b - \hat b\rVert_1,$$

with \(w_i = 1\) for objects and 0.1 for ∅, a down-weighting for class imbalance in the spirit of Day 87, and \(\lambda_\text{iou} = 2\), \(\lambda_{L1} = 5\), normalized by the number of objects. L1 alone is scale-dependent (the same relative error costs more on a large box); generalized IoU (Rezatofighi et al., 2019) is not. The Hungarian algorithm (Kuhn, 1955) solves the assignment exactly in cubic time, via scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment in DETR's code. Brute force is hopeless: 7 objects in 100 slots allow \(100!/93! \approx 8\times10^{13}\) assignments.

Worked example · matching beats greedy

Two objects, three slots, illustrative matching costs (lower is better):

Ground truthSlot 1Slot 2Slot 3
cat0.20.31.5
dog0.41.21.4

Greedy: the cat takes its cheapest slot, 1 (0.2), leaving the dog slot 2 (1.2): total 1.4. The optimum over all six assignments, which linear_sum_assignment returns, is cat → slot 2, dog → slot 1: 0.3 + 0.4 = 0.7. Slot 1 was the best cat in isolation but is trained as the dog, and slot 3, whatever it predicted, is trained toward ∅.

Derivation · why one-to-one matching removes NMS
  1. Permutation invariance. The loss takes the best assignment over all orderings, so no arbitrary order (say, objects sorted by position) is imposed, and a correct set is never penalized for being listed differently.
  2. Duplicates are penalized. Each object is matched to exactly one slot. If two slots predict the same cat, one is matched to it and the other to ∅, and is pushed toward “no object”. Under anchor assignment both would be positives. The target itself now says: one prediction per object.
  3. The model can comply, because slots communicate. Self-attention among the decoder's queries lets each slot condition on what the others predict. Without it, two slots looking at one object could not agree which of them takes it.

The paper tests this directly. NMS applied to the first decoder layer's predictions improves AP, because one layer has had no chance to resolve duplicates; the benefit shrinks with depth, and at the last layers NMS slightly hurts by removing true positives.

Auxiliary decoding losses are load-bearing. Shared prediction heads (plus a shared layer norm) follow every decoder layer, each with its own matching and loss. The authors found them helpful especially for predicting the right number of objects per class, and they make every layer's output a detection set, which the per-layer NMS analysis relies on. Object queries specialize. Figure 7 plots the boxes of 20 of the 100 slots over the validation set: each slot has a few modes of position and size, and nearly all have one for large, image-wide boxes.

Evidence

Against a tuned baseline. The authors retrained Faster R-CNN with FPN using DETR's ingredients (GIoU, random crops, a 9× schedule), which the paper says adds one to two AP: the tune-the-baseline theme of Day 78. DETR with ResNet-50 reaches about 42 AP on COCO validation, on par with that baseline at similar parameter count and lower reported FLOPs. The breakdown is the story: clearly better on large objects, which the authors attribute to global attention, clearly worse on small ones. Dilating the last stage (DC5) helps small objects at a large cost in compute.

Ablations and probes. Removing the encoder costs several AP, most on large objects; decoder attention lands on object extremities; GIoU carries most of the box loss's value. Given 24 giraffes, more than any training image holds, DETR finds them all. For panoptic segmentation a head upsamples each object's attention map with an FPN-like CNN (Day 88), competitive with Panoptic FPN (Day 96).

What “no hand-designed components” means. The detector is a network and a loss, with no anchors, thresholds, proposals or NMS, which is why panoptic needed only one more head and why inference fits in under 50 lines of PyTorch. It is not design-free: \(N\), the matching weights (1, 5, 2), the ∅ weight and the schedule are hand-chosen. The choices moved from post-processing into the objective.

Limits & what broke
  • Hundreds of epochs. The best models trained for 500 epochs (300 for ablations, about three days on 16 V100 GPUs), against a dozen to a few dozen for standard Faster R-CNN schedules. Cross-attention starts nearly uniform over all \(H\cdot W\) positions and must learn to focus.
  • Small objects. One stride-32 map, because encoder attention is quadratic in \(H\cdot W\). Deformable DETR (Zhu et al., ICLR 2021) fixed both: each query attends to a few sampled points on several pyramid levels, Day 79's offsets on Day 88's pyramid, with better small-object accuracy in ten times fewer epochs.
  • One-to-one supervision is sparse. Each object trains one query, and early matches flip between slots. DN-DETR added denoising queries, DAB-DETR made queries explicit anchor boxes (anchors returned in a new form), and DINO (Zhang et al., 2022; not the DINO of Day 120) combined such ideas to put a DETR-style detector at the top of COCO.
