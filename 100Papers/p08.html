Generative Models: VAEs, GANs, and Diffusion

Thirty papers on one question, how to learn a distribution you can sample from, answered by latent-variable models, adversarial games, autoregressive and flow models, and the diffusion models that overtook them all. The month leans on the score-function estimator from the reinforcement-learning month and on the transformer, keeps one tradeoff in view throughout (sample quality, mode coverage, sampling speed), and ends with the latent diffusion transformers that next month's video generators build on.

By the end of this month you can

  • Derive the ELBO both from Jensen's inequality and as \(\log p(x) - \KL(q\,\|\,p(z\mid x))\), derive the closed-form Gaussian KL, and explain why reparameterization gives lower-variance gradients than the score-function estimator.
  • Derive the optimal discriminator and the \(2\,\mathrm{JSD} - \log 4\) value of the GAN game, and use the parallel-lines example to show why the Wasserstein distance still gives gradients when Jensen–Shannon does not.
  • Compute FID from Inception features, name Inception Score's failure modes, and say what must accompany any reported FID (sample count, feature extractor, resizing code).
  • Derive DDPM's closed form for \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), the posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\), and \(L_{\text{simple}}\) from the bound, and state exactly how \(L_{\text{simple}}\) reweights it.
  • Show that a noise predictor is a rescaled score, derive the probability-flow ODE from the Fokker–Planck equation, and explain how DDIM, consistency models and flow matching each cut the number of sampling steps.
  • Implement classifier-free guidance, convert between the two guidance-scale conventions, and explain why large scales trade diversity for fidelity and eventually oversaturate.
  • Explain the compress-then-model pattern from VQ-VAE to latent diffusion and DiT, including the \(f^2\) compute arithmetic, and why zero-initialized pathways (ControlNet, adaLN-Zero) make adaptation safe.

L241 · DAY 241

Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (VAE)

60m videoLevel 3

Kingma & Welling, ICLR 2014 (arXiv:1312.6114)

A deep latent-variable model becomes trainable with plain backpropagation once you (1) maximize a variational lower bound using one encoder network for every datapoint and (2) write the sampling step as a deterministic function of independent noise, so gradients flow straight through it.

Cold open

One network maps an image to a distribution over a few latent numbers, a second maps them back, and one SGD loop trains both. The paper's 2-D latent manifolds for MNIST and Frey Face sweep smoothly from one digit or expression to the next: a generative model with a likelihood bound, trained like a classifier.

This month asks one question: how do you learn a distribution you can sample from? Keep the triangle below in view for all thirty lessons.

The problem

Take a latent-variable model \(p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\,p_\theta(x \mid z)\) with \(p(z) = \mathcal N(0, I)\) and a neural decoder. Two quantities are intractable: the marginal likelihood \(p_\theta(x) = \int p(z)\,p_\theta(x \mid z)\,dz\), and the posterior \(p_\theta(z \mid x) = p_\theta(x, z)/p_\theta(x)\). EM (Day 19) needs that posterior in its E-step. Mean-field variational Bayes needs closed-form expectations, which a neural decoder destroys. Per-datapoint MCMC is too slow for large datasets. Wake-sleep trains an inference network, but its two phases optimize two different objectives.

Definition · amortized inference

Train one encoder \(q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\phi(x), \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2_\phi(x)\big)\) that outputs the approximate posterior for any \(x\), instead of fitting a separate variational distribution per datapoint. Inference becomes one forward pass.

The method: the ELBO, derived two ways

Derivation 1 · Jensen $$\log p_\theta(x) = \log \int q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\frac{p_\theta(x, z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,dz \;\ge\; \E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x, z) - \log q_\phi(z \mid x)\right] =: \mathcal L(\theta, \phi; x),$$

because \(\log\) is concave, so the log of an average is at least the average of the logs.

Derivation 2 · the exact gap

Since \(p_\theta(x) = p_\theta(x, z)/p_\theta(z \mid x)\) for every \(z\), average over \(q\) and multiply and divide by \(q\):

$$\log p_\theta(x) = \E_q\!\left[\log \frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right] + \E_q\!\left[\log\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)}{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\right] = \mathcal L + \KL\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big).$$

The KL is non-negative, so this recovers the bound and says exactly how loose it is: maximizing \(\mathcal L\) over \(\phi\) pushes \(q\) toward the true posterior; over \(\theta\) it raises the likelihood.

Split \(\log p_\theta(x,z) = \log p_\theta(x\mid z) + \log p(z)\) and the bound takes the form every lesson this month reuses:

$$\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x) = \underbrace{\E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\KL\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularization}}.$$

The reparameterization trick

The \(\theta\)-gradient is easy. The \(\phi\)-gradient is the problem, because \(\phi\) controls the distribution being averaged over. The generic answer is the score-function estimator \(\nabla_\phi \E_q[f(z)] = \E_q[f(z)\,\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z\mid x)]\), the REINFORCE identity of Day 213: unbiased, but it uses only the value of \(f\), never its slope, and the authors call its variance impractically high.

Derivation · moving the randomness

Write the sample as \(z = g_\phi(\varepsilon, x) = \mu_\phi(x) + \sigma_\phi(x) \odot \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\), a distribution that does not depend on \(\phi\). Then

$$\nabla_\phi\, \E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[f(z)] = \nabla_\phi\, \E_{\varepsilon}\big[f(g_\phi(\varepsilon, x))\big] = \E_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_z f(z)\big|_{z = g_\phi(\varepsilon,x)}\;\nabla_\phi g_\phi(\varepsilon, x)\big].$$

The expectation no longer depends on \(\phi\), so the gradient moves inside, and it now uses \(\nabla_z f\), the slope of the decoder's log-likelihood. That extra information is why the variance drops.

That substitution is the paper's technical contribution. The resulting estimate of \(\mathcal L\) is the SGVB estimator; the training loop is AEVB. One sample of \(\varepsilon\) per datapoint suffices, the authors report, with minibatches of 100.

Derivation · the Gaussian KL in closed form

For \(q = \mathcal N(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2)\) and \(p = \mathcal N(0, I)\) in \(J\) dimensions, use \(\E_q[(z_j-\mu_j)^2] = \sigma_j^2\) and \(\E_q[z_j^2] = \mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2\):

$$\E_q[\log q] = -\tfrac12\sum_j\big(\log 2\pi + \log\sigma_j^2 + 1\big),\qquad \E_q[\log p] = -\tfrac12\sum_j\big(\log 2\pi + \mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2\big),$$ $$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \E_q[\log q] - \E_q[\log p] = \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^{J}\big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - \log\sigma_j^2 - 1\big).$$

It is zero exactly when \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\). Only the reconstruction term needs sampling.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def negative_elbo(encoder, decoder, x):
    mu, logvar = encoder(x)                     # q(z|x) = N(mu, diag(exp(logvar)))
    eps = torch.randn_like(mu)                  # the only random number, an input
    z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps      # reparameterized sample
    logits = decoder(z)                         # Bernoulli decoder (binarized MNIST)
    rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction="sum")
    kl = 0.5 * torch.sum(mu.pow(2) + logvar.exp() - logvar - 1)
    return (rec + kl) / x.shape[0]              # minimize this

Evidence

The experiments are small: MNIST and Frey Face, MLP encoder and decoder, Bernoulli outputs for MNIST and Gaussian outputs for the faces. Against wake-sleep, the authors report that AEVB reached better lower bounds faster at every latent size tried, and that extra latent dimensions did not overfit, which they credit to the KL term. For small latents, estimated marginal likelihoods also favoured AEVB over wake-sleep and Monte Carlo EM. Not shown: sample quality on natural images, or behaviour with expressive decoders. Both came later, and both went badly at first.

Why samples are blurry. With a fixed-variance Gaussian decoder, \(\log p_\theta(x\mid z) = -\|x - \mu_\theta(z)\|^2/2s^2 + \text{const}\), so the best decoder mean is the average of all images consistent with \(z\), and detail the latent does not pin down is averaged away. Maximum likelihood is mean-seeking, and people display \(\mu_\theta(z)\) rather than a noisy sample. The blur comes from this likelihood choice, not from the latent-variable idea: hierarchical VAEs (NVAE, VDVAE), VQ-VAE with a learned prior (Day 243) and Stable Diffusion's autoencoder (Day 264) are all sharp.

Limits & what broke
  • Posterior collapse. With a powerful autoregressive decoder the cheapest optimum is \(q_\phi(z\mid x) = p(z)\): KL zero, \(z\) ignored. Bowman et al. (2016) hit this on text and proposed KL annealing (ramp the KL weight from 0 to 1). Kingma et al. (2016) proposed free bits: replace each group's KL by \(\max(\lambda, \KL_j)\), so pushing it below \(\lambda\) nats earns nothing.
  • A diagonal Gaussian posterior is crude. It cannot match a curved or multimodal posterior, and the gap is exactly that KL. Flows in the encoder (Day 258) and importance-weighted bounds (IWAE, Burda et al. 2016) tighten it.
  • Amortization gap. One encoder for all datapoints can be worse than per-datapoint optimization (Cremer et al. 2018).
  • Priority. Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra derived the same estimator independently (ICML 2014). Credit both.

Bridge

The ELBO is the object this month keeps returning to: a diffusion model's training loss is a VAE bound with a fixed, many-layered encoder (Day 259, Day 260). On the triangle, the 2014 VAE has coverage and one-pass sampling but weak samples. Tomorrow (Day 242) turns one knob, the weight on the KL term, and asks whether the latents become interpretable. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 19 (EM and the posterior), ⟵ Day 213 (the score-function estimator).

β-VAE and the Disentanglement Debate

40m videoLevel 3

Higgins et al. ICLR 2017; Locatello et al. ICML 2019 (arXiv:1811.12359)

Turning up the weight on a VAE's KL term makes its latents line up with interpretable factors on simple datasets. Two years later, Locatello et al. proved that without inductive biases or supervision this kind of disentanglement is not identifiable, and showed empirically that seeds and hyperparameters mattered more than the method.

Cold open

Fix an image, encode it, then sweep one latent coordinate from −3 to 3 while holding the others still. In the β-VAE paper's traversals, one coordinate rotates a 3D chair while another changes its leg style; on CelebA faces, one changes skin colour. Nobody labelled these factors. The method was one hyperparameter. That is why the paper caught on, and why the follow-up that questioned it mattered so much.

The problem

A plain VAE (Day 241) has no reason to give each latent coordinate a single meaning: any rotation of the latent space fits the data equally well. InfoGAN (Chen et al. 2016) obtained some disentanglement by maximizing mutual information between a latent subset and the output, but GAN training made it unstable. Earlier methods (such as DC-IGN) needed some knowledge of the factors. Higgins et al. wanted something unsupervised, stable and simple.

The method: one weight on the KL

The β-VAE objective is the ELBO with the regularization term scaled by \(\beta\):

$$\mathcal L_\beta = \E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big] - \beta\,\KL\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big),\qquad \beta > 1.$$

The paper motivates it as a constrained problem: maximize reconstruction subject to \(\KL(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)) < \epsilon\). Its Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L_\beta\) plus a constant, with \(\beta\) the KKT multiplier. A tighter constraint means a larger \(\beta\).

Derivation · the information-bottleneck reading

Let \(q(z) = \E_{p_{\text{data}}(x)}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]\) be the aggregate posterior. Insert \(q(z)\) into the averaged KL:

$$\E_x \KL\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) = \E_x\E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log\frac{q_\phi(z\mid x)}{q(z)} + \log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right] = I_q(x; z) + \KL\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z)\big).$$

So the KL term upper-bounds the mutual information between data and code. Raising \(\beta\) narrows the channel through which \(x\) can reach the decoder: an information bottleneck (⟵ Day 320). Burgess et al. (2018) argue that, with an isotropic factorized prior, the cheapest way to send a few bits is along separate axes, one factor per axis, and propose raising a target capacity \(C\) gradually.

Measuring it. Higgins et al. also proposed a metric. Using a dataset whose generative factors are known (their 2D shapes data, now called dSprites), fix one factor, draw pairs of images that share it, average the absolute differences of their latent codes, and train a linear classifier to predict which factor was fixed. High accuracy means the fixed factor is visible in a small, consistent set of coordinates.

The tradeoff. The paper's sweeps show disentanglement scores rising with \(\beta\) up to a point while reconstructions get blurrier, because a narrower channel carries less detail. They normalize \(\beta\) by latent and input size so values are comparable across architectures. Later metrics followed: FactorVAE (Kim & Mnih 2018), which penalizes the total correlation of \(q(z)\) with a discriminator and has its own majority-vote metric; DIP-VAE (Kumar et al. 2018), which matches the covariance of \(q(z)\) to the prior and introduced SAP; and β-TCVAE (Chen et al. 2018), which introduced MIG, the gap in mutual information between the two latents most informative about each factor.

The other half: Locatello et al.

Theorem · non-identifiability (Locatello et al. 2019, informal)

Let \(z\) have a factorized density \(p(z) = \prod_i p(z_i)\) in \(d > 1\) dimensions. Then there are infinitely many bijections \(f\) such that \(f(z)\) has exactly the same distribution as \(z\), yet every coordinate of \(f(z)\) depends on every coordinate of \(z\).

For a standard Gaussian prior a rotation is such an \(f\). So if a generative model \(x = g(z)\) is perfectly disentangled, the model \(x = g(f^{-1}(\tilde z))\) with \(\tilde z = f(z)\) produces the identical distribution over \(x\) from an entangled code with the same prior. A method that sees only samples of \(x\) cannot prefer one over the other. Any success must come from inductive biases in the model and training, or from supervision.

Evidence

Higgins et al. compared β-VAE with a plain VAE, InfoGAN and DC-IGN on dSprites, 3D chairs, 3D faces and CelebA. On dSprites their metric favoured β-VAE, and the traversals look cleaner than the baselines'. The evidence is mostly qualitative plus one metric they designed, on synthetic data with a handful of independent factors.

Locatello et al. trained more than 12,000 models: six methods (β-VAE, AnnealedVAE, FactorVAE, β-TCVAE and two DIP-VAE variants), six metrics, seven datasets built around known factors. The authors report that: the methods do make the sampled representation's aggregate posterior factorize, though the mean representation used for evaluation can stay correlated; random seeds and hyperparameters matter more than the choice of method, with no method consistently best; good hyperparameters do not transfer reliably across datasets, and without ground-truth labels there is no way to select a well-disentangled model; the metrics agree only partly, to a degree that depends on the dataset; and they found no evidence that more disentangled representations reduce the sample complexity of downstream tasks. That last point is the sobering measurement lesson: a metric can be optimized for years without being shown to predict usefulness.

Limits & what broke
  • Toy data. dSprites-style datasets have a few independent, known factors. Real data has correlated factors, so "one axis per factor" is not even well defined.
  • Metrics need ground truth. Every disentanglement metric requires the true factors, which is exactly what the unsupervised setting lacks.
  • Read the theorem precisely. It does not say disentanglement is impossible. It says it is not identifiable from \(p(x)\) alone. Weak supervision (pairs of images known to differ in a few factors; Locatello et al. 2020) and auxiliary variables (identifiable VAEs, Khemakhem et al. 2020) restore identifiability under assumptions.
  • The cost. On the month's triangle, \(\beta > 1\) moves a VAE further from the sample-quality corner: blurrier reconstructions in exchange for structure.

Bridge

Both papers tug on the same KL term from Day 241. Tomorrow's paper removes it as a free parameter altogether: with discrete latents and a uniform prior the KL is a constant, posterior collapse disappears, and the prior can be an autoregressive model (Day 243). Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 241, ⟵ Day 320 (forward reference: the information bottleneck in depth).

L243 · DAY 243

Neural Discrete Representation Learning (VQ-VAE)

45m videoLevel 3

van den Oord, Vinyals & Kavukcuoglu, NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1711.00937)

Replace the Gaussian latent with a nearest-neighbour lookup into a learned codebook, push gradients past the lookup with the straight-through estimator, and two things follow: the KL term becomes a constant, so posterior collapse cannot happen, and the discrete codes can be modelled afterwards by a powerful autoregressive prior.

Cold open

A 128×128 ImageNet image goes in. What comes out of the encoder is a 32×32 grid of integers, each one an index into a codebook of 512 vectors. The decoder rebuilds a recognizable image from those indices alone. Fit a PixelCNN to the grids, sample new grids, decode them, and you have a generative model whose expensive part works on about a thousand tokens instead of about fifty thousand subpixels.

The problem

Day 241 ended with posterior collapse: give a VAE a strong autoregressive decoder and it learns to ignore \(z\). Discrete latents are also a natural fit for language, speech and symbolic structure, but earlier discrete VAEs trained with score-function estimators (NVIL, VIMCO) or continuous relaxations had high-variance gradients and lagged their continuous cousins. The paper wants discrete codes that train as smoothly as an autoencoder.

The method: quantize, then lie about the gradient

Definition · vector quantization layer

The encoder outputs \(z_e(x) \in \R^D\) at each spatial position. A codebook \(e_1, \dots, e_K \in \R^D\) is learned. The posterior is one-hot on the nearest code:

$$q(z = k \mid x) = \begin{cases}1 & k = \argmin_j \|z_e(x) - e_j\|_2\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\qquad z_q(x) = e_k.$$

This is k-means assignment (⟵ Day 20) inside a network.

Derivation · why the KL is constant

With a uniform prior \(p(z = k) = 1/K\) and the one-hot posterior above,

$$\KL(q\,\|\,p) = \sum_k q(k\mid x)\log\frac{q(k\mid x)}{1/K} = 1\cdot\log\frac{1}{1/K} = \log K.$$

The regularizer no longer depends on any parameter, so it drops out of training. There is no term that rewards making \(q\) uninformative, which is why the decoder cannot collapse the posterior.

The straight-through estimator. The map \(z_e \mapsto z_q\) is piecewise constant: its true Jacobian is zero almost everywhere and undefined on the boundaries. VQ-VAE ignores this. In the backward pass it copies the decoder's gradient with respect to \(z_q\) and uses it as the gradient with respect to \(z_e\), treating the quantizer as the identity. That is the faked gradient, stated exactly: \(\partial z_q/\partial z_e := I\). It is a lie that works because \(z_q\) is close to \(z_e\) (the commitment term keeps it so), so the decoder's gradient at \(z_q\) is a reasonable guess at its gradient at \(z_e\).

Objective · three terms, three things moved $$L = -\log p\big(x \mid z_q(x)\big) + \big\|\operatorname{sg}[z_e(x)] - e\big\|_2^2 + \beta\,\big\|z_e(x) - \operatorname{sg}[e]\big\|_2^2,$$

where \(\operatorname{sg}\) is stop-gradient. Reconstruction trains the decoder, and the encoder through the straight-through copy. Codebook moves each selected \(e\) toward the encoder outputs assigned to it, a k-means step by gradient. Commitment moves the encoder toward its chosen code, so its outputs cannot drift or grow without bound while the codebook chases them. The paper uses \(\beta = 0.25\) and reports that results were not very sensitive to it.

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def vector_quantize(z_e, codebook, beta=0.25):
    """z_e: (N, D) encoder outputs; codebook: (K, D) learnable tensor."""
    idx = torch.cdist(z_e, codebook).argmin(dim=1)   # non-differentiable lookup
    z_q = codebook[idx]
    codebook_loss = F.mse_loss(z_q, z_e.detach())     # moves the codes
    commit_loss = F.mse_loss(z_e, z_q.detach())       # moves the encoder
    z_st = z_e + (z_q - z_e).detach()                 # value z_q, gradient to z_e
    return z_st, idx, codebook_loss + beta * commit_loss

The line z_e + (z_q - z_e).detach() is the whole straight-through estimator.

Codebook updates by EMA. An appendix gives an alternative to the codebook loss: keep exponential moving averages of the count and the sum of encoder outputs assigned to each code, and set each code to their ratio, an online k-means. The paper suggests a decay of 0.99. Many later implementations use this variant.

Two-stage generation. Train the VQ-VAE with a uniform prior. Then freeze it, encode the training set to index grids, and fit an autoregressive prior over them: a PixelCNN for images (Day 257), a WaveNet for audio (Day 256). Sample indices from the prior and decode.

Evidence

On CIFAR-10 the authors report a likelihood bound in bits per dimension close to a continuous VAE's and better than a VIMCO-trained discrete model, the first discrete model they know of to approach the continuous one. ImageNet reconstructions from the 32×32×1, K = 512 grid preserve most of the image, and class-conditional samples from the PixelCNN prior are recognizable but clearly below GAN quality. On speech (VCTK), the codes capture content: decoding the same codes with a different speaker embedding performs voice conversion, and the authors report that the discrete codes align with phonemes far above chance with no supervision. Video experiments on DeepMind Lab generate action-conditional rollouts entirely in latent space. What is missing: a sample-quality comparison against GANs on equal terms; that came with the hierarchical VQ-VAE-2 (Razavi et al. 2019).

Limits & what broke
  • Codebook collapse. Codes that win no assignments get no updates and stay dead, so effective codebook size shrinks. Later fixes include restarting dead codes at random encoder outputs (used in Jukebox, Dhariwal et al. 2020), low-dimensional and normalized lookups (ViT-VQGAN, Yu et al. 2022), and replacing the learned codebook with fixed rounding (finite scalar quantization, Mentzer et al. 2024).
  • The estimator is biased. Straight-through is not the gradient of anything. It works when quantization error is small, and gets worse as codes spread apart.
  • Still a pixel loss. The decoder is trained with MSE, so fine texture is still averaged away; the fix is tomorrow's adversarial and perceptual losses.
  • Sampling cost moved, not removed. The prior is still autoregressive over the grid: 1,024 sequential steps for 32×32.

Bridge

Discrete latents make an autoregressive prior tractable, which is the architectural reason this design keeps returning: DALL·E's dVAE tokens (Day 265) became BEiT's prediction targets (⟵ Day 119), and neural audio codecs quantize speech the same way (⟶ Day 283). Tomorrow's VQGAN (Day 244) keeps this two-stage plan but swaps the MSE decoder for a perceptual and adversarial one, and the PixelCNN prior for a transformer. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 241, ⟵ Day 20.

Taming Transformers for High-Resolution Image Synthesis (VQGAN)

40m videoLevel 3

Esser, Rombach & Ommer, CVPR 2021 (arXiv:2012.09841)

Let a convolutional autoencoder, trained with adversarial and perceptual losses, compress an image into a short grid of codebook indices, and let a transformer model how those indices compose. Realism in the decoder buys a compression ratio high enough that a transformer prior becomes affordable.

Cold open

Landscapes from rough semantic layouts, scenes from depth maps, faces at high resolution, all sampled by a transformer one token at a time, at sizes up to the megapixel range. Pixel-level transformers of the time (Image GPT, Chen et al. 2020) worked at 64×64 or below. The gap was not a better transformer. It was a better tokenizer.

The problem

Transformers have no locality bias and attention costs grow with the square of the sequence length. A 256×256 RGB image is 196,608 subpixels, hopeless as a sequence. VQ-VAE (Day 243) shortens it, but with an MSE decoder you can only compress so far before reconstructions turn to mush: high compression means each code must stand for a large patch, and an L2 loss averages away the texture inside it. CNNs are good at exactly that local texture. Transformers are good at long-range composition. The paper's design gives each architecture the job it is good at.

The method

Stage 1: a perceptually rich codebook. The autoencoder is a VQ-VAE with two changes. The L2 reconstruction loss is replaced by a perceptual loss (LPIPS-style distances in a pretrained network's features, ⟵ Day 253), and a patch-based discriminator \(D\) is added that judges local realism rather than whole images. The objective is

$$\mathcal Q^* = \arg\min_{E, G, \mathcal Z}\;\max_D\;\E_x\Big[\mathcal L_{\text{VQ}}(E, G, \mathcal Z) + \lambda\,\mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}(\{E, G, \mathcal Z\}, D)\Big],$$

where \(\mathcal L_{\text{VQ}}\) holds the reconstruction, codebook and commitment terms of Day 243 and \(\mathcal L_{\text{GAN}} = \log D(x) + \log(1 - D(\hat x))\).

Detail · the adaptive weight λ

The adversarial term's scale is set automatically, by balancing gradient magnitudes at the decoder's last layer \(G_L\):

$$\lambda = \frac{\big\|\nabla_{G_L}\mathcal L_{\text{rec}}\big\|}{\big\|\nabla_{G_L}\mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}\big\| + \delta},\qquad \delta = 10^{-6}.$$

If the GAN gradient grows large relative to the reconstruction gradient, \(\lambda\) shrinks, and vice versa. This removes a hand-tuned weight that otherwise differs per dataset.

Stage 2: a transformer over indices. Freeze stage 1. Encode each training image to a grid \(s \in \{0,\dots,|\mathcal Z|-1\}^{h \times w}\), flatten it in raster order, and train a GPT-style transformer (⟵ Day 129) on \(p(s) = \prod_i p(s_i \mid s_{<i})\). Conditioning is just more tokens: a class label, or the code grid of a segmentation map or depth map produced by a second VQGAN, is prepended to the sequence.

The arithmetic that matters. With downsampling factor \(f\), a 256×256 image becomes a \((256/f)^2\) grid. Attention cost per layer scales with the square of that length:

That is only worth doing if the decoder can still produce a realistic image from so few codes, and that is what the adversarial and perceptual losses provide: they let the decoder invent plausible texture instead of reproducing exact pixels.

Beyond the training size. For megapixel outputs the transformer is applied in a sliding window: each new token attends only to a fixed-size window of nearby tokens (16×16 in the paper's setup), so cost grows linearly with image area. This works best when the conditioning (a layout, a depth map) carries the global structure.

Evidence

The paper's ablations make the case qualitatively and with FID. Comparing first stages at matched compression, the authors report that VQGAN's reconstructions stay far better than a VQ-VAE trained with pixel losses, and that a transformer on VQGAN codes beats transformers on pixels or on lower-compression codes within the same compute. A sweep over \(f\) on faces shows the tradeoff directly: small \(f\) keeps detail but the transformer's context covers too little of the image to compose a coherent face, while larger \(f\) gives coherent global structure. They demonstrate class-conditional ImageNet, semantic and depth-conditioned synthesis, super-resolution and pose-guided generation with one recipe. What it does not show: that autoregressive order is a good inductive bias for images, which later work doubted.

Limits & what broke
  • Raster order. Token \(i\) sees only tokens above and to its left, so early decisions cannot be revised and errors propagate downward. Bidirectional masked-token models (MaskGIT, Chang et al. 2022) generate in a handful of parallel steps instead.
  • Slow sampling. 256 sequential transformer calls per 256×256 image, many more with sliding windows. On the triangle: quality and coverage improved, speed got worse.
  • Sampling settings move the numbers. Good samples relied on top-k sampling, and reported ImageNet scores depend on the sampling setup (in some comparisons, classifier-based rejection sampling). Check which setting a number comes from.
  • Codebook usage. Large codebooks are underused, as in Day 243.

Bridge

The two-stage "compress, then model" pattern became the dominant design in generative modelling. Latent diffusion (Day 264) keeps VQGAN's autoencoder recipe almost unchanged and replaces the transformer prior with a diffusion model. Before that, the month turns to the adversarial idea itself: tomorrow is the original GAN (Day 245). Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 243, ⟵ Day 253 (perceptual losses, forward reference), ⟵ Day 129.

Generative Adversarial Networks

55m videoLevel 3

Goodfellow et al., NIPS 2014 (arXiv:1406.2661)

Train a sampler by making it fool a classifier. At the optimal classifier, the game's value is \(2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g) - \log 4\), so the generator is implicitly minimizing Jensen–Shannon divergence, and no likelihood is ever written down.

Cold open

Samples of MNIST digits, faces and CIFAR-10 objects, each shown next to its nearest neighbour in the training set to argue they are not copies. The model that drew them has no density you can evaluate and no inference network. It is a function from noise to images, trained by a second network whose only job is to tell real from fake.

The problem

Deep generative models in 2014 either needed Markov chains or intractable normalizers. Boltzmann machines need MCMC to estimate the partition function's gradient; generative stochastic networks sample by running a chain; the VAE (Day 241, concurrent) needs an inference network and a tractable likelihood. The GAN pitch: sampling is one forward pass, training is backpropagation, and there is no chain and no inference anywhere.

The method: a two-player game

A generator \(G\) maps noise \(z \sim p_z\) to samples, inducing a distribution \(p_g\). A discriminator \(D(x) \in (0,1)\) outputs the probability that \(x\) is real. They play

$$\min_G \max_D\; V(D, G) = \E_{x \sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[\log D(x)\big] + \E_{z \sim p_z}\big[\log\big(1 - D(G(z))\big)\big].$$

Read it term by term: the first term rewards \(D\) for saying "real" on real data; the second rewards \(D\) for saying "fake" on generated data, and \(G\) wants that second term as small as possible. For \(D\) this is exactly binary cross-entropy with labels 1 and 0.

Proof · the optimal discriminator

Fix \(G\). Writing the second expectation over \(x = G(z) \sim p_g\),

$$V(D, G) = \int \Big[p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x) + p_g(x)\log\big(1 - D(x)\big)\Big]\,dx.$$

Maximize the integrand pointwise. For \(a, b \ge 0\), not both zero, \(y \mapsto a\log y + b\log(1-y)\) has derivative \(a/y - b/(1-y)\), zero at \(y = a/(a+b)\), and is concave, so that is the maximum. Hence

$$D^*_G(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)}.$$
Proof · the generator minimizes JSD

Substitute \(D^*\) and let \(m = \tfrac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)\). Since \(\frac{p}{p+q} = \frac{p}{2m} \), each log splits into \(\log\frac{p}{m} - \log 2\):

$$C(G) = \max_D V = \E_{p_{\text{data}}}\!\left[\log\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{2m}\right] + \E_{p_g}\!\left[\log\frac{p_g}{2m}\right] = \KL(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,m) + \KL(p_g\,\|\,m) - \log 4.$$

With \(\mathrm{JSD}(p\|q) = \tfrac12\KL(p\|m) + \tfrac12\KL(q\|m)\), this is \(C(G) = 2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g) - \log 4\). JSD is non-negative and zero only when the two distributions are equal, so the global minimum \(-\log 4\) is attained exactly at \(p_g = p_{\text{data}}\), where \(D^* \equiv \tfrac12\).

Algorithm · minibatch GAN training (paper's Algorithm 1)
  1. For \(k\) steps: sample \(m\) noise vectors and \(m\) real examples; update \(D\) by ascending \(\nabla \frac1m\sum_i[\log D(x_i) + \log(1 - D(G(z_i)))]\).
  2. Sample \(m\) noise vectors; update \(G\) by descending \(\nabla \frac1m\sum_i \log(1 - D(G(z_i)))\).
  3. Repeat. The authors used \(k = 1\), the cheapest option.

Theory versus practice, honestly. The convergence argument (the paper's Proposition 2) assumes \(D\) is optimal before every generator step and that \(G\) updates \(p_g\) directly, where the objective is convex. Neither holds. With \(k = 1\), alternating single gradient steps are not solving the inner maximization; they are a dynamical system with its own behaviour. The paper says this itself: in practice one optimizes network parameters, not \(p_g\).

The non-saturating loss

Early in training, \(G\) is bad and \(D\) rejects its samples with confidence: the logit \(a\) on a fake sample is very negative. Then \(\log(1 - \sigma(a))\) is flat, with derivative \(-\sigma(a) \to 0\), and the generator receives almost no gradient. The paper's fix is to have \(G\) maximize \(\log D(G(z))\) instead, whose derivative with respect to the logit is \(-(1 - \sigma(a)) \to -1\).

The non-saturating loss changes the objective, so the JSD proof no longer strictly applies. Arjovsky and Bottou (2017) showed that, at the optimal discriminator, its gradient is that of \(\KL(p_g\,\|\,p_{\text{data}}) - 2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g)\): a reverse-KL-flavoured objective that punishes fake samples where there is no data, but barely punishes missing modes. Practitioners use it anyway because it trains.

Non-convergence and mode collapse

Gradient play on a game need not converge even when an equilibrium exists. The smallest example is \(\min_x \max_y xy\), with equilibrium \((0,0)\):

import math

def play(alternating, eta=0.1, steps=200):
    x, y = 1.0, 1.0                  # min over x, max over y of f(x, y) = x * y
    for _ in range(steps):
        gx, gy = y, x                # df/dx, df/dy
        x = x - eta * gx             # minimizer descends
        if alternating:
            gy = x                   # maximizer sees the new x
        y = y + eta * gy             # maximizer ascends
    return math.hypot(x, y)

print(play(False))   # about 3.83: simultaneous updates spiral outward
print(play(True))    # about 1.36: alternating updates orbit, never converge

Simultaneous steps multiply the radius by \(\sqrt{1 + \eta^2}\) each time. Alternating steps are a rotation with determinant 1, so they cycle forever around the equilibrium.

Mode collapse is the paper's "Helvetica scenario": if \(G\) is trained too much without updating \(D\), it maps many values of \(z\) to the same output. The authors flag it as the main practical disadvantage, alongside the need to keep \(D\) and \(G\) in step.

Evidence

The paper trains MLP and convolutional GANs on MNIST, the Toronto Face Database and CIFAR-10. Since a GAN has no likelihood, the authors fit a Gaussian Parzen window to generated samples and report test log-likelihood under it, finding GANs competitive with deep belief nets, stacked contractive autoencoders and GSNs. They state plainly that this estimator has high variance and works poorly in high dimensions. The convincing evidence was the samples and the smooth interpolations in \(z\), not the table. This is what "implicit generative model" means: you can sample, but you cannot evaluate \(p_g(x)\), so there is no perplexity or bits/dim to report, and evaluation stayed a mess until Inception Score and FID (Day 247).

Limits & what broke
  • JSD saturates. If \(p_{\text{data}}\) and \(p_g\) have disjoint supports, as low-dimensional image manifolds typically do, JSD is the constant \(\log 2\) and a near-optimal \(D\) gives the generator no useful gradient. This is the argument of Day 248.
  • Parzen-window scores are meaningless. Theis, van den Oord and Bethge (2016) showed they can rank models badly; the community stopped reporting them.
  • Instability. Fully connected GANs trained, but convolutional ones were notoriously fragile until the architectural rules of tomorrow's paper.
  • No encoder. There is no way to map an image back to \(z\). BiGAN and ALI (both 2016) added one.

Tomorrow: the checklist that made convolutional GANs train.

Unsupervised Representation Learning with Deep Convolutional GANs (DCGAN)

35m videoLevel 2

Radford, Metz & Chintala, ICLR 2016 (arXiv:1511.06434)

A short list of architectural rules, found by search rather than derived, made convolutional GANs train reliably across datasets, and the resulting models turned out to have latent spaces you can do arithmetic in and discriminators whose features transfer to classification.

Cold open

64×64 bedrooms from LSUN, generated after a single pass over the data. Then the picture everyone remembers: average the latent vectors of three "smiling woman" samples, subtract three "neutral woman", add three "neutral man", decode, and get smiling men. A year earlier, convolutional GANs mostly failed to train at all.

The problem

The original GAN (Day 245) worked with small MLPs. Scaling it up with CNNs, the natural architecture for images, had been largely unsuccessful; LAPGAN (Denton et al. 2015) got around it by generating a Laplacian pyramid with a separate conditional GAN per scale. Nobody had a single convolutional recipe that trained stably on several datasets, let alone one whose internals were worth studying.

The method: the checklist

Algorithm · DCGAN architecture guidelines
  1. Replace pooling with strided convolutions in \(D\) and fractionally-strided (transposed) convolutions in \(G\): let each network learn its own resampling.
  2. Use batch normalization in both networks, except at \(G\)'s output layer and \(D\)'s input layer.
  3. Remove fully connected hidden layers. \(G\)'s first layer is a single matrix multiply from \(z\) reshaped into a 4×4 feature map; \(D\)'s last conv layer is flattened into one sigmoid output.
  4. ReLU in \(G\) everywhere except the output, which uses tanh.
  5. LeakyReLU in \(D\) everywhere (slope 0.2).

Training details matter as much as the list: images scaled to \([-1, 1]\) to match tanh, minibatches of 128, weights initialized from \(\mathcal N(0, 0.02^2)\), Adam with learning rate 0.0002 and \(\beta_1 = 0.5\). The authors report that the default \(\beta_1 = 0.9\) caused oscillation and instability.

Honest framing. These rules are not derived from theory. The paper says they came from extensive model exploration, and presents them as what worked. The batch-norm exception is the clearest example: applying BN to every layer, the authors report, caused sample oscillation and instability, which went away when BN was dropped from \(G\)'s output and \(D\)'s input. Teach the list as an empirical result, not a principle.

What the latent space shows

Vector arithmetic. Inspired by word-vector analogies, the authors take latent vectors \(z\) whose samples show a concept and combine them. A detail usually cropped from slides: arithmetic on single samples was unstable, so they averaged the \(z\) vectors of three exemplars per concept before adding and subtracting. With averaging, "smiling woman − neutral woman + neutral man" and "man with glasses − man without glasses + woman without glasses" produce consistent results.

Walking the manifold. Interpolating between two random \(z\) vectors in the bedroom model gives smooth transitions: in the paper's figure, a room without a window gradually gains a large one, and a TV slowly turns into a window. The authors use this against the memorization objection. A model that had memorized training images would show sharp jumps between memorized points; semantic changes that happen gradually suggest it has learned relevant structure. They add that after one epoch with a small learning rate the model is unlikely to have memorized, and they deduplicated LSUN with an autoencoder-based hashing step to reduce the chance of memorized near-copies.

Peeking inside. Guided backpropagation shows \(D\) features that fire on beds and windows. The authors locate \(G\) filters that draw windows and drop them; the generator then fills the space with other objects. That is direct evidence the generator has object-level internal structure.

Evidence

As a representation learner, \(D\) is trained on ImageNet-1k with no labels; its convolutional features from every layer are pooled to small grids, flattened and fed to a linear L2-SVM. The authors report CIFAR-10 accuracy competitive with the strongest unsupervised feature learners of the time (k-means based pipelines), and strong results on SVHN with only 1,000 labels. This was a self-supervised result before the term was in common use. Sample-quality evidence is mostly visual: LSUN bedrooms, faces scraped from the web, ImageNet-1k at 32×32. There was still no agreed sample-quality metric.

Limits & what broke
  • Still unstable when trained longer. The paper's own conclusion notes that longer training sometimes collapses a subset of filters to a single oscillating mode.
  • Batch norm in D couples samples. A sample's output depends on the rest of its minibatch, which hides mode collapse and breaks per-sample penalties. WGAN-GP removes BN from the critic and spectral norm replaces it (Day 249).
  • 64×64 ceiling. Higher resolutions needed progressive growing (Day 250).
  • Arithmetic is fragile. It needs averaging and hand-picked exemplars, and Z is not the most linear space available, which StyleGAN's mapping network addresses (Day 251).

Bridge

DCGAN made GANs trainable; it did not make them measurable. Tomorrow (Day 247) gives the field its first scores, the Inception Score and FID, and a bag of training tricks aimed squarely at mode collapse. On the triangle, DCGAN moved toward quality without touching coverage.

Improved Techniques for Training GANs (+ FID)

40m videoLevel 3

Salimans et al. NIPS 2016 (arXiv:1606.03498); Heusel et al. NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1706.08500)

Salimans et al. gave GAN training a toolkit against mode collapse and the first widely used score, the Inception Score. Heusel et al. replaced that score with FID, which actually compares generated images with real ones, and proved that giving the two players different learning rates converges to a local Nash equilibrium.

Cold open

How do you score a model that has no likelihood? Salimans et al. first asked people: Mechanical Turk annotators judged real versus generated CIFAR-10 images. That does not scale, so they proposed a number computed by an ImageNet classifier and showed it tracked the human judgements. For two years that number, the Inception Score, was how GAN papers were ranked.

The problem

GANs (Day 245) have no density, so no bits/dim, and Parzen-window estimates were meaningless. Training was fragile even with DCGAN's rules (Day 246): gradient descent on a game need not converge, and the generator often collapsed onto a few outputs. The field needed both a thermometer and medicine.

The method: two scores

Definition · Inception Score

With \(p(y\mid x)\) the label distribution of a pretrained Inception network and \(p(y) = \E_{x\sim p_g}[p(y\mid x)]\),

$$\mathrm{IS} = \exp\Big(\E_{x \sim p_g}\,\KL\big(p(y\mid x)\,\|\,p(y)\big)\Big) = \exp\big(H(y) - H(y \mid x)\big).$$

The exponent is the mutual information between image and label: high when each image is classified confidently (low \(H(y\mid x)\)) and labels are spread across classes (high \(H(y)\)). It is at most the number of classes the network distinguishes, about 1,000 for ImageNet.

Four separate failure modes of IS. (1) It never looks at real data: a generator that outputs one perfect image per class, or copies the training set, scores near the maximum. (2) It is blind to diversity within a class, so intra-class mode collapse is invisible. (3) It is tied to ImageNet's 1000 classes and to particular network weights: meaningless for faces or bedrooms, and different implementations give different numbers (Barratt & Sharma 2018). (4) It can be gamed: optimizing images directly against the classifier raises the score with adversarial noise. The first is disqualifying on its own.

Definition · Fréchet Inception Distance

Embed real and generated images with Inception's 2048-dimensional pool features, fit a Gaussian to each set, and compute the Fréchet (2-Wasserstein) distance between the Gaussians:

$$\mathrm{FID} = \underbrace{\|\mu_r - \mu_g\|_2^2}_{\text{wrong content}} + \underbrace{\tr\!\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\big(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g\big)^{1/2}\Big)}_{\text{wrong spread}}.$$
Reading the formula

In one dimension it is \((\mu_r - \mu_g)^2 + \sigma_r^2 + \sigma_g^2 - 2\sigma_r\sigma_g = (\mu_r - \mu_g)^2 + (\sigma_r - \sigma_g)^2\): distance between means plus distance between standard deviations. When the covariances commute, the trace term is \(\sum_i(\sqrt{\lambda_{r,i}} - \sqrt{\lambda_{g,i}})^2\) over shared eigen-directions. Mode collapse shrinks \(\Sigma_g\), so the second term grows: FID sees diversity loss that IS cannot. Lower is better, and zero means the two Gaussians match.

import numpy as np
from scipy import linalg

def fid(feat_real, feat_fake):
    """Rows are Inception pool features (N x 2048) of real and generated images."""
    mu_r, mu_g = feat_real.mean(axis=0), feat_fake.mean(axis=0)
    s_r = np.cov(feat_real, rowvar=False)
    s_g = np.cov(feat_fake, rowvar=False)
    covmean, _ = linalg.sqrtm(s_r @ s_g, disp=False)
    covmean = covmean.real                      # drop tiny imaginary parts from round-off
    return float(np.sum((mu_r - mu_g) ** 2) + np.trace(s_r + s_g - 2 * covmean))

Always report N. The FID estimated from \(N\) samples is biased upward, and the bias depends on the model, so two models can swap rank as \(N\) changes (Bińkowski et al. 2018, who propose the unbiased KID). The convention that emerged is FID-50k: 50,000 generated samples against statistics of the training set. FID is also sensitive to the feature extractor's exact weights and to how images are resized and compressed before feature extraction (Parmar et al. 2022); these reproducibility problems persist, so compare numbers only from the same code.

The method: training tricks

  • Feature matching. Train \(G\) to match the mean of an intermediate discriminator layer: \(\|\E_x f(x) - \E_z f(G(z))\|_2^2\). A weaker but steadier target than fooling \(D\).
  • Minibatch discrimination. Let \(D\) see batch statistics. For features \(f(x_i) \in \R^A\) and a learned tensor \(T \in \R^{A\times B\times C}\), form \(M_i = f(x_i)T \in \R^{B\times C}\), compute \(c_b(x_i, x_j) = \exp(-\|M_{i,b} - M_{j,b}\|_{1})\), and append \(o(x_i)_b = \sum_j c_b(x_i, x_j)\) to \(D\)'s features. A collapsed batch has suspiciously high similarity, which \(D\) can now detect, so this attacks mode collapse directly.
  • Historical averaging. Add \(\|\theta - \frac1t\sum_{i=1}^t\theta_i\|^2\) to each player's cost, discouraging the orbiting behaviour of Day 245's \(xy\) game.
  • One-sided label smoothing. Use target 0.9 for real samples and keep 0 for fakes. Smoothing the fake target \(\beta > 0\) gives \(D^* = (\alpha p_{\text{data}} + \beta p_g)/(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)\), which stays large where \(p_g\) is large and \(p_{\text{data}}\) is not, so \(G\) is never pushed out of those regions. Hence one-sided.
  • Virtual batch normalization. Normalize each example with statistics from a fixed reference batch, removing the dependence on the rest of its own minibatch. Expensive, so used only in \(G\).
Theorem · two time-scale update rule (Heusel et al., informal)

Update \(D\) with step sizes \(b(n)\) and \(G\) with \(a(n)\), both satisfying Robbins–Monro conditions, and with \(a(n)/b(n) \to 0\). Under regularity assumptions, stochastic approximation theory (Borkar 1997) gives convergence to a stationary local Nash equilibrium. Intuition: the fast player effectively sees the slow player as frozen, which restores the "inner problem solved first" structure that alternating equal-rate updates lack. In practice this means simply using a larger learning rate for \(D\) than for \(G\).

Evidence

Salimans et al. report that IS correlated well with Turker judgements on CIFAR-10, and that their tricks gave strong semi-supervised classification results by training \(D\) as a \(K{+}1\)-way classifier on MNIST, SVHN and CIFAR-10. Their ImageNet samples are frank about the limit: recognizable textures and fur, incoherent animal anatomy. Heusel et al. corrupt images with Gaussian noise, blur, black rectangles, swirls, salt-and-pepper noise, and by mixing in ImageNet images, and show that FID rises consistently with the corruption level, while IS behaves inconsistently for some corruptions. They also report that TTUR improved FID over single-rate training across several image datasets.

Limits & what broke
  • FID cannot see memorization. Copying the training set gives an excellent FID against the training set.
  • One number for two corners. FID mixes fidelity and coverage. Precision and recall for generative models (Sajjadi et al. 2018; Kynkäänniemi et al. 2019) separate them, matching two corners of the month's triangle.
  • ImageNet bias. Kynkäänniemi et al. (2023) show FID can be moved by matching ImageNet class statistics without improving images, a problem for faces or medical data.
  • The tricks are partial. Feature matching helps semi-supervised learning but often gives worse-looking samples; minibatch discrimination is heavier than the one-line minibatch-stddev of Day 250.

Bridge

These are fixes around the loss. Tomorrow's paper (Day 248) argues the loss itself is wrong: Jensen–Shannon divergence gives no signal when supports do not overlap, and the Wasserstein distance does. Every later result this month, diffusion included, is ranked by FID, so keep its caveats in mind.

Wasserstein GAN

50m videoLevel 4

Arjovsky, Chintala & Bottou, ICML 2017 (arXiv:1701.07875)

When real and generated distributions sit on disjoint low-dimensional manifolds, Jensen–Shannon and KL are constant or infinite and give the generator nothing to follow. The Earth Mover's distance stays continuous, and Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality turns it into "maximize over 1-Lipschitz functions", which a neural network can approximately do.

Cold open

A standard GAN's discriminator loss tells you almost nothing about whether samples are getting better. The WGAN paper's central plot shows its critic's loss falling steadily during training as LSUN bedroom samples go from noise to bedrooms, across several architectures. For the first time a GAN had a training curve you could read.

The problem

The companion paper (Arjovsky & Bottou, ICLR 2017) made the argument precise. Natural images concentrate near a low-dimensional manifold, and a generator mapping a low-dimensional \(z\) produces a distribution supported on a low-dimensional set too. Two such sets in a high-dimensional space are typically disjoint, or meet in a set of measure zero. Then a perfect discriminator exists, \(\mathrm{JSD} = \log 2\) (Day 245), and the gradient of the original loss vanishes. Disjoint supports are not a pathological corner: they are the normal case for image models, especially early in training.

The method: a distance that does not saturate

Definition · Earth Mover's (Wasserstein-1) distance $$W(P_r, P_g) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Pi(P_r, P_g)}\; \E_{(x, y)\sim\gamma}\big[\|x - y\|\big],$$

where \(\Pi(P_r, P_g)\) is the set of joint distributions (transport plans) with marginals \(P_r\) and \(P_g\). Read it as the minimum cost of moving the mass of one distribution onto the other, cost = mass × distance.

Worked example · the parallel lines

Let \(Z \sim U[0,1]\), let \(P_0\) be the distribution of \((0, Z)\) and \(P_\theta\) that of \((\theta, Z)\): two vertical segments in the plane, \(|\theta|\) apart. For \(\theta \ne 0\) the supports are disjoint, so

  • \(\KL(P_0\|P_\theta) = +\infty\): \(P_0\) has mass where \(P_\theta\) has none.
  • \(\mathrm{JSD}(P_0\|P_\theta) = \log 2\): with \(m = \tfrac12(P_0 + P_\theta)\), on \(P_0\)'s support \(dP_0/dm = 2\), so each KL to \(m\) is \(\log 2\), whatever \(\theta\) is.
  • Total variation is 1.
  • \(W(P_0, P_\theta) = |\theta|\): move each point \((0,z)\) to \((\theta, z)\), cost \(|\theta|\); no plan does better, since every unit of mass must travel at least \(|\theta|\) horizontally.

All four are 0 at \(\theta = 0\). Only \(W\) is continuous there, and only \(W\) has a useful gradient elsewhere.

Theorem · continuity (the paper's Theorem 1, informal)

If \(g_\theta\) is continuous in \(\theta\), then \(W(P_r, P_\theta)\) is continuous in \(\theta\). If \(g_\theta\) is locally Lipschitz (with a mild moment condition on \(z\)), \(W\) is continuous everywhere and differentiable almost everywhere. Neither statement holds for JS or KL.

Making it computable. The infimum over couplings is intractable in high dimensions. The Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality rewrites it as a supremum over functions:

$$W(P_r, P_\theta) = \sup_{\|f\|_L \le 1}\; \E_{x \sim P_r}[f(x)] - \E_{x \sim P_\theta}[f(x)].$$

Over \(K\)-Lipschitz functions instead, the supremum is \(K\cdot W\). So if a network \(f_w\) ranges over some family of \(K\)-Lipschitz functions, maximizing the difference of means estimates \(W\) up to the constant \(K\), and the generator can descend \(-\E_z[\nabla_\theta f_w(g_\theta(z))]\). An intractable transport problem has become "train a network to separate means, subject to a slope limit".

Algorithm · WGAN (paper's defaults)
  1. Repeat \(n_{\text{critic}} = 5\) times: sample a real batch and a noise batch (size 64); ascend \(\frac1m\sum f_w(x_i) - \frac1m\sum f_w(g_\theta(z_i))\) with RMSProp, learning rate \(5\times10^{-5}\); then clip every weight to \([-c, c]\), \(c = 0.01\).
  2. Sample a noise batch; descend \(-\frac1m\sum f_w(g_\theta(z_i))\) with RMSProp.

Three departures from the GAN recipe follow from the theory. No sigmoid and no log: the critic outputs an unbounded score, not a probability. Train the critic toward optimality: because \(W\) is continuous, a better critic gives a better gradient, so there is no need to keep \(D\) weak, the balancing act that made GANs fragile. Clip the weights: weights in a compact box make \(f_w\) Lipschitz for some unknown \(K\). The authors are blunt: "Weight clipping is a clearly terrible way to enforce a Lipschitz constraint." Too large a \(c\) makes the critic slow to train; too small a \(c\) gives vanishing gradients in deep critics.

RMSProp, not Adam. The authors report that WGAN training sometimes became unstable with momentum-based optimizers such as Adam on the critic, or with high learning rates. They observed that when the critic's loss jumped, Adam's update direction became misaligned with the gradient, and attributed it to the critic's objective being nonstationary. RMSProp has no momentum term.

Evidence

On LSUN bedrooms, the critic's estimate of \(W\) decreases as sample quality improves, both for a DCGAN generator and for a plain MLP generator; the matching JS estimate from a standard GAN does not track quality. WGAN also trained architectures that broke standard GANs: a DCGAN generator without batch norm, and an MLP generator. The authors state that they saw no evidence of mode collapse in any WGAN experiment. What the evidence does not show is dramatically better samples than a well-tuned DCGAN; the paper's gains are in stability and in having a meaningful loss curve.

Limits & what broke
  • Clipping wrecks the critic. Gulrajani et al. (Day 249) showed clipped critics push weights to the two clip values and learn overly simple functions, and can still explode or vanish gradients.
  • Slow. Five critic steps per generator step and a small learning rate make WGAN slower to converge than a tuned DCGAN.
  • The estimate is not \(W\). \(K\) is unknown and a finite network is not the optimal critic, so the curve is an informative proxy, not a distance. Later analyses argue WGANs work well even though their critics approximate \(W\) poorly, so "it minimizes Wasserstein distance" is a motivation more than a description.

Bridge

The idea that survived is the Lipschitz critic; the enforcement did not. Tomorrow (Day 249) gives two better ways to constrain slope: a gradient penalty, and dividing each layer by its largest singular value. Optimal transport also returns at the end of the month, as the straight paths of flow matching (Day 269).

WGAN-GP and Spectral Normalization

40m videoLevel 3

Gulrajani et al. NIPS 2017 (arXiv:1704.00028); Miyato et al. ICLR 2018 (arXiv:1802.05957)

Two better ways to keep a critic Lipschitz than clipping its weights: penalize its gradient norm toward 1 at random points between real and fake samples (WGAN-GP), or divide each weight matrix by its largest singular value, estimated with one power-iteration step per update (spectral normalization). The second is nearly free, which is why it won.

Cold open

Gulrajani et al. plot a histogram of a weight-clipped critic's weights: almost all of them sit at the two clip values \(\pm c\). The critic has been reduced to a near-binary network. With a gradient penalty instead, the same critic's weights spread out, and the authors train GANs on a 101-layer ResNet and a character-level language model, settings where earlier GAN losses failed.

The problem

WGAN (Day 248) needs a 1-Lipschitz critic and enforces it by clipping, which the authors themselves called terrible. Clipping biases the critic toward overly simple functions and, depending on \(c\), makes gradients explode or vanish as they pass through a deep critic. The theory was right; the enforcement was crude.

The method: a gradient penalty

Proposition · gradient norm of the optimal critic (Gulrajani et al., informal)

Let \(f^*\) be an optimal 1-Lipschitz critic and \(\pi\) an optimal coupling of \(P_r\) and \(P_g\). If \(f^*\) is differentiable and \(x \ne y\) under \(\pi\), then for a coupled pair (generated \(x\), real \(y\)) and \(x_t = t x + (1-t) y\), \(t \in [0,1]\), with probability 1 the gradient \(\nabla f^*(x_t)\) has norm exactly 1 and points from \(x_t\) toward \(y\).

So rather than constraining weights, constrain the property the optimum has: unit gradient norm along lines joining real and generated points. The critic loss becomes

$$L = \E_{\tilde x\sim P_g}\big[D(\tilde x)\big] - \E_{x\sim P_r}\big[D(x)\big] + \lambda\,\E_{\hat x}\Big[\big(\|\nabla_{\hat x}D(\hat x)\|_2 - 1\big)^2\Big],\qquad \hat x = \epsilon x + (1-\epsilon)\tilde x,\ \epsilon\sim U[0,1].$$

Pairs \((x, \tilde x)\) are drawn independently, not from the optimal coupling, which is unknown; this is a cheap stand-in. The paper uses \(\lambda = 10\) throughout and that value became near-universal, with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-4}\), \(\beta_1 = 0\), \(\beta_2 = 0.9\)) and five critic steps per generator step.

import torch

def gradient_penalty(critic, real, fake, lam=10.0):
    """fake should already be detached from the generator."""
    eps = torch.rand(real.size(0), *[1] * (real.dim() - 1), device=real.device)
    x_hat = (eps * real + (1 - eps) * fake).requires_grad_(True)
    out = critic(x_hat)
    grad, = torch.autograd.grad(out.sum(), x_hat, create_graph=True)  # keep graph: penalty is trained
    norms = grad.flatten(1).norm(2, dim=1)
    return lam * ((norms - 1) ** 2).mean()

Why toward 1, not below 1. The paper penalizes deviation in both directions (a two-sided penalty) and reports that this does not over-constrain the critic in practice, plausibly because the optimal critic has unit gradient norm almost everywhere on and between the supports anyway.

Why batch norm must go. The penalty is a statement about each input's gradient. Batch norm makes each output depend on the whole minibatch, so the per-sample gradient is no longer the object being penalized. The authors remove BN from the critic and recommend layer normalization as a drop-in replacement.

The method: spectral normalization

Derivation · a layerwise Lipschitz bound

A linear layer \(h \mapsto Wh\) has Lipschitz constant \(\sigma(W)\), its largest singular value. ReLU and leaky ReLU are 1-Lipschitz, and Lipschitz constants multiply under composition, so for \(f = W_L \circ a \circ \cdots \circ a \circ W_1\),

$$\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \le \prod_{l=1}^{L}\sigma(W_l).$$

Replace each weight by \(\bar W = W/\sigma(W)\) and every factor is 1, so \(\|f\|_{\text{Lip}} \le 1\).

Algorithm · one power iteration per step
  1. Keep a persistent vector \(\tilde u\) per layer (random at initialization).
  2. Each training step: \(\tilde v \leftarrow W^\top\tilde u/\|W^\top\tilde u\|\), then \(\tilde u \leftarrow W\tilde v/\|W\tilde v\|\).
  3. Estimate \(\sigma(W) \approx \tilde u^\top W\tilde v\) and use \(\bar W = W/\sigma(W)\) in the forward pass; gradients flow through the normalization.

One iteration is a poor estimate from scratch, but \(W\) changes slowly between steps and \(\tilde u\) is carried over, so the power method is effectively amortized across training. The cost is two matrix–vector products per layer per step: nearly free. For convolutions the kernel is reshaped into a matrix of shape \(c_{\text{out}} \times (c_{\text{in}} k_h k_w)\); that matrix's norm is not exactly the convolution operator's norm, so the bound is approximate for conv layers.

Evidence

WGAN-GP. On 2-D toy distributions the clipped critic learns overly simple value surfaces while the penalized critic captures the data's shape. The authors train a range of architectures that break standard GANs (no BN in the generator, MLP generators, tanh nonlinearities, a 101-layer ResNet) and get competitive CIFAR-10 Inception Scores. A character-level language model with a convolutional generator emitting softmax vectors also trains, a demonstration rather than a competitive LM. Spectral normalization. On CIFAR-10 and STL-10 the authors report that SN stays good across a grid of learning rates and Adam settings where WGAN-GP, weight normalization and other baselines degrade, and that SN plus a projection discriminator produced class-conditional ImageNet samples across all 1000 classes with a single generator and discriminator pair.

Limits & what broke
  • GP guarantees nothing off the lines. It is a soft penalty on sampled points; the critic can be steep elsewhere.
  • GP is expensive. It needs a second backward pass through the critic (double backprop) every step.
  • The product bound is loose. SN can over-constrain: the network's true Lipschitz constant is usually far below the product of layer norms, so the critic may use less capacity than allowed.
  • Successors. Zero-centred penalties on real data only (R1, Mescheder et al. 2018) proved better behaved for convergence and became StyleGAN's default (Day 251). SN spread to generators in SAGAN and BigGAN, where it delayed but did not prevent collapse (Day 255).

Bridge

With a loss that trains reliably, the next question was resolution. Tomorrow (Day 250) uses the WGAN-GP loss to grow a GAN from 4×4 to 1024×1024, adding layers as training proceeds. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 248.

Progressive Growing of GANs

35m videoLevel 2

Karras, Aila, Laine & Lehtinen, ICLR 2018 (arXiv:1710.10196)

Start both networks at 4×4 and add layers for each doubling of resolution, fading every new block in smoothly. The GAN learns coarse structure while the problem is easy and adds detail only later, which made 1024×1024 faces possible in 2017.

Cold open

Megapixel faces, generated, with pores and stray hairs. To train them the authors first had to build the dataset: CelebA-HQ, 30,000 cleaned-up 1024×1024 faces derived from CelebA. Most GAN results at the time were 128×128 or smaller.

The problem

High resolution makes GANs harder in two ways. The discriminator can tell high-resolution fakes from real images by fine detail long before the generator gets global structure right, so its gradients stop being informative. And memory limits force small minibatches at large image sizes, which further destabilizes training. Training directly at 1024×1024 asks the generator to solve everything at once.

The method: grow both networks

Training begins with a generator and discriminator that work at 4×4. When that stage has converged, layers for 8×8 are added to both, mirror-symmetrically, and so on up to 1024×1024. All existing layers stay trainable throughout. The authors' argument: small images contain much less information and far fewer modes, so early stages are stable, and each later stage asks a much simpler question ("add detail to an image whose layout is already right") than mapping a latent vector straight to a megapixel image. Most iterations also happen at low resolution, so the authors report training several times faster than a fixed-resolution baseline.

Algorithm · fading in a new resolution (generator side)
  1. Upsample the last existing block's features 2× with nearest-neighbour filtering.
  2. Old path: apply the old toRGB (a 1×1 convolution) to the upsampled features. Because a 1×1 conv commutes with nearest-neighbour upsampling, this equals the previous stage's RGB output, upsampled.
  3. New path: pass the upsampled features through the new block, then a new toRGB.
  4. Output \((1 - \alpha)\cdot\text{old} + \alpha\cdot\text{new}\), with \(\alpha\) rising linearly from 0 to 1 over the transition phase; then train at the new resolution with \(\alpha = 1\).
  5. The discriminator mirrors this: average-pool the input 0.5× into the old fromRGB, blend with the new fromRGB → new block path.

The method: three small tricks

  • Minibatch standard deviation. Near the end of \(D\), compute the standard deviation of every feature at every spatial location across the minibatch, average all of them into one number, and append it as a single constant feature map. \(D\) can now notice when a batch of fakes is less varied than a batch of reals. A one-line, parameter-free relative of minibatch discrimination (Day 247), and the authors report it measurably increases variation.
  • Equalized learning rate. Initialize weights from \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\) and rescale them at runtime by the per-layer constant from He's initializer (the paper writes \(\hat w_i = w_i/c\)), instead of baking that scale into the initialization. Adam and RMSProp normalize each update by a running estimate of the gradient's scale, so the step size is independent of a parameter's own scale; parameters with a larger dynamic range then effectively learn more slowly. Rescaling at runtime gives every weight the same dynamic range and so the same learning speed.
  • Pixelwise feature normalization. After each conv layer in \(G\), normalize each pixel's feature vector to unit average square: \(b_{x,y} = a_{x,y}\big/\sqrt{\tfrac1N\sum_j (a^j_{x,y})^2 + 10^{-8}}\). This stops signal magnitudes from escalating as \(G\) and \(D\) compete.

The main experiments use the WGAN-GP loss (Day 249), with a small extra term that keeps the critic's output from drifting far from zero.

Evidence

CelebA-HQ samples at 1024×1024 are the headline. LSUN categories at 256×256 show the method is not face-specific, and the authors report a then-record unsupervised Inception Score on CIFAR-10. For measurement they propose a multi-scale sliced Wasserstein distance between patches drawn from Laplacian pyramid levels of real and generated images, which looks at statistics at each scale, plus MS-SSIM between generated pairs as a variation check. An ablation table adds the contributions one at a time; the authors report that each one improves the metrics, with progressive growing itself giving both better results and faster convergence. The figures are convincing; the custom metrics were not widely adopted, and FID soon became the comparison standard.

Limits & what broke
  • Phase artifacts. StyleGAN2 (Day 252) traced a characteristic failure to progressive growing: details such as teeth and eyes have a strong preferred position on the canvas and fail to move with the pose of the face. Each resolution's layers were once the output layers and learned to produce maximal-frequency detail, an inductive bias that leaked into the final model. StyleGAN2 dropped progressive growing for skip connections.
  • Schedule hyperparameters. Phase lengths, per-resolution batch sizes and learning rates multiply the tuning burden.
  • MS-SSIM is coarse. The authors note it catches only large-scale mode collapse, not loss of colour or texture variation.

Bridge

The coarse-to-fine idea outlived its implementation: cascaded diffusion models (Imagen's 64 → 256 → 1024 pipeline, Day 266) let each stage specialize the same way. StyleGAN (Day 251) keeps equalized learning rate and minibatch stddev from this paper and rebuilds the generator around a new idea: styles injected at every resolution.

A Style-Based Generator Architecture for GANs (StyleGAN)

50m videoLevel 3

Karras, Laine & Aila, CVPR 2019 (arXiv:1812.04948)

Stop feeding the latent code in at the bottom. Map it through an MLP to an intermediate space W, inject it at every resolution as a per-layer style that rescales normalized activations, start synthesis from a learned constant, and add per-pixel noise for stochastic detail. The result is control by scale, and a latent space that can be less entangled because it is not forced to follow a fixed distribution.

Cold open

The style-mixing grid: one row of source faces, one column of others, and every cell a face that takes its pose, face shape and glasses from one source and its colouring or fine texture from the other. It may be the best demo in generative modelling. The paper also released FFHQ, 70,000 high-quality 1024×1024 faces from Flickr, which became a standard benchmark.

The problem

In every generator so far (Day 246, Day 250), \(z\) enters through the first layer and its influence is mixed through all later ones. ProGAN made 1024×1024 faces, but there was no handle on which attributes a given direction controls, and interpolations showed attributes changing together. The generator was a black box mapping a Gaussian to images.

The method: mapping network and styles

  • Mapping network. An 8-layer MLP \(f: \mathcal Z \to \mathcal W\), both 512-dimensional.
  • AdaIN (adaptive instance normalization, from Huang & Belongie's style transfer): for feature map \(x_i\) and style \(y = (y_{s}, y_{b}) = A(w)\), $$\mathrm{AdaIN}(x_i, y) = y_{s,i}\,\frac{x_i - \mu(x_i)}{\sigma(x_i)} + y_{b,i}.$$ Normalization (⟵ Day 48) wipes out the previous style; the new scale and bias set it. Each style therefore controls one convolution and is overridden by the next.
  • Constant input. Synthesis starts from a learned 4×4×512 tensor. The authors report that once styles carry \(w\), also feeding it as the input stops helping.
  • Noise inputs. Per-pixel noise at every layer gives the generator a direct source of randomness for things like hair placement and freckles, so it does not have to spend capacity faking randomness from \(w\).
  • Style mixing regularization. For 90% of training images, two latents are mapped to \(w_1, w_2\) and the generator switches from \(w_1\) to \(w_2\) at a random layer. This stops the network from assuming adjacent layers' styles are correlated.

Why an intermediate latent space

This argument is subtle and is the heart of the paper. Suppose the training data lacks some combination of factors, say long-haired men are absent. \(\mathcal Z\) is sampled from a fixed Gaussian, so every region of it must map to some image; to avoid producing the missing combination, the map from \(\mathcal Z\) to factors of variation must curve, bending the empty region out of existence. That warping is entanglement. \(\mathcal W\) is not sampled from a fixed distribution; its density is whatever the learned mapping produces, so it can reproduce the data's uneven factor density and leave the map from \(\mathcal W\) to factors closer to linear. The authors argue the generator has an incentive to do this because a less entangled representation is easier for it to generate from. It is an argument about pressure, not a guarantee.

Definition · two disentanglement measures

Perceptual path length: interpolate between two latents, subdivide the path into tiny steps of size \(\epsilon = 10^{-4}\), and average the perceptual image distance per step, scaled by \(1/\epsilon^2\). Spherical interpolation in \(\mathcal Z\), linear in \(\mathcal W\). Short paths mean images change smoothly, with no sudden appearance of features. Linear separability: label generated images with attribute classifiers for 40 CelebA attributes, fit a linear SVM in latent space for each, and measure the conditional entropy of the true label given the SVM's side of the hyperplane; report the exponential of the summed entropies. Lower is better for both.

Coarse, middle, fine. Copying styles from source B at coarse layers (4²–8²) takes pose, general hair style, face shape and eyeglasses from B. Middle layers (16²–32²) carry smaller facial features and whether the eyes are open. Fine layers (64²–1024²) carry mainly colour scheme and microstructure. The truncation trick moves to \(\mathcal W\): \(w' = \bar w + \psi(w - \bar w)\), trading variety for typicality.

Evidence

An ablation table builds from a tuned ProGAN baseline: add the mapping network and styles, remove the traditional input, add noise, add mixing regularization. The authors report FID improving at almost every step on both CelebA-HQ and FFHQ. The noise is ablated directly: re-sampling only the noise changes hair strands, skin pores and background texture while identity and pose stay put, and removing noise at coarse versus fine layers changes large-scale curls versus fine detail. Perceptual path length and linear separability are both lower in \(\mathcal W\) than in \(\mathcal Z\), and lower with a deeper mapping network. LSUN bedrooms, cars and cats show the architecture carries over beyond faces.

Limits & what broke
  • Droplets. Most StyleGAN images carry a blob-like artifact, often subtle at full resolution and obvious in intermediate feature maps. Tomorrow's paper traces it to AdaIN itself (Day 252).
  • Phase artifacts. StyleGAN still grows progressively, so teeth and eyes keep their preferred positions on the canvas as the head turns.
  • Metrics designed for the claim. Both disentanglement measures were introduced in the same paper that benefits from them, and they rely on faces and CelebA attribute classifiers.
  • Misuse. Photorealistic faces of people who do not exist arrived on public websites within months, which made fake-image detection and attribution (tomorrow) a practical concern.

Bridge

Tomorrow (Day 252) is the debugging sequel that removes the droplet and phase artifacts by redesigning normalization and dropping progressive growing. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 48, ⟵ Day 250.

Analyzing and Improving the Image Quality of StyleGAN (StyleGAN2)

40m videoLevel 3

Karras et al., CVPR 2020 (arXiv:1912.04958)

A blob artifact in StyleGAN's images is traced to AdaIN normalizing each feature map by its own statistics. Replacing that with weight demodulation, which normalizes using expected statistics folded into the convolution weights, removes it; dropping progressive growing removes a second artifact; and a path-length penalty makes the generator smoother and easier to invert.

Cold open

Look closely at almost any StyleGAN image and there is a faint water-droplet blob somewhere. Look at the intermediate feature maps and it is unmistakable: a bright, localized spike that appears around 64×64 and grows at higher resolutions. This paper is a debugging story. A visual symptom leads to a specific architectural cause, then to a principled fix, and the paper is worth studying for that process as much as for the result.

The problem

StyleGAN (Day 251) had two characteristic artifacts, droplets and "phase" artifacts, plus a metric puzzle: its perceptual path length correlated with how good images looked, but nothing in training optimized it. The authors wanted to know where each artifact came from and to fix causes rather than symptoms.

The method: the diagnosis

AdaIN normalizes the mean and variance of each feature map separately. That destroys any information carried in the relative magnitudes of the features. The authors' hypothesis: the generator smuggles signal-strength information past the normalization by creating one strong, localized spike that dominates the statistics; the spike sets the normalization, and the generator can then scale the signal elsewhere as it likes. Supporting evidence: when the normalization step is removed from the generator, the droplets disappear entirely. The artifact is the generator working around a constraint.

The method: weight demodulation

The redesign first moves bias and noise out of the style block and drops mean normalization, keeping only a scaling by standard deviation. Then the per-channel style scale is folded into the convolution weights. For input channel \(i\), output channel \(j\) and kernel position \(k\), with style scale \(s_i\) from \(w\):

Derivation · demodulation from an i.i.d. assumption

Modulate: \(w'_{ijk} = s_i\, w_{ijk}\). The output is \(y_j = \sum_{i,k} w'_{ijk}\, x_{i,k}\). If the inputs \(x_{i,k}\) are independent with zero mean and unit variance, then

$$\Var(y_j) = \sum_{i,k} (w'_{ijk})^2\,\Var(x_{i,k}) = \sum_{i,k}(w'_{ijk})^2.$$

Demodulate: divide each output channel's weights by that standard deviation,

$$w''_{ijk} = \frac{w'_{ijk}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i,k}(w'_{ijk})^2 + \epsilon}},$$

so the output has unit standard deviation under the assumption. No statistics of the actual feature maps are measured, so there is no per-map normalization for the generator to game, and relative magnitudes survive.

The authors are explicit that this is an approximation: it is a statistical analogue of instance normalization that holds only if inputs really are i.i.d., which they are not. It is weaker than true normalization, and that weakness is the point. In practice each sample has its own weights, implemented efficiently with grouped convolutions.

The method: smoothness, and no more growing

Definition · path length regularization $$\E_{w,\,y\sim\mathcal N(0, I)}\Big(\big\|J_w^\top y\big\|_2 - a\Big)^2,\qquad J_w = \frac{\partial g(w)}{\partial w},$$

where \(y\) is a random image with Gaussian pixel values and \(a\) is a running average of \(\|J_w^\top y\|_2\). It asks that a fixed-size step in \(\mathcal W\) change the image by a fixed amount whatever the direction; the penalty is minimized when \(J_w\) is orthogonal up to a global scale. \(J_w^\top y\) is cheap: it is \(\nabla_w\big(g(w)\cdot y\big)\), one backward pass.

Lazy regularization. The R1 penalty on the discriminator and the path-length penalty are computed only once every 16 minibatches, with their weights scaled up to compensate. The authors report no loss in quality and a substantial saving in training cost.

Removing progressive growing. The phase artifact: teeth and eyes keep a preferred position on the canvas instead of moving with the face as it turns. The authors argue that in progressive growing (Day 250) each resolution is briefly the output resolution, forced to produce maximal-frequency detail, and that this inductive bias leaks into the final network. They compare skip-connection and residual designs and settle on output skips in the generator and residual connections in the discriminator. An analysis of how much each resolution contributes to the output shows the network still focuses on low resolutions early in training and shifts to fine detail later, the benefit of progressive growing without the schedule. The same analysis showed the highest-resolution layers were underused, so they enlarged them.

Projection and attribution. Optimizing \(w\) and the noise maps to reconstruct a target image, the authors find that images generated by StyleGAN2 can be projected back almost exactly, while real photographs cannot. The reconstruction distance therefore indicates whether an image came from this particular generator.

Evidence

A configuration table steps from StyleGAN through weight demodulation, lazy regularization, path-length regularization, the new architecture and larger networks, on FFHQ and LSUN Car; the authors report FID, precision and recall, and perceptual path length all improving overall. They also show that FID and PPL can disagree: among models with similar FID, those with lower PPL produce visibly better shapes, which they offer as the reason to regularize PPL. Droplet artifacts are absent in the redesigned feature maps, and the phase artifact is fixed in the interpolation videos.

Limits & what broke
  • The i.i.d. assumption. Demodulation is exact only for independent unit-variance inputs. It works because exact normalization is not what the network needs.
  • Texture sticking. Fine detail still stays glued to pixel coordinates during motion. StyleGAN3 (Karras et al. 2021) traced this to aliasing and rebuilt the layers to be alias-free.
  • Attribution is narrow. Projection detects images from one known generator; it is not a general deepfake detector, and post-processing can defeat it.
  • Narrow domains. StyleGAN2 dominates faces, cars and churches. On diverse class-conditional data like ImageNet, diffusion models overtook GANs within a couple of years (Day 263).

Bridge

The GAN architecture thread pauses here. Tomorrow (Day 253) goes back to 2016 for the losses that VQGAN and Stable Diffusion's autoencoder depend on: style as a Gram matrix of features, and perceptual loss as a general-purpose training signal.

Neural Style Transfer + Perceptual Losses

40m videoLevel 2

Gatys, Ecker & Bethge CVPR 2016; Johnson, Alahi & Fei-Fei ECCV 2016 (arXiv:1603.08155)

Inside a frozen VGG network, an image's content is the spatial layout of its mid-level features and its style is their Gram matrix, which records which features co-occur and discards where. Gatys et al. optimized an image to match both; Johnson et al. trained a feed-forward network against the same losses, turning a per-image optimization into a single forward pass.

Cold open

A photograph of the riverfront in Tübingen, repainted as Van Gogh's The Starry Night, as Munch's The Scream, as a Turner. Every brushstroke is invented; the buildings stay where they were. No network was trained to do this. A pretrained ImageNet classifier and an optimizer did it, one image at a time.

The problem

Separating "what is depicted" from "how it is depicted" had no good formal handle. Non-parametric texture transfer (image quilting and its relatives) copied patches from a source image and worked only for some styles. Gatys and colleagues had shown in 2015 that Gram matrices of CNN features make a strong parametric texture model. The question was whether those statistics could be imposed on the content of another image.

The method: two losses in VGG feature space

Let \(F^l \in \R^{N_l \times M_l}\) be layer \(l\)'s activations for the image being generated: \(N_l\) feature maps, each flattened over its \(M_l = H_l W_l\) positions. Let \(P^l\) be the same for the content photo, and \(A^l\) the Gram matrix of the style image. The network is a frozen VGG-19 (⟵ Day 64).

Definition · content and style losses (Gatys et al.) $$\mathcal L_{\text{content}} = \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\big(F^l_{ij} - P^l_{ij}\big)^2 \quad\text{at a single mid layer},$$ $$G^l_{ij} = \sum_k F^l_{ik}F^l_{jk},\qquad E_l = \frac{1}{4N_l^2M_l^2}\sum_{i,j}\big(G^l_{ij} - A^l_{ij}\big)^2,\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{style}} = \sum_l w_l E_l,$$

with the content layer conv4_2 and style layers conv1_1 through conv5_1, equally weighted. The total \(\alpha\mathcal L_{\text{content}} + \beta\mathcal L_{\text{style}}\) is minimized over the pixels of the output image with L-BFGS, starting from white noise; the network's weights never change. The paper's figures use content-to-style ratios \(\alpha/\beta\) of roughly \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-4}\), and replace max pooling with average pooling, which the authors found gave more appealing results.

Derivation · why matching Gram matrices matches texture statistics

Write \(f_k\) for the feature vector at position \(k\), so \(G = \sum_k f_k f_k^\top\), and \(g_k\) for the other image's. Expanding the Frobenius norm with \(\langle aa^\top, bb^\top\rangle_F = (a^\top b)^2\):

$$\|G_f - G_g\|_F^2 = \sum_{k,k'}(f_k^\top f_{k'})^2 + \sum_{k,k'}(g_k^\top g_{k'})^2 - 2\sum_{k,k'}(f_k^\top g_{k'})^2.$$

That is (unnormalized) squared maximum mean discrepancy between the two sets of local feature vectors under the kernel \(k(a, b) = (a^\top b)^2\), a point made by Li et al. (2017). The style loss compares the distributions of local features, treating positions as exchangeable samples. That is a reasonable definition of texture.

Layer choice sets the scale of style. Matching Gram matrices only at low layers reproduces small-scale colour and texture; including higher layers, whose features have larger receptive fields, transfers larger structures like brushstroke patterns and local shapes.

The method: amortize the optimization

Gatys's method needs hundreds of forward and backward passes through VGG per output image. Johnson et al. train an image transformation network \(f_W\) (strided convolutions down, residual blocks, transposed convolutions up) once per style, on COCO images, to minimize the same kind of perceptual losses computed by a frozen VGG-16: a feature reconstruction loss at one layer (relu3_3 for style transfer) and Gram-matrix style losses at four layers, plus a total-variation smoothness term. At test time, stylization is one forward pass. This is the same move VAEs make for inference (Day 241): replace per-example optimization with a network that predicts its result.

They also use the feature loss alone for single-image super-resolution at ×4 and ×8. Trained against feature reconstruction instead of per-pixel loss, outputs are visibly sharper even though PSNR and SSIM are lower: the per-pixel metrics reward exactly the blur the perceptual loss avoids.

Evidence

Gatys et al. is qualitative: style transfers across many paintings, grids showing the content–style tradeoff as \(\alpha/\beta\) varies, and how results change with the layers used for style. Johnson et al. report that their network reaches style losses comparable to running the optimization method for many iterations while being about three orders of magnitude faster, fast enough for real-time video. For super-resolution they show sharper edges and textures than per-pixel-trained networks, with lower PSNR, and discuss why PSNR is the wrong judge.

Limits & what broke
  • One network per style. Johnson's networks each learn one painting. Arbitrary style transfer came with AdaIN (Huang & Belongie 2017), which matches per-channel mean and variance instead of full Gram matrices; StyleGAN borrowed it (Day 251).
  • Stationary textures only. Gram matrices cannot represent long-range or layout-dependent structure, so styles with global composition transfer poorly.
  • VGG is not a human. The losses inherit ImageNet's biases and can produce hallucinated texture or checkerboard artifacts. LPIPS (Zhang et al. 2018) calibrates deep-feature distances against human similarity judgements and is the modern descendant.

Bridge

Perceptual loss became a standard tool: in super-resolution, in VQGAN's autoencoder (Day 244) and in Stable Diffusion's (Day 264). Tomorrow (Day 254) is image-to-image translation trained with adversarial losses instead, with and without paired data. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 64.

pix2pix and CycleGAN

40m videoLevel 2

Isola, Zhu, Zhou & Efros CVPR 2017 (arXiv:1611.07004); Zhu, Park, Isola & Efros ICCV 2017 (arXiv:1703.10593)

With paired data, a conditional GAN plus an L1 loss is a general-purpose image translator: L1 gets the low frequencies right and a patch discriminator supplies the high frequencies. Without pairs, two translators trained to undo each other (cycle consistency) can still learn a mapping, but the cycle constraint is necessary, not sufficient, and the network will cheat it if it can.

Cold open

Edges become handbags, label maps become street scenes and building facades, aerial photos become maps, daytime becomes night: one architecture and one loss for all of them. Months later, horses become zebras and Monet paintings become photographs, trained from two unpaired piles of images. Within months, people online were feeding pix2pix sketches of cats.

The problem

Every translation task (colourization, segmentation-to-photo, super-resolution) had its own hand-designed loss. The obvious general loss, L2 or L1 to the target, produces blur: when several outputs are plausible, the per-pixel optimum averages them. GANs (Day 245) produce sharp images but, unconditioned, ignore the input. And many interesting translations (horse to zebra, painting to photo) have no paired examples at all.

The method: pix2pix

Definition · pix2pix objective $$G^* = \arg\min_G\max_D\; \mathcal L_{\text{cGAN}}(G, D) + \lambda\,\E_{x,y,z}\big[\|y - G(x, z)\|_1\big],\qquad \lambda = 100,$$ $$\mathcal L_{\text{cGAN}} = \E_{x,y}\big[\log D(x, y)\big] + \E_{x,z}\big[\log\big(1 - D(x, G(x, z))\big)\big].$$

The discriminator sees the input \(x\) together with the output, so it judges whether the output is a plausible translation of this input, not just a plausible image.

Why the combination works. The L1 minimizer is the per-pixel median of plausible outputs: correct in layout and low-frequency colour, blurry where details are uncertain. A discriminator restricted to local patches cares only about local texture and sharpness. The paper's ablation shows the division of labour: L1 alone gives blur, cGAN alone gives sharp images with artifacts and weaker correspondence to the input, and the sum gives sharp, faithful outputs. This idea, a reconstruction loss for low frequencies plus an adversarial loss for high ones, transfers far beyond this paper; VQGAN's autoencoder uses it (Day 244).

  • Generator: a U-Net (⟵ Day 92). Input and output share structure (an edge in the sketch is an edge in the photo), so skip connections let low-level information bypass the bottleneck. The ablation shows a plain encoder-decoder does worse.
  • Discriminator: PatchGAN. A fully convolutional net that classifies each patch as real or fake and averages the answers. With the default architecture each output sees a 70×70 receptive field. This models the image as a Markov random field: pixels farther apart than a patch are treated as independent, so the discriminator acts as a learned texture/style loss. It has far fewer parameters than a whole-image discriminator and runs on any image size. The ablation: 1×1 patches mostly improve colour, 16×16 are sharp but tile, 70×70 is the sweet spot, and a full 286×286 image discriminator is no better and harder to train.
  • Noise is mostly ignored. The authors found the generator learned to ignore an input noise vector, so they inject randomness only through dropout, at training and test time. Outputs are close to deterministic: one plausible answer, not a distribution.

The method: CycleGAN

Without pairs, an adversarial loss can only push \(G(X)\) to look like the distribution of \(Y\). Infinitely many mappings induce the same output distribution, including ones that ignore the input entirely. CycleGAN adds a second generator \(F: Y \to X\) and asks the two to be approximate inverses.

Definition · CycleGAN objective $$\mathcal L = \mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}(G, D_Y) + \mathcal L_{\text{GAN}}(F, D_X) + \lambda\Big(\E_x\|F(G(x)) - x\|_1 + \E_y\|G(F(y)) - y\|_1\Big),\qquad\lambda = 10.$$

In practice the GAN terms use a least-squares loss for stability, the discriminators are 70×70 PatchGANs, and each discriminator is updated from a buffer of the 50 most recent generated images rather than only the latest. For painting↔photo, an identity loss \(\E_y\|G(y) - y\|_1 + \E_x\|F(x) - x\|_1\) (weight \(0.5\lambda\)) stops the generators from changing the colour composition of inputs that are already in the target domain.

Evidence

pix2pix is evaluated with "real vs fake" perceptual studies on Mechanical Turk (maps↔aerial photos, colourization) and with an FCN-score on Cityscapes: run a pretrained segmentation network on generated photos and measure how well it recovers the input labels. Ablations cover the loss (L1, GAN, cGAN, L1+cGAN), the generator (encoder-decoder vs U-Net) and the patch size; the conclusions above come from them. CycleGAN uses the same two evaluations, with pix2pix trained on paired data as an upper bound; the authors report it beats unpaired baselines (CoGAN, BiGAN/ALI, SimGAN, a feature-loss GAN) but falls short of pix2pix. Removing either the GAN loss or the cycle loss hurts substantially. Their own failure figure shows geometric changes (dog↔cat) mostly fail: the mappings change texture and colour, not shape.

Limits & what broke
  • CycleGAN is a master of steganography (Chu, Zhmoginov & Sandler 2017). Translating an aerial photo to a map loses detail, yet \(F\) reconstructs the photo almost perfectly. The generator hides the missing information in low-amplitude, high-frequency patterns in the map, invisible to people and to the discriminator, which \(F\) decodes. Cycle consistency was satisfied by cheating, and the hidden channel makes the model sensitive to tiny adversarial perturbations. It is the most instructive failure in the paper's afterlife: the objective you wrote is the objective you get.
  • Cycle consistency is not correctness. Any pair of bijections that are inverses of each other satisfies it. In medical image translation, distribution-matching losses can add or remove features such as tumours (Cohen, Luck & Honari 2018).
  • One answer per input. pix2pix is nearly deterministic; BicycleGAN (Zhu et al. 2017) and later diffusion-based translators model the full set of plausible outputs. On the triangle, these models give up mode coverage almost completely.

Large Scale GAN Training for High Fidelity Natural Image Synthesis (BigGAN)

40m videoLevel 3

Brock, Donahue & Simonyan, ICLR 2019 (arXiv:1809.11096)

Class-conditional GANs on ImageNet improved dramatically just by training bigger: much larger batches and wider layers. A few changes on top, above all orthogonal regularization, made the truncation trick work, which gives an explicit fidelity-versus-diversity dial at inference time. The paper also measured the instability that scale brings and concluded it can be delayed but not prevented.

Cold open

The abstract's numbers on ImageNet at 128×128: an Inception Score of 166.5 and an FID of 7.4, against a previous best of 52.52 and 18.65. Samples of dogs, butterflies and burgers that, at a glance, pass for photographs. Before this paper, class-conditional ImageNet GAN samples were recognizable textures arranged into implausible objects.

The problem

The strongest ImageNet GAN at the time, SAGAN (Zhang et al. 2019), combined self-attention, spectral normalization in both networks (Day 249), a hinge loss and class-conditional batch norm. It still produced few convincing images of complex classes. Nobody had asked systematically what happens if you simply scale such a model up, or what breaks when you do.

The method: scale first

Starting from SAGAN, the authors increase the batch size from 256 to 2048 and report that this alone improves the Inception Score by 46%. Increasing the width (channels per layer) by 50%, which roughly doubles the parameter count, adds a further 21%. Both help immediately; neither is a new idea. The price is that bigger batches also reach training collapse sooner, which is why the paper's other half is about instability.

  • Shared class embeddings. Instead of a separate embedding per conditional batch-norm layer, one class embedding is linearly projected to every layer's gains and biases, cutting parameters and compute.
  • Hierarchical latent ("skip-z"). \(z\) is split into chunks, one per resolution; each chunk is concatenated with the class embedding and fed to that block's conditional batch norm, so the latent can directly influence features at every scale.
  • Orthogonal initialization and an EMA of generator weights for evaluation, inherited or adapted from prior work.

The method: truncation, and why it needed regularization

Definition · the truncation trick

Train with \(z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\). At sampling time, draw \(z\) from a truncated normal: any coordinate whose magnitude exceeds a threshold is resampled until it falls inside. Lower thresholds keep \(z\) near the mode of the prior, where the generator is best trained.

Some large models produced saturation artifacts when fed truncated noise. The authors' diagnosis: truncation only helps if the generator maps the whole prior smoothly, so that the region near the mode is not special. They enforce smoothness with a relaxed orthogonal regularizer,

$$R_\beta(W) = \beta\,\big\|W^\top W \odot (\mathbf 1 - I)\big\|_F^2,$$

which penalizes the cosine similarity between different filters but, unlike classic orthogonal regularization \(\|W^\top W - I\|_F^2\), leaves their norms free. The authors report that this made a much larger fraction of their models amenable to truncation. Orthogonal regularization is what makes the dial turn smoothly.

The method: anatomy of a collapse

At this scale, training eventually collapses: quality degrades suddenly and does not recover. The paper's most transferable section is the diagnosis. The authors track the top three singular values \(\sigma_0, \sigma_1, \sigma_2\) of every weight matrix through training (cheap with the power iteration of Day 249).

  • Generator: in most layers \(\sigma_0\) grows steadily during training and explodes at collapse. Directly constraining \(\sigma_0\) in \(G\) prevents the explosion, but not the collapse. The spectral blow-up is a symptom.
  • Discriminator: spectra are noisy, and jump at collapse. A strong gradient penalty on \(D\) stabilizes training, but only at a severe cost in IS. Evidence that \(D\) overfits: its accuracy on held-out real images falls during training, consistent with memorizing the training set rather than learning a general notion of realism.

Their conclusion is that stability comes from the interaction of the two networks, not from either one, and that collapse can be delayed but not prevented without hurting performance. The practical recipe is to relax the constraints, accept that collapse will happen late in training, and keep the checkpoint from just before it.

Evidence

The ablation table builds from SAGAN through batch size, width, shared embeddings, skip-z and orthogonal regularization, reporting FID and IS for each; the gains are large and mostly additive. Results are given at 128×128, 256×256 and 512×512, with truncation curves for each. A deeper variant with bottleneck residual blocks (BigGAN-deep) improves further. On JFT-300M, a much larger internal dataset with many more classes, the authors report that the design choices carry over, that extra width helps more than on ImageNet, and that training stayed stable far longer, which fits the discriminator-memorization account of collapse. The numbers are evaluated with truncation chosen per metric, so read which threshold each figure uses.

Limits & what broke
  • Truncation spends coverage. Headline IS uses aggressive truncation; on the month's triangle, BigGAN buys quality by moving away from mode coverage, and it says so with its curves.
  • Compute. Training ran on TPU pods. Few groups could reproduce or extend it, which concentrated GAN progress in a few labs.
  • Collapse is managed, not solved. Early stopping before collapse is a workaround, and the discriminator's memorization is an unresolved generalization problem.
  • Hard classes stay hard. Crowds, complex scenes and classes with many small parts remain visibly worse than textured animals.
  • Superseded on its own benchmark. Two years later a diffusion model with guidance beat BigGAN-deep's ImageNet FID (Day 263).

Bridge

BigGAN matters to the series' long-running question of whether scale is everything: here it helped a non-likelihood model just as it would later help transformers and diffusion (⟶ Day 270). WaveNet: A Generative Model for Raw Audio

45m videoLevel 3

van den Oord et al., 2016 (arXiv:1609.03499)

Model a waveform one sample at a time with a stack of causal, dilated convolutions whose receptive field doubles with every layer, and predict each sample as a 256-way categorical over μ-law-companded amplitudes. Training is fully parallel; generation is fully sequential.

Cold open

Speech audio at 16 kHz is 16,000 numbers per second, and WaveNet predicts every one of them, each conditioned on thousands of the ones before. In the paper's text-to-speech listening tests it was rated more natural than the best parametric and concatenative systems in both US English and Mandarin; DeepMind's announcement summarized the result as closing more than half of the gap to natural human speech. Trained on piano recordings instead, the same model produces plausible piano.

The problem

Production text-to-speech in 2016 was either concatenative (stitching recorded fragments, natural but inflexible) or statistical parametric (a model predicts vocoder parameters; flexible but buzzy and muffled). Modelling the raw waveform directly was considered impractical: important structure spans hundreds of milliseconds, which at 16 kHz means thousands of timesteps, too long for RNNs to handle well at that rate. The paper adapts the autoregressive image models of the same group (PixelCNN, Day 257, published months earlier) to one dimension.

The method: causal dilated convolutions

The joint distribution is factorized by the chain rule, \(p(x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})\), and every conditional is computed by the same convolutional network. Causal convolutions guarantee that the prediction at time \(t\) uses only inputs before \(t\). Dilated convolutions (⟵ Day 80) apply a filter with gaps: layer \(\ell\) with dilation \(d\) computes \(\sum_i k_i\, x_{t - d\cdot i}\), skipping \(d - 1\) inputs between taps.

Derivation · the receptive-field arithmetic

A layer with kernel size \(k\) and dilation \(d\) extends the receptive field by \((k - 1)d\) samples. WaveNet uses \(k = 2\) and dilations \(1, 2, 4, \dots, 512\), repeated. One stack of 10 layers gives

$$R_1 = 1 + \sum_{i=0}^{9} 2^i = 1 + 1023 = 1024 \text{ samples}.$$

Four such stacks give \(R_4 = 1 + 4 \times 1023 = 4093\) samples, about \(4093/16000 \approx 0.26\) s of audio, from 40 layers. Undilated kernel-2 convolutions would need 4,092 layers for the same span. Repeating stacks, rather than continuing to double, keeps each layer's filter meaningful and adds capacity; the paper motivates each 1–512 block as a more efficient, discriminative counterpart to a 1×1024 convolution.

import math

def receptive_field(dilations, kernel_size=2):
    return 1 + sum((kernel_size - 1) * d for d in dilations)

stack = [2 ** i for i in range(10)]            # 1, 2, 4, ..., 512
print(receptive_field(stack))                  # 1024
print(receptive_field(stack * 4) / 16000)      # 4093 samples, about 0.256 s at 16 kHz

def mu_law_level(x, mu=255):
    """Map an amplitude in [-1, 1] to one of 256 integer levels."""
    y = math.copysign(math.log1p(mu * abs(x)) / math.log1p(mu), x)
    return int((y + 1) / 2 * mu + 0.5)

The method: what is predicted, and how

A softmax over amplitudes. Raw 16-bit audio has 65,536 possible values per sample, too many for a softmax. The authors first apply μ-law companding,

$$f(x_t) = \operatorname{sign}(x_t)\,\frac{\ln(1 + \mu|x_t|)}{\ln(1 + \mu)},\qquad -1 < x_t < 1,\ \mu = 255,$$

and quantize the result to 256 levels. Companding spends more levels on quiet amplitudes, where hearing is more sensitive, and the authors report the reconstruction sounds very similar to the original. The surprising choice is to model each sample with a categorical distribution rather than a Gaussian or mixture: a categorical makes no assumption about shape, so it can represent multimodal or skewed conditionals, following the earlier finding with PixelRNN that softmax outputs worked better even for continuous data. They justify it empirically.

Gated units, residuals and skips. Each layer computes

$$z = \tanh(W_{f} * x) \odot \sigma(W_{g} * x),$$

a gated activation borrowed from gated PixelCNN, which the authors report worked much better for audio than ReLU. Each layer's output goes through a 1×1 convolution and is added back to its input (a residual connection), and a separate 1×1 projection is sent to a skip path. The skip outputs of all layers are summed, passed through ReLU and 1×1 convolutions, and become the softmax logits.

Conditioning. Global conditioning adds a learned projection of a single vector \(h\), such as a speaker embedding, inside both the tanh and the sigmoid at every layer: \(z = \tanh(W_f * x + V_f^\top h) \odot \sigma(W_g * x + V_g^\top h)\). Local conditioning uses a time series at a lower rate, such as linguistic features and fundamental frequency for TTS: it is upsampled to the audio rate with transposed convolutions and added through 1×1 convolutions. Speaker identity is global; what is being said is local.

The asymmetry

During training every \(x_t\) is known, so all 16,000 conditionals per second are computed in one parallel pass. During generation each sample must be drawn before the next can be computed: one full network evaluation per sample, strictly in sequence. Autoregressive language models (Day 129) have exactly the same property.

Evidence

Without text, a multi-speaker model trained on VCTK produces speech-like babbling in a chosen voice, complete with breathing and mouth sounds. For TTS, WaveNets locally conditioned on linguistic features and \(\log F_0\) were compared with Google's best parametric (LSTM-based) and concatenative (HMM-driven unit selection) systems in paired-preference tests and mean opinion scores; the authors report that WaveNet was preferred over both and scored a clearly higher mean opinion score, much closer to that of natural speech. Music models trained on piano recordings produce harmonically plausible fragments but lack long-range structure, and a WaveNet adapted for phoneme recognition on TIMIT gave a strong result for a model working on raw audio. The receptive field of the TTS models was a few hundred milliseconds; longer structure came from the conditioning features.

Limits & what broke
  • Sampling speed. The original model generated audio far slower than real time, unusable in production. Caching intermediate activations (Fast WaveNet, Paine et al. 2016) helped; Parallel WaveNet (van den Oord et al. 2018) distilled the teacher into an inverse-autoregressive-flow student that generates all samples at once, trained by "probability density distillation", and that is what was deployed.
  • Not end-to-end. The TTS system still needed a separate front-end to produce linguistic features and pitch. Tacotron 2 (Shen et al. 2018) conditioned a WaveNet vocoder on predicted mel spectrograms instead.
  • Short memory. A quarter of a second of context gives local realism but no musical or prosodic structure beyond it.
  • Superseded. GAN vocoders (MelGAN, HiFi-GAN) and neural audio codecs with token-level language models (⟶ Day 283) replaced sample-level autoregression.

Bridge

On the triangle, WaveNet takes quality and coverage (an exact likelihood) and pays the full price in sampling speed. Tomorrow (Day 257) is the image version it was adapted from: PixelRNN and PixelCNN, where the masks and gated units come from, and where the "blind spot" lives. Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 80 (dilated convolutions).

Pixel Recurrent Neural Networks and PixelCNN

40m videoLevel 3

van den Oord, Kalchbrenner & Kavukcuoglu, ICML 2016 (arXiv:1601.06759)

Treat an image as one long sequence of subpixels in raster order and predict each with a 256-way softmax given everything before it. The result is an exact, tractable likelihood, reported in bits per dimension, paid for with a sequential sampler that needs one network evaluation per subpixel.

Cold open

Hide the bottom half of a photograph and ask the model to finish it: several completions, each different, each consistent with the visible half. The paper reported new state-of-the-art log-likelihoods on MNIST and CIFAR-10, around 3 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10, and introduced the downsampled 32×32 and 64×64 ImageNet density-estimation benchmarks that later likelihood models were measured on.

The problem

GANs (Day 245) give no likelihood at all, and VAEs (Day 241) only a bound. Autoregressive density estimators such as NADE and MADE were exact but had only been applied to small, mostly binary images. A likelihood is valuable because it is a real number you can compare across models, and it measures coverage of the whole data distribution. The challenge was making an exact model expressive enough for natural images.

The method: the chain rule over pixels

For an \(n \times n\) image read row by row, left to right,

$$p(x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n^2} p(x_i \mid x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}),\qquad p(x_i \mid x_{<i}) = p(x_{i,R}\mid x_{<i})\,p(x_{i,G}\mid x_{<i}, x_{i,R})\,p(x_{i,B}\mid x_{<i}, x_{i,R}, x_{i,G}).$$

Each factor is a softmax over the 256 intensity values. As in WaveNet (Day 256), the discrete distribution makes no assumption about shape, and the authors report it outperforms continuous output distributions. The bits-per-dimension score is the negative log-likelihood in base 2 divided by the number of subpixels, \(3n^2\).

Three ways to compute the conditionals. The Row LSTM processes the image one row at a time, with a convolutional state-to-state transition; its receptive field is a triangle above the pixel and misses context to the sides. The Diagonal BiLSTM skews the image so that diagonals become columns and runs LSTMs in two directions, so the receptive field covers the entire available context; it is the most expressive and slowest. PixelCNN replaces recurrence with a stack of masked convolutions that keep the spatial resolution. Its receptive field is bounded, but every conditional is computed in one parallel pass during training, which makes it much faster to train.

Masked convolutions: where implementations break

Definition · mask types A and B

Spatially, every masked kernel zeroes the weights on rows below the centre and to the right of the centre in the centre row. The two types differ at the centre itself:

  • Type A (first layer only): the centre position is also masked for the channel being predicted and for later channels. The R output sees no channel of the current pixel; G sees R; B sees R and G.
  • Type B (all later layers): connections from a channel to itself at the centre are allowed. By then, a feature at the centre position already depends only on permitted inputs, so letting it see itself leaks nothing.

Using type B in the first layer lets each output see its own target value: the training loss collapses toward zero and samples are garbage. It is the most common bug.

import numpy as np

def spatial_mask(k, mask_type):
    """k x k kernel mask for one channel; 1 = connection allowed."""
    m = np.ones((k, k))
    c = k // 2
    m[c, c + (mask_type == "B"):] = 0    # type A also masks the centre
    m[c + 1:, :] = 0                     # every row below the centre
    return m

print(spatial_mask(5, "A"))
print(spatial_mask(5, "B"))

This handles one channel. For RGB, the centre entry is further split by channel group: R, G, B features may see centre inputs of earlier groups (and, for type B, their own group).

The blind spot. Stack enough masked 3×3 convolutions and the receptive field should cover everything above and to the left. It does not. At \(r\) rows above the target, each upward step can move at most one column right, so the reachable region ends \(r\) columns to the right, however many layers you add. Context to the upper right is permitted but never seen.

The fix: Gated PixelCNN (van den Oord et al., NIPS 2016). Split the network into a vertical stack, which sees every row above the current one with no blind spot, and a horizontal stack, which sees the current row to the left and receives the vertical stack's output. Replace ReLUs with gated units \(\tanh(W_f * x) \odot \sigma(W_g * x)\), the activation WaveNet adopted. The authors report this closes most of the likelihood gap to PixelRNN at a fraction of the training cost. The same paper adds conditioning: a class embedding or other vector \(h\) enters as \(\tanh(W_f * x + V_f^\top h) \odot \sigma(W_g * x + V_g^\top h)\). Conditional PixelCNN with class embeddings is an ancestor of conditional generation generally.

PixelCNN++ (Salimans, Karpathy, Chen & Kingma, ICLR 2017) is the version people use. It replaces the 256-way softmax with a discretized logistic mixture, which assigns similar probability to neighbouring intensity values and trains faster; conditions on whole pixels rather than one subpixel at a time; uses downsampling instead of dilation to see far; adds shortcut connections and dropout. The authors report a better CIFAR-10 likelihood than earlier PixelCNNs.

Evidence

The paper compares Row LSTM, Diagonal BiLSTM and PixelCNN on MNIST, CIFAR-10 and the new ImageNet benchmarks. The Diagonal BiLSTM gives the best likelihoods, the Row LSTM is close, and PixelCNN is somewhat worse but much faster to train. Deeper models with residual connections help. Samples are locally coherent, with plausible textures and edges, but global structure is weak, especially on ImageNet. The likelihood numbers are what kept this family alive alongside GANs: an exact score to compare, which GANs could never provide.

Limits & what broke
  • Sampling is \(O(\text{number of subpixels})\). A 32×32 RGB image needs 3,072 sequential network evaluations; 256×256 needs 196,608. That is why PixelCNN ended up as a prior over a small grid of discrete latents (VQ-VAE, Day 243) instead of a pixel model.
  • Likelihood is not sample quality. Theis, van den Oord and Bethge (2016) showed the two can diverge badly; much of an image's log-likelihood is spent on imperceptible noise, and good bits/dim can coexist with poor samples.
  • Raster order is arbitrary. The model must commit to the top of an image before seeing anything below it.

Bridge

On the triangle, autoregressive models own mode coverage (exact likelihood) and good local quality, and pay for it in speed. Tomorrow's family, normalizing flows (Day 258), promises exact likelihood and one-pass sampling, at a different price.

Normalizing Flows: NICE, RealNVP, and Glow

50m videoLevel 4

Dinh et al. 2015/2017 (arXiv:1605.08803); Kingma & Dhariwal NeurIPS 2018 (arXiv:1807.03039)

An invertible network gives an exact likelihood through the change-of-variables formula and samples in one pass, provided the log-determinant of its Jacobian is cheap. That log-determinant is the whole technical difficulty of flows, and coupling layers exist purely to make it a sum.

Cold open

Glow's 256×256 faces, with smooth interpolations between real photographs and sliders for "smiling" or "blond" built from simple latent-space averages. The same model reports an exact log-likelihood, not a bound, and samples with a single forward pass. That is the third option of the month: neither a GAN's missing density nor an autoregressive model's slow sampler.

The problem

Autoregressive models (Day 257) have exact likelihoods and sequential sampling. VAEs (Day 241) sample fast but give only a bound. GANs (Day 245) sample fast with no likelihood. A bijection \(x = g(z)\) with a simple base density \(p_Z\) would give all three properties at once, if its density could be computed.

The method: change of variables

Derivation · the change-of-variables formula

In one dimension, let \(z = f(x)\) be increasing and differentiable. Probability is conserved: \(P(X \le x) = P(Z \le f(x))\). Differentiating, \(p_X(x) = p_Z(f(x))\,f'(x)\); for decreasing \(f\), take \(|f'(x)|\). In \(D\) dimensions a small volume around \(x\) is stretched by the factor \(|\det J_f(x)|\), so

$$\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z\big(f(x)\big) + \log\big|\det J_f(x)\big|,\qquad J_f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \in \R^{D\times D}.$$

For a composition \(f = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1\) with \(h_0 = x\), \(h_k = f_k(h_{k-1})\), determinants multiply, so log-determinants add: \(\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z(h_K) + \sum_k \log|\det J_{f_k}(h_{k-1})|\).

A general \(D \times D\) determinant costs \(O(D^3)\). For a 256×256 RGB image, \(D = 196{,}608\): impossible every training step. Everything else in this lesson is a way around that one cost.

The method: coupling layers

NICE (Dinh, Krueger & Bengio, 2015) splits \(x\) into halves and uses an additive coupling: \(y_1 = x_1\), \(y_2 = x_2 + m(x_1)\), where \(m\) is any network. The Jacobian is block lower-triangular with identity blocks on the diagonal, so its determinant is exactly 1. The inverse is \(x_2 = y_2 - m(y_1)\): you never need to invert \(m\). A final diagonal scaling layer supplies the volume change.

RealNVP (Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein & Bengio, ICLR 2017) uses an affine coupling:

$$y_{1:d} = x_{1:d},\qquad y_{d+1:D} = x_{d+1:D} \odot \exp\big(s(x_{1:d})\big) + t(x_{1:d}).$$
Derivation · why the log-determinant is a sum $$J = \begin{pmatrix} I_d & 0\\[2pt] \dfrac{\partial y_{d+1:D}}{\partial x_{1:d}} & \operatorname{diag}\!\big(\exp s(x_{1:d})\big)\end{pmatrix}.$$

The top-right block is zero because \(y_{1:d}\) ignores \(x_{d+1:D}\). The matrix is lower triangular, so its determinant is the product of its diagonal: \(\log|\det J| = \sum_j s(x_{1:d})_j\). The dense bottom-left block, which contains all the complexity of the networks \(s\) and \(t\), never enters. The inverse is just as cheap: \(x_{d+1:D} = \big(y_{d+1:D} - t(y_{1:d})\big)\odot\exp\big(-s(y_{1:d})\big)\).

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class AffineCoupling(nn.Module):
    """RealNVP-style coupling on vectors: the first half conditions the second."""
    def __init__(self, dim, hidden=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = dim // 2
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(self.d, hidden), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(hidden, 2 * (dim - self.d)))

    def forward(self, x):                     # data -> latent, with log|det J|
        x1, x2 = x[:, :self.d], x[:, self.d:]
        s, t = self.net(x1).chunk(2, dim=1)
        s = torch.tanh(s)                     # bounded log-scales for stability
        return torch.cat([x1, x2 * torch.exp(s) + t], dim=1), s.sum(dim=1)

    def inverse(self, y):                     # latent -> data, exact and cheap
        y1, y2 = y[:, :self.d], y[:, self.d:]
        s, t = self.net(y1).chunk(2, dim=1)
        s = torch.tanh(s)
        return torch.cat([y1, (y2 - t) * torch.exp(-s)], dim=1)

Masks, squeezing and multi-scale. For images, RealNVP chooses the untouched half with binary masks: a spatial checkerboard or a channel-wise split, alternating the mask between layers so every variable is eventually transformed. A squeeze reshapes \(s \times s \times c\) into \(\tfrac s2 \times \tfrac s2 \times 4c\), trading resolution for channels so that channel masks can mix nearby pixels. After each scale (checkerboard couplings, squeeze, channel couplings) half of the dimensions are factored out as final latents, and only the rest continue. This saves computation and gives a coarse-to-fine latent hierarchy.

The method: Glow

Kingma and Dhariwal simplify the step to actnorm (a per-channel affine layer initialized from data statistics, replacing batch norm) → invertible 1×1 convolution → affine coupling. Earlier flows reversed or shuffled channels between couplings. Glow learns that permutation instead: a 1×1 convolution with a \(c \times c\) matrix \(W\), applied at every position of an \(h \times w \times c\) tensor, has

$$\log\left|\det \frac{\partial\, \mathrm{conv}_{1\times1}(h)}{\partial h}\right| = h \cdot w \cdot \log|\det W|.$$

Computing \(\det W\) is \(O(c^3)\), which is affordable but wasteful, so Glow parameterizes \(W = PL(U + \operatorname{diag}(s))\) with \(P\) a fixed permutation, \(L\) lower-triangular with unit diagonal, \(U\) strictly upper-triangular. Then \(\log|\det W| = \sum_i \log|s_i|\), an \(O(c)\) cost: the LU trick.

Evidence

RealNVP reports bits/dim on CIFAR-10, downsampled ImageNet, LSUN and CelebA: competitive, though behind PixelRNN, with much sharper samples than VAEs and fast sampling. Glow reports better bits/dim than RealNVP on every benchmark it tries; its ablation compares reversal, a fixed random shuffle and the learned 1×1 convolution, and the learned version gives lower negative log-likelihood and faster convergence. Its showcase is qualitative: high-resolution face samples, often drawn at a reduced temperature (sampling \(z\) from a narrower Gaussian) for better-looking images, and attribute edits made by adding the average latent difference between images with and without an attribute.

Limits & what broke
  • Dimension-preserving latents. Every layer must keep all \(D\) dimensions, so expressiveness comes from depth. Glow's high-resolution models are very large and expensive for likelihoods that still trail autoregressive models.
  • Topology. A continuous bijection cannot turn one Gaussian blob into well-separated modes without stretching thin bridges of probability between them, which shows up as off-manifold samples.
  • Likelihood misleads. Nalisnick et al. (2019) found that flows trained on CIFAR-10 assign higher likelihood to SVHN images than to CIFAR-10 test images, so a density model's likelihood is not a reliable out-of-distribution detector.
  • Dequantization. Pixels are discrete; a continuous density must be trained on pixels plus uniform noise, or it can put unbounded density on the discrete points. Reported bits/dim depend on doing this correctly.
  • What superseded it. Diffusion models beat flows on sample quality per parameter within two years.

Bridge

The change-of-variables machinery did not die. Its continuous-time version, the instantaneous change of variables of continuous normalizing flows, computes exact likelihoods through diffusion's probability-flow ODE (Day 262), and flow matching (Day 269) trains continuous flows without ever computing a determinant. Tomorrow (Day 259) is the 2015 paper that started diffusion, five years before anyone noticed.

Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics

45m videoLevel 4

Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan & Ganguli, ICML 2015 (arXiv:1503.03585)

Destroy the structure in data with a slow, fixed diffusion process until only noise is left, then learn to run that process backwards. Because the reverse of a sufficiently small diffusion step has the same functional form as the forward step, every reverse step can be a simple Gaussian, and the whole chain can be trained with a variational bound.

Cold open

A swiss roll of 2-D points dissolves, step by step, into a shapeless Gaussian cloud. Then a learned process runs the film backwards and the roll reassembles. This is the diffusion model, complete with forward process, reverse process and variational bound, in 2015. It sat largely unused for five years, until DDPM (Day 260).

The problem

The paper frames generative modelling as a tradeoff between tractability (densities you can evaluate and sample exactly, like a Gaussian) and flexibility (models that can fit arbitrary data, like energy-based models, whose normalizing constants require expensive Monte Carlo). Deep models of 2015 typically gave up one for the other. The idea borrowed from statistical physics, from annealed importance sampling and the Jarzynski equality, is to build a flexible distribution out of many tractable steps.

The method: forward and reverse chains

Definition · the two processes

Forward (fixed): \(q(x^{(0:T)}) = q(x^{(0)})\prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x^{(t)}\mid x^{(t-1)})\) with Gaussian kernels \(q(x^{(t)}\mid x^{(t-1)}) = \mathcal N\big(x^{(t)};\, \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x^{(t-1)},\, \beta_t I\big)\), or binomial bit-flip kernels for binary data. After enough steps, \(x^{(T)}\) is essentially standard normal whatever the data was.

Reverse (learned): \(p(x^{(0:T)}) = \pi(x^{(T)})\prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)})\) with \(p(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)}) = \mathcal N\big(\mu_\theta(x^{(t)}, t),\, \Sigma_\theta(x^{(t)}, t)\big)\). A neural network outputs the mean and covariance of each reverse step.

The forward process contains no neural network. It is a fixed family of Gaussian kernels whose only free quantities are the \(T\) scalars \(\beta_t\). That is unusual and important: the "encoder" of this latent-variable model needs no training, and its posteriors are available in closed form. (Precisely: for Gaussian diffusion the 2015 paper does tune the schedule \(\beta_2, \dots, \beta_T\) by gradient ascent on its bound, holding \(\beta_1\) fixed and small; DDPM later fixes every \(\beta_t\) as a hyperparameter, which is when "no learned parameters in the forward process" becomes literally true.)

The key physical fact

Result · reversal of small diffusion steps (Feller 1949; the paper's justification)

In the limit of small step size \(\beta\), the time-reversal of a Gaussian diffusion process is again a Gaussian diffusion, with the same functional form as the forward process. The same holds for binomial diffusion.

This is what makes the method possible: if each reverse step is Gaussian, the network only has to predict a mean and a covariance, not an arbitrary distribution. It is worth seeing why it is true.

Derivation · why a small reverse step is Gaussian (heuristic)

Ignore the \(\sqrt{1-\beta}\) shrink for clarity, so \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N(x_t; x_{t-1}, \beta I)\). By Bayes, the true reverse conditional is

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) \propto \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\|x_t - x_{t-1}\|^2}{2\beta}\Big)\; p_{t-1}(x_{t-1}).$$

The Gaussian factor confines \(x_{t-1}\) to a ball of radius about \(\sqrt\beta\) around \(x_t\). If \(\beta\) is small compared with the scale on which \(p_{t-1}\) varies, a first-order Taylor expansion inside that ball is accurate: \(\log p_{t-1}(x_{t-1}) \approx \log p_{t-1}(x_t) + (x_{t-1} - x_t)^\top\nabla\log p_{t-1}(x_t)\). A Gaussian times the exponential of a linear function is a Gaussian, so completing the square,

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) \approx \mathcal N\big(x_{t-1};\; x_t + \beta\,\nabla\log p_{t-1}(x_t),\;\beta I\big).$$

Same covariance as the forward step; the mean is nudged uphill along the gradient of the log-density. Keeping the \(\sqrt{1-\beta}\) factor adds a further \(O(\beta)\) term to the mean and changes nothing qualitative. That gradient is the score, the object diffusion models turn out to learn (Day 262). The approximation breaks when steps are large, which is why the method needs many small ones.

The method: the bound, term by term

The model's likelihood is an integral over all reverse trajectories. Writing it as an expectation over forward trajectories, \(p(x^{(0)}) = \E_{q(x^{(1:T)}\mid x^{(0)})}\big[\pi(x^{(T)})\prod_t p(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)})/q(x^{(t)}\mid x^{(t-1)})\big]\), the same importance-weighting idea as annealed importance sampling. Applying Jensen's inequality to its log, and using Bayes' rule on each forward step, the paper obtains a bound \(K \le \E\log p(x^{(0)})\):

$$K = -\sum_{t=2}^{T}\E_{q}\Big[\KL\big(q(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)}, x^{(0)})\,\big\|\,p(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)})\big)\Big] + H_q(X^{(T)}\mid X^{(0)}) - H_q(X^{(1)}\mid X^{(0)}) - H_p(X^{(T)}).$$
  • The KL terms compare each learned reverse step with the forward process's posterior given the clean data, \(q(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)}, x^{(0)})\), which is Gaussian and known in closed form. Both sides are Gaussian, so each KL is analytic: no sampling inside the KL.
  • The entropy terms depend only on the forward process and the prior, so with a fixed schedule they are constants.

This is exactly the ELBO of a hierarchical VAE (⟵ Day 241) with \(T\) layers of latents \(x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(T)}\), all the same size as the data, a fixed encoder \(q\), and a learned decoder chain \(p\). Everything that made VAEs hard (learning the encoder, posterior collapse) is gone, because nobody learns the encoder.

A bonus: conditioning by multiplication. Because each step is Gaussian, multiplying the model by a second distribution \(r(x)\), for example "agree with these known pixels", only perturbs each reverse step's mean and covariance. The paper uses this for inpainting and denoising: an early form of guided sampling.

Evidence

The experiments are toy-to-small: the 2-D swiss roll; a binary "heartbeat" sequence with a regular pulse that the model learns to reproduce; MNIST; CIFAR-10; dead-leaves images; and inpainting of bark textures. The authors report log-likelihood bounds and show samples. The toy results are clean. The natural-image samples are recognizably textured but far from GAN quality of the time, let alone what diffusion would reach in 2020. What the evidence shows is that the framework is sound and trainable; what it does not show is that it can compete.

Limits & what broke
  • Sample quality. Five years of GAN progress happened while this paper waited. The missing pieces were engineering: a fixed variance schedule, predicting the noise rather than the mean, a U-Net, and a reweighted loss. All four arrive tomorrow.
  • Sampling cost. Small steps are required for the Gaussian reversal to hold, so sampling needs many sequential network evaluations. This remained diffusion's weakness for years (Day 261, Day 268).
  • Learning the covariance. Predicting a full reverse covariance per step made training harder; DDPM fixed it, and Improved DDPM later learned it more carefully.
  • The honest history lesson. The idea was right and the engineering was not there yet. Score-based models (Song & Ermon 2019) arrived at nearly the same place from a different direction before Ho et al. joined the two.

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM)

60m videoLevel 3Second keystone episode

Ho, Jain & Abbeel, NeurIPS 2020 (arXiv:2006.11239)

Keep the 2015 diffusion model, fix the variances, make the network predict the added noise, and drop the bound's weights to get a plain mean-squared error. That objective produced the first diffusion samples to rival GANs.

Cold open

Unconditional CIFAR-10: the abstract reports an Inception Score of 9.46 and an FID of 3.17, which the authors note beats most models in the literature, including class-conditional ones. LSUN samples at 256×256 look comparable to ProgressiveGAN's. No discriminator, no game: the loss is a mean-squared error on noise.

The problem

Diffusion models (Day 259) had a sound bound and weak samples; score-based models (Song & Ermon 2019) did better but still trailed GANs. This paper's answer is three small decisions.

The method: the forward process and its closed form

With a fixed schedule \(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T\), define \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t}\alpha_s\). The forward process adds Gaussian noise while shrinking the signal:

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal N\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big).$$
Derivation · the workhorse identity q(x_t | x_0)

Claim: \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\). True at \(t = 1\); assume it at \(t - 1\) and add one step with fresh noise \(\varepsilon_t\):

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\bar\varepsilon\big) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \underbrace{\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\bar\varepsilon + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t}_{\text{sum of independent Gaussians}}.$$

The noise has variance \(\alpha_t - \bar\alpha_t + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t\). Hence

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal N\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,I\big).$$

Use 1: training is cheap. No simulating \(t\) steps of the chain: draw \(\varepsilon\) and jump straight to \(x_t\), with a different random \(t\) per example.

The method: the tractable posterior and the bound

Derivation · q(x_{t−1} | x_t, x_0)

The reverse conditional \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) depends on the unknown data distribution, but conditioned on \(x_0\) it is a product of two known Gaussians in \(x_{t-1}\): \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\,q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)\). Adding their precisions, \(\alpha_t/\beta_t + 1/(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) = (1-\bar\alpha_t)/\big(\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\big)\), so

$$q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N\big(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I\big),\quad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t,\quad \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t.$$

The negative ELBO (⟵ Day 241) is yesterday's bound with this posterior inside:

$$L = \E_q\Big[\underbrace{\KL\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\|\,p(x_T)\big)}_{L_T} + \sum_{t>1}\underbrace{\KL\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}}\ \underbrace{-\log p_\theta(x_0\mid x_1)}_{L_0}\Big].$$

Decision 1: fix the variances. Set \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) = \mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t, t), \sigma_t^2 I)\) with \(\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t\) or \(\tilde\beta_t\) (similar results, the authors report). With forward variances fixed too, \(L_T\) is a constant, and each KL becomes a distance between means:

$$L_{t-1} = \E_q\Big[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\big\|\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\big\|^2\Big] + C.$$

The method: predict the noise

Derivation · from mean-matching to ε-prediction

Use 2 of the closed form: invert it, \(x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\), and substitute into \(\tilde\mu_t\). Using \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\) and \(\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t\), the \(x_t\) coefficients combine to \(1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\):

$$\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big).$$

Decision 2: since \(x_t\) is the network's input, the only unknown is \(\varepsilon\). So parameterize \(\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big)\). Then

$$L_{t-1} - C = \E_{x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[\underbrace{\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)}}_{w_t}\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big].$$

Decision 3: drop the weights. The training loss is

$$L_{\text{simple}} = \E_{t\sim\mathcal U\{1,\dots,T\},\ x_0,\ \varepsilon}\Big[\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big].$$

The \(t = 1\) term stands in for \(L_0\), and \(L_T\) is dropped. \(L_{\text{simple}}\) is not the ELBO; it is a reweighted version of it. With \(\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t\) the dropped weight \(w_t = \beta_t/\big(2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)\big)\) is about \(\tfrac12\) at \(t = 1\) and falls roughly 50× within the first hundred steps, then stays nearly flat. Relative to the bound, \(L_{\text{simple}}\) down-weights low-noise steps, which the authors say lets the network concentrate on the harder denoising at larger \(t\).

Be precise about "ε-prediction reweights"

With the true weights, ε-prediction and mean-matching are the same objective; dropping \(w_t\) does the reweighting, and "toward high noise" is relative to the bound. Since \(\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\|^2 = \mathrm{SNR}(t)\,\|x_0 - \hat x_0\|^2\), \(\mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\), an unweighted ε-loss favours low-noise steps compared with unweighted \(x_0\)-regression. Predicting ε was an empirical choice.

import torch

T = 1000
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)            # linear schedule
alphas = 1.0 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, dim=0)

def loss_simple(eps_model, x0):                  # Algorithm 1; x0 in [-1, 1]
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (x0.shape[0],))
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    a = abar[t].view(-1, *[1] * (x0.dim() - 1))
    xt = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps    # closed form, no chain
    return ((eps - eps_model(xt, t)) ** 2).mean()

@torch.no_grad()
def sample(eps_model, shape):                    # Algorithm 2, sigma_t^2 = beta_t
    x = torch.randn(shape)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):                 # 1000 sequential calls
        eps = eps_model(x, torch.full((shape[0],), t))
        x = (x - betas[t] / (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps) / alphas[t].sqrt()
        if t > 0:
            x = x + betas[t].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(x)
    return x

The sampling line is \(x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big) + \sigma_t z\), with no noise added at the final step.

The method: architecture and schedule

\(\varepsilon_\theta\) is a U-Net (⟵ Day 92) on a PixelCNN++-like backbone, with group normalization, self-attention at 16×16, and a sinusoidal timestep embedding (⟵ Day 129) added in each residual block; one network serves all \(T\) steps. \(T = 1000\), \(\beta_t\) linear from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\): small relative to data in \([-1, 1]\), so each reverse step is close to Gaussian (Day 259), while \(x_T\) ends nearly independent of \(x_0\).

Two connections to name. Each \(\varepsilon_\theta(\cdot, t)\) is a denoising autoencoder at one noise level (⟵ Day 53). And since \(\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), regressing \(\varepsilon\) is denoising score matching: \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \approx -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t)\). The authors note the sampler then resembles Langevin dynamics with a learned gradient of the data density (⟶ Day 262).

Evidence

The ablation of parameterization and objective is the table to read. Predicting \(\tilde\mu\) works only with the true bound; predicting \(\varepsilon\) is comparable under the bound and much better with \(L_{\text{simple}}\); learning a diagonal reverse variance made training unstable. Likelihood points the other way: the authors report about 3.7 bits/dim for the bound-trained model and slightly worse for \(L_{\text{simple}}\), both behind the best autoregressive models, and show most of the codelength goes to imperceptible detail.

Limits & what broke
  • Likelihood and quality diverge. The best-sample loss is not the bound, so bits/dim and FID come from different training choices. Say which you mean.
  • Sampling cost is the obvious weakness. 1,000 sequential U-Net calls per sample versus one GAN pass: on the triangle, quality and coverage, no speed. DDIM (Day 261) and consistency models (Day 268) attack this.
  • Fixed variances and the linear schedule cap likelihood and waste steps at low resolution; Improved DDPM fixes both (Day 261).
  • Pixel space is expensive at high resolution; latent diffusion addresses it (Day 264).

Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models + Improved DDPM

40m videoLevel 3

Song, Meng & Ermon ICLR 2021 (arXiv:2010.02502); Nichol & Dhariwal ICML 2021 (arXiv:2102.09672)

DDPM's training loss depends only on the marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), so any forward process with the same marginals shares it. DDIM picks a non-Markovian one whose reverse can be deterministic and can skip steps, giving fast sampling from an unchanged DDPM network. Improved DDPM fixes the noise schedule and learns the reverse variances, recovering likelihood and making few-step sampling work too.

Cold open

The same trained network, sampled in 20 to 100 steps instead of 1,000. The DDIM abstract reports high-quality samples 10× to 50× faster in wall-clock time than DDPM. And because the fast sampler is deterministic, a noise vector now names an image: interpolate between two noise vectors and the images interpolate meaningfully; run the sampler backwards and you recover a noise vector for a real image.

The problem

DDPM (Day 260) needs 1,000 sequential network calls per sample, and simply skipping steps in its stochastic sampler degrades samples badly. Its likelihoods trailed autoregressive models, it fixed the reverse variances by hand, and its linear schedule was designed by eye. One paper attacks speed; the other attacks everything else.

The method: DDIM

Notation warning: the DDIM paper writes \(\alpha_t\) for what DDPM calls \(\bar\alpha_t\). This lesson keeps DDPM's \(\bar\alpha_t\) throughout.

Definition · a non-Markovian family with DDPM's marginals

For any choice of \(\sigma_t \ge 0\), define the inference process through

$$q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal N\Big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2}\;\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},\ \sigma_t^2 I\Big).$$
Derivation · the marginals are unchanged

Suppose \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\). Then the fraction above is exactly \(\varepsilon\), and a draw from \(q_\sigma\) is \(x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\,\varepsilon + \sigma_t z\). Its noise has variance \((1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1} - \sigma_t^2) + \sigma_t^2 = 1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}\). So \(q_\sigma(x_{t-1}\mid x_0) = \mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})I)\), DDPM's marginal, and by induction from \(t = T\) every marginal matches. The implied forward process \(q_\sigma(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0)\) depends on \(x_0\): it is not Markovian, but nothing in training needed it to be.

Theorem · same objective (DDIM's Theorem 1, informal)

For every \(\sigma > 0\), the variational objective \(J_\sigma\) of the model built on \(q_\sigma\) equals a weighted DDPM loss \(L_\gamma\) plus a constant. If the network's parameters are not shared across \(t\), the minimizer of \(L_\gamma\) does not depend on the weights \(\gamma\), so a network trained with \(L_{\text{simple}}\) is a valid \(\varepsilon_\theta\) for every member of the family.

In practice parameters are shared across \(t\), so this is an argument rather than a guarantee, but it is the reason no retraining is needed, and the reason DDIM was adopted instantly.

Algorithm · the DDIM update
  1. Predict the clean image: \(\hat x_0 = \big(x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\big)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\).
  2. Step: \(x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) + \sigma_t z\): predicted \(x_0\), plus the direction pointing to \(x_t\), plus fresh noise.
  3. With \(\sigma_t = \eta\sqrt{(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})/(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\,\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t/\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\): \(\eta = 1\) gives \(\sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t\), exactly DDPM's sampler; \(\eta = 0\) gives the deterministic DDIM.
  4. To skip steps, run the same update on a strided subsequence \(\tau_1 < \dots < \tau_S\) of \(\{1, \dots, T\}\), replacing \(t, t-1\) by \(\tau_i, \tau_{i-1}\). Only \(\bar\alpha\) at those times enters.
Derivation · deterministic DDIM is an ODE solver

Set \(\sigma_t = 0\) and divide the update by \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\). With \(\bar x = x/\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\) and \(\bar\sigma = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\), and since \(\hat x_0 = \bar x_t - \bar\sigma_t\,\varepsilon_\theta\),

$$\bar x_{t-1} = \bar x_t + (\bar\sigma_{t-1} - \bar\sigma_t)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t),$$

an Euler step for \(d\bar x = \varepsilon_\theta\,d\bar\sigma\). DDIM is a numerical solver for an ODE; the authors note it matches the probability-flow ODE of a variance-exploding SDE (Day 262). Fewer, larger steps are just a coarser discretization of the same trajectory.

What determinism buys. The map from \(x_T\) to \(x_0\) is a fixed function, invertible by running the ODE the other way. The authors show consistency (the same \(x_T\) gives images with the same high-level content whether you use 20 or 1,000 steps), semantically meaningful interpolation by spherical interpolation between two \(x_T\), and near-exact reconstruction by encoding an image to \(x_T\) and decoding it back. DDPM's stochastic sampler cannot do any of these. This invertible map between noise and image is what later image-editing methods build on.

The method: Improved DDPM

  • Cosine schedule. \(\bar\alpha_t = f(t)/f(0)\) with \(f(t) = \cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{t/T + s}{1 + s}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)\), \(s = 0.008\), and \(\beta_t\) clipped at 0.999 near the end. The motivation is the authors' observation that the linear schedule destroys information too fast at low resolution: on 64×64 images, skipping up to the last fifth of the reverse process barely changed FID. The cosine schedule spends steps where they matter.
  • Learned variances. DDPM's two choices \(\beta_t\) and \(\tilde\beta_t\) are the extremes of a reasonable range, nearly equal except at small \(t\), where most of the likelihood bound comes from. The network outputs a vector \(v\) and the variance interpolates in log space: \(\Sigma_\theta = \exp\big(v\log\beta_t + (1-v)\log\tilde\beta_t\big)\).
  • Hybrid objective. \(L_{\text{hybrid}} = L_{\text{simple}} + \lambda L_{\text{vlb}}\) with \(\lambda = 0.001\), with a stop-gradient on the mean inside \(L_{\text{vlb}}\), so the bound trains only the variances while \(L_{\text{simple}}\) still drives the mean.
  • Importance-sampled bound. Optimizing \(L_{\text{vlb}}\) directly is very noisy because a few early terms dominate. Sampling \(t\) with probability \(p_t \propto \sqrt{\E[L_t^2]}\), estimated from a running history of the last ten values of each term, reduces that noise; the authors report it gives their best likelihoods.

Evidence

DDIM sweeps the number of steps (from 10 to 1,000) against \(\eta\) on CIFAR-10 and CelebA. The authors report that the deterministic sampler (\(\eta = 0\)) gives the best FID at small step counts while DDPM's (\(\eta = 1\)) degrades sharply as steps are removed, and that a few dozen DDIM steps approach the quality of 1,000 DDPM steps. Improved DDPM reports that learned variances and the cosine schedule make likelihoods competitive with strong likelihood-based models, and that with learned variances, strided sampling with about 50 steps keeps most of the sample quality. Comparing samplers, they report DDIM is better below about 50 steps and their learned-variance sampler at 50 or more. Against BigGAN-deep at similar FID, their models show higher recall, evidence for diffusion's mode-coverage corner of the triangle, and quality improves smoothly as model size and training compute grow.

Limits & what broke
  • Few steps still hurt. At around 10 DDIM steps samples are visibly degraded. Higher-order ODE solvers (DPM-Solver, Lu et al. 2022; the EDM sampler, Karras et al. 2022) and distillation (Day 268) pushed further.
  • Inversion is approximate. With coarse steps, encode-then-decode drifts, and with classifier-free guidance (Day 263) naive DDIM inversion breaks down; later editing work had to correct it.
  • Schedules depend on resolution. The cosine schedule was tuned at 64×64; at higher resolutions the same \(\bar\alpha_t\) leaves too much signal, and later work shifts the schedule toward more noise.
  • Theorem 1's assumption. The no-retraining argument assumes unshared parameters across \(t\). It works in practice, but it is not a guarantee.

Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations

55m videoLevel 4

Song, Sohl-Dickstein, Kingma, Kumar, Ermon & Poole, ICLR 2021 (arXiv:2011.13456)

Let the number of noise levels go to infinity and diffusion becomes a stochastic differential equation. Reversing it requires only the score \(\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\) at every time, which a network learns by denoising, and the same score defines a deterministic ODE with identical marginals. DDPM, score-matching models and DDIM all become special cases of one framework.

Warning · the hardest lesson of the month

This lesson uses stochastic differential equations and the Fokker–Planck equation. If it does not land on first viewing, Day 261 carries the practical consequences (deterministic sampling, few steps, inversion) without the stochastic calculus, and the two boxed results below, "ε is a rescaled score" and "conditional score = score + classifier gradient", are enough for the rest of the month.

Cold open

The abstract reports an unconditional CIFAR-10 Inception Score of 9.89 and FID of 2.20, a likelihood of 2.99 bits/dim, and the first 1024×1024 images from a score-based model. More important than any number: one equation that contains every diffusion model of the previous two lessons.

The problem

Two lineages looked unrelated. Score matching with Langevin dynamics (SMLD, Song & Ermon 2019) learned the gradient of the log-density at a ladder of noise levels and sampled with annealed Langevin dynamics. DDPM (Day 260) maximized a reweighted ELBO and sampled ancestrally. Both used a fixed, discrete set of noise levels, neither offered exact likelihoods, and their samplers were ad hoc.

The method: scores and why noise is needed

The score of a density is \(s(x) = \nabla_x\log p(x)\). It ignores the normalizing constant, since \(\nabla\log(\tilde p/Z) = \nabla\log\tilde p\), which is why it is learnable when \(p\) is not. Given the score, Langevin dynamics samples from \(p\) (⟵ Day 55 for MCMC in learning):

$$x_{k+1} = x_k + \eta\,\nabla_x\log p(x_k) + \sqrt{2\eta}\,z_k,\qquad z_k\sim\mathcal N(0, I),$$

exact in the limit of small \(\eta\) and many steps. Denoising score matching (Vincent 2011) learns the score of a noised density without knowing \(p\): regress \(s_\theta(\tilde x)\) onto \(\nabla_{\tilde x}\log q(\tilde x\mid x) = -(\tilde x - x)/\sigma^2\); the minimizer is the score of the noised marginal. Why many noise levels: real data lies near a low-dimensional manifold, where the score of the clean density is undefined off the manifold, and in low-density regions there are too few samples to estimate it, so Langevin started far from the data is lost. Heavy noise fills space with usable scores; light noise is accurate near the data. Annealing from heavy to light gets both.

The method: forward and reverse SDEs

Replace the ladder with a continuum \(t \in [0, T]\) and a forward SDE \(dx = f(x, t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw\). Two families recover the two lineages:

  • Variance exploding (VE): \(dx = \sqrt{d[\sigma^2(t)]/dt}\;dw\). No drift, growing variance: the continuum limit of SMLD's noise ladder.
  • Variance preserving (VP): \(dx = -\tfrac12\beta(t)\,x\,dt + \sqrt{\beta(t)}\,dw\). DDPM is a discretized VP SDE: with \(\beta_i = \beta(t)\Delta t\), DDPM's step \(x_i = \sqrt{1-\beta_i}\,x_{i-1} + \sqrt{\beta_i}\,z\) and \(\sqrt{1 - \beta\Delta t} \approx 1 - \tfrac12\beta\Delta t\) give \(x_{t+\Delta t} - x_t \approx -\tfrac12\beta x\,\Delta t + \sqrt{\beta\Delta t}\,z\), an Euler–Maruyama step of the VP SDE. Its marginal is DDPM's closed form with \(\bar\alpha(t) = e^{-\int_0^t\beta(s)ds}\).
Theorem · reverse-time SDE (Anderson 1982)

The forward SDE's time reversal is the diffusion

$$dx = \big[f(x, t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]\,dt + g(t)\,d\bar w,$$

run from \(t = T\) down to 0, where \(d\bar w\) is Brownian motion in reverse time. The only unknown is the score of each marginal \(p_t\).

So train a time-conditioned network with continuous-time denoising score matching, \(\min_\theta \E_t\,\lambda(t)\,\E_{x_0}\E_{x_t\mid x_0}\|s_\theta(x_t, t) - \nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t\mid x_0)\|^2\), then integrate the reverse SDE with any numerical solver.

Derivation · ε-prediction is a rescaled score

For the VP SDE, \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\), so the regression target is \(\nabla_{x_t}\log p_{0t}(x_t\mid x_0) = -(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0)/(1-\bar\alpha_t) = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). Parameterize \(s_\theta = -\varepsilon_\theta/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). Then

$$\big\|s_\theta - \nabla\log p_{0t}\big\|^2 = \frac{\|\varepsilon_\theta - \varepsilon\|^2}{1 - \bar\alpha_t}.$$

The paper weights by \(\lambda(t) \propto 1/\E\|\nabla\log p_{0t}\|^2\), which here is \(\propto 1 - \bar\alpha_t\), and the loss becomes exactly DDPM's \(L_{\text{simple}}\). \(\varepsilon_\theta(x, t) = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;s_\theta(x, t)\): a noise predictor is a score estimator. VAEs' bound, DDPM's MSE and score matching are one object seen three ways.

The method: the probability-flow ODE

Derivation · a deterministic flow with the same marginals

The marginals of the forward SDE (scalar \(g\)) obey the Fokker–Planck equation \(\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot(f p_t) + \tfrac12 g^2\Delta p_t\). Since \(\nabla p = p\,\nabla\log p\), the diffusion term is \(\tfrac12g^2\nabla\cdot(p_t\nabla\log p_t)\), so

$$\partial_t p_t = -\nabla\cdot\Big[\big(f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla\log p_t\big)\,p_t\Big].$$

That is the continuity equation of particles moving deterministically with velocity \(f - \tfrac12g^2\nabla\log p_t\). Hence the ODE

$$dx = \big[f(x, t) - \tfrac12 g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]\,dt$$

transports \(p_0\) to \(p_T\) through exactly the same marginals as the SDE, with no noise at all.

Two things follow, and they are why deterministic samplers and likelihood evaluation both exist. Deterministic sampling: any ODE solver can generate, and DDIM's update is one discretization (Day 261); the map from noise to data is invertible, so images have unique latent codes. Exact likelihood: the ODE is a continuous normalizing flow, so the instantaneous change of variables (the continuous version of Day 258) gives \(\log p_0(x_0) = \log p_T(x_T) + \int_0^T \nabla\cdot\tilde f_\theta(x_t, t)\,dt\), with \(\tilde f_\theta\) the ODE's velocity and the divergence estimated by Hutchinson's trace estimator.

The method: samplers and control

Algorithm · predictor–corrector sampling
  1. Predictor: one step of a numerical solver for the reverse SDE (for example reverse diffusion or Euler–Maruyama), moving from \(t\) to \(t - \Delta t\).
  2. Corrector: a few Langevin steps at the new time using \(s_\theta(\cdot, t-\Delta t)\), pulling the sample back toward the correct marginal \(p_{t-\Delta t}\).
  3. Repeat to \(t = 0\). Ancestral DDPM sampling is predictor-only; SMLD's annealed Langevin is corrector-only.
Result · controllable generation by adding scores

By Bayes' rule, \(p_t(x\mid y) \propto p_t(x)\,p_t(y\mid x)\), and the normalizer does not depend on \(x\), so

$$\nabla_x\log p_t(x\mid y) = \nabla_x\log p_t(x) + \nabla_x\log p_t(y\mid x).$$

Plug this conditional score into the reverse SDE and an unconditional model samples conditionally, given the gradient of a classifier \(p_t(y\mid x_t)\) trained on noisy inputs. The paper uses this for class-conditional CIFAR-10 and, with a known forward model, for inpainting and colourization without retraining.

Evidence

The authors compare samplers at matched compute and report that predictor–corrector sampling beats predictor-only and corrector-only samplers for both VE and VP models. With improved architectures (named NCSN++ and DDPM++) and the continuous objective, they obtain the CIFAR-10 numbers above; the likelihood figure comes from the probability-flow ODE. High-resolution CelebA-HQ samples, class-conditional samples with a noise-conditional classifier, and inpainting and colourization demos round out the evidence.

Limits & what broke
  • Expensive. The best FIDs came from SDE samplers with on the order of thousands of network evaluations, and ODE likelihoods need adaptive solvers plus a stochastic trace estimate.
  • Weighting matters. The \(\lambda(t)\) that gives good samples is not the one that maximizes likelihood; later work derived a likelihood weighting for which the loss bounds the KL.
  • Classifier guidance is clumsy. It needs a separate classifier trained on noisy images. Tomorrow's papers make it practical, then remove the classifier entirely (Day 263).
  • The ODE trajectories are curved. Curvature is what forces many solver steps, which later motivates consistency models (Day 268) and straight-path flows (Day 269).

Bridge

The conditional-score equation above is the equation that makes tomorrow obvious: guidance is adding a scaled classifier gradient to the score (Day 263). Prerequisites: ⟵ Day 260, ⟵ Day 261, ⟵ Day 55.

Classifier Guidance and Classifier-Free Guidance

45m videoLevel 3

Dhariwal & Nichol NeurIPS 2021 (arXiv:2105.05233); Ho & Salimans 2022 (arXiv:2207.12598)

Guidance pushes every denoising step toward images that are more typical of the condition. Classifier guidance adds a scaled gradient from a classifier trained on noisy images; classifier-free guidance gets the same effect from a single network trained with the condition sometimes dropped, by extrapolating away from its unconditional prediction. One scale then trades diversity for fidelity.

Cold open

"Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis": the abstract reports ImageNet FIDs of 2.97 at 128×128, 4.59 at 256×256 and 7.72 at 512×512, and matching BigGAN-deep with as few as 25 forward passes per sample. A year later, classifier-free guidance became the knob that made text-to-image models usable; nearly every image generator since exposes it as "guidance scale".

The problem

Conditional diffusion models still trailed BigGAN (Day 255) on ImageNet FID, and they had no equivalent of the truncation trick: no way to buy fidelity with diversity at sampling time. Day 262 had shown the route, \(\nabla\log p_t(x\mid y) = \nabla\log p_t(x) + \nabla\log p_t(y\mid x)\), but not how to make it work at scale.

The method: a better U-Net

Dhariwal and Nichol first ablate the architecture of Improved DDPM (Day 261). Changes they kept include: more depth relative to width; more attention heads (64 channels per head); attention at 32×32, 16×16 and 8×8 rather than only 16×16; BigGAN-style residual blocks for upsampling and downsampling; and adaptive group normalization, \(\mathrm{AdaGN}(h, y) = y_s\,\mathrm{GroupNorm}(h) + y_b\), where \(y = (y_s, y_b)\) is projected from the timestep and class embeddings, a cousin of StyleGAN's AdaIN (Day 251). The resulting model is often called ADM.

The method: classifier guidance

Derivation · shifting the mean of a reverse step

Let a reverse step be \(p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1}) = \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)\). The conditional step is proportional to \(p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})\,p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\). The Gaussian is narrow, so expand the classifier's log-probability to first order around \(\mu\): \(\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t) \approx (x_t - \mu)^\top g + C\) with \(g = \nabla_x\log p_\phi(y\mid x)\big|_{x=\mu}\). Then

$$\log\big[p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{t+1})\,p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\big] \approx -\tfrac12(x_t-\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x_t-\mu) + (x_t-\mu)^\top g + C' = \log\mathcal N(x_t;\ \mu + \Sigma g,\ \Sigma) + C''.$$

So guided sampling draws from \(\mathcal N(\mu + s\,\Sigma g, \Sigma)\), with a guidance scale \(s\). For deterministic (DDIM) sampling the same idea, via the score identity of Day 262, becomes \(\hat\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\theta(x_t) - s\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\nabla_{x_t}\log p_\phi(y\mid x_t)\).

Two details matter. The classifier must be trained on noisy images \(x_t\), with the timestep as input, because guidance queries it at every noise level; an ordinary ImageNet classifier gives meaningless gradients on noise. And the scale must be above 1: since \(s\,\nabla\log p(y\mid x) = \nabla\log p(y\mid x)^s\) up to normalization, scaling sharpens the classifier's distribution, concentrating samples on its modes. The authors report that with \(s = 1\) an unconditional model's samples were often not recognizably of the requested class, while larger scales gave class-consistent, higher-fidelity, less diverse samples.

The method: classifier-free guidance

Algorithm · classifier-free guidance (Ho & Salimans)
  1. Train one network \(\varepsilon_\theta(z, c)\), replacing the condition by a null token \(\varnothing\) with probability \(p_{\text{uncond}}\) (they compare 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5; the two smaller values work better). It learns the conditional and unconditional model at once.
  2. Sample with \(\tilde\varepsilon_\theta(z, c) = (1 + w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(z, c) - w\,\varepsilon_\theta(z, \varnothing)\) in place of \(\varepsilon_\theta\), at a cost of two network evaluations per step (batched together).
Derivation · the implicit classifier

Bayes gives an implicit classifier \(p^i(c\mid z) \propto p(z\mid c)/p(z)\), so \(\nabla_z\log p^i(c\mid z) = \nabla_z\log p(z\mid c) - \nabla_z\log p(z)\). In noise-prediction units (score \(= -\varepsilon/\sigma\)) that is \(-\tfrac1\sigma\big[\varepsilon(z, c) - \varepsilon(z, \varnothing)\big]\). Classifier guidance with weight \(w\) on this implicit classifier, added to the conditional score, gives

$$\tilde\varepsilon = \varepsilon(z, c) + w\big[\varepsilon(z, c) - \varepsilon(z, \varnothing)\big] = (1 + w)\,\varepsilon(z, c) - w\,\varepsilon(z, \varnothing).$$

The extrapolation is the whole story. Small \(w\) exaggerates exactly what makes an image look like its condition. Large \(w\) pushes predictions outside the range the network saw in training: colours saturate, contrast blows out, and diversity collapses toward a few stereotyped images. Imagen's dynamic thresholding (Day 266) was invented to tame this regime. Strictly, \(\tilde\varepsilon\) is not the score of any particular noised distribution, so guided sampling has no exact target; it is a heuristic that works.

Practical note · which "w"?

Two conventions are in use. Ho and Salimans write \((1 + w)\varepsilon_c - w\,\varepsilon_\varnothing\), so \(w = 0\) is the plain conditional model. Stable Diffusion's code writes \(\varepsilon_\varnothing + s(\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_\varnothing)\), so \(s = 1 + w\) and \(s = 1\) is the plain conditional model. Its reference scripts default to \(s = 7.5\), which is \(w = 6.5\) in the paper's notation. Practitioners commonly report preferring lower scales, roughly 1.5–3, for photorealistic images, and higher ones for illustration; treat these ranges as folklore that depends on the model, not as results from either paper.

Evidence

Dhariwal and Nichol's architecture ablation reports FID improving with each change above. Sweeping the classifier scale traces a curve: as \(s\) grows, precision and Inception Score rise while recall falls, and FID first improves and then worsens. Guidance also helps conditional models, and their best ImageNet numbers combine a conditional model with guidance. Ho and Salimans show the same tradeoff without any classifier on ImageNet 64×64 and 128×128: small guidance weights give the best FID, larger weights the best IS, mirroring classifier guidance and BigGAN's truncation. Guidance trades FID for Inception Score in a measurable way, which makes it the diffusion counterpart of the truncation dial.

Limits & what broke
  • Classifier guidance is a deployment burden. It needs a separately trained, noise-aware classifier for the label space you care about, plus a gradient through it at every step. For free-form text there is no such classifier. Removing it entirely is why CFG won.
  • CFG doubles the cost per step. Two forward passes (conditional and unconditional), later reduced by distilling the guided model into one network.
  • Coverage is spent. On the triangle, guidance moves diffusion from coverage toward quality, exactly as truncation did for GANs.
  • High-scale artifacts. Oversaturation and loss of detail at large scales; thresholding and rescaling tricks are workarounds, not fixes.

Bridge

Classifier-free guidance plus a text encoder is the recipe for text-to-image. Tomorrow (Day 264) makes it cheap enough to run on a consumer GPU by moving diffusion into a compressed latent space and injecting text with cross-attention. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 262.

High-Resolution Image Synthesis with Latent Diffusion Models (Stable Diffusion)

50m videoLevel 3

Rombach, Blattmann, Lorenz, Esser & Ommer, CVPR 2022 (arXiv:2112.10752)

Split generation into two stages: an autoencoder, trained once with perceptual and adversarial losses, compresses images into a smaller spatial latent that keeps what the eye notices, and a diffusion model learns the distribution of those latents. With 4–8× downsampling per side this cuts compute dramatically at little cost in quality, and cross-attention lets one U-Net take text, layouts or classes.

Cold open

Diffusion that runs on a consumer GPU. In August 2022 a latent diffusion model trained on LAION image–text pairs was released as Stable Diffusion with downloadable weights; within months it ran on laptops and inside image editors and had spawned thousands of community fine-tunes. The paper behind it is mostly an argument about where to spend bits.

The problem

Pixel-space diffusion (Day 263) is expensive. The authors cite hundreds of GPU-days to train the strongest pixel-space models, and every sampling step runs a large U-Net at full resolution. Worse, much of that compute is wasted. Their rate–distortion analysis of a trained pixel-space diffusion model shows a perceptual compression phase, where most of the bits encode high-frequency detail nobody can see, followed by a semantic compression phase, where the actual content of the image is decided. A likelihood-style model spends most of its capacity on the first.

The method: two stages

Stage 1, perceptual compression. An encoder \(\mathcal E\) maps \(x \in \R^{H\times W\times 3}\) to \(z = \mathcal E(x) \in \R^{h\times w\times c}\) with downsampling factor \(f = H/h\), and a decoder \(\mathcal D\) maps back. The autoencoder is trained with a perceptual loss and a patch-based adversarial loss, the VQGAN recipe (⟵ Day 244, ⟵ Day 253), so reconstructions are sharp even at high compression. To keep the latent well-behaved, one of two light regularizers is added: KL-reg, a very small KL penalty pulling \(q(z\mid x)\) toward \(\mathcal N(0, I)\) (weighted around \(10^{-6}\)), which keeps the latent near-Gaussian without turning the autoencoder into a blurry VAE; or VQ-reg, a vector-quantization layer absorbed into the decoder. Unlike VQGAN and DALL·E, the latent keeps its 2-D layout, so the second stage can still use a convolutional U-Net's inductive biases.

Stage 2, semantic compression. Freeze the autoencoder and train a diffusion model on latents with the usual objective (⟵ Day 260):

$$L_{\text{LDM}} = \E_{\mathcal E(x),\ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\ t}\Big[\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(z_t, t, \tau_\theta(y))\big\|_2^2\Big].$$

Because the autoencoder is frozen, the stages are decoupled: one autoencoder serves many diffusion models, and diffusion experiments never pay for perceptual detail.

Arithmetic · what f = 8 buys

A 512×512×3 image becomes a 64×64×4 latent. Spatial positions fall by \(f^2 = 64\) (from 262,144 to 4,096); stored values fall from 786,432 to 16,384, about 48×. Every convolution in the U-Net runs on 64× fewer positions, and each self-attention layer, whose cost grows with the square of the number of positions, is cheaper still. That is the difference between a datacentre job and a desktop GPU.

Cross-attention conditioning. A domain-specific encoder \(\tau_\theta\) maps the condition \(y\) to a sequence of \(M\) tokens. Inside the U-Net, flattened features \(\varphi_i(z_t)\) attend to them (⟵ Day 129):

$$\mathrm{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt d}\Big)V,\qquad Q = W_Q\,\varphi_i(z_t),\ K = W_K\,\tau_\theta(y),\ V = W_V\,\tau_\theta(y).$$

The image asks questions; the condition answers. Because the condition is just a token sequence, the same mechanism serves captions, bounding-box layouts and class labels; spatially aligned conditions such as semantic maps can instead be concatenated to \(z_t\). In the paper, \(\tau_\theta\) for text is a transformer trained jointly; Stable Diffusion v1 used a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder (Day 271).

The latent is not semantically meaningful. \(z\) is a compressed image, a 64×64 grid that looks like a small, oddly coloured version of the picture. Directions in it do not correspond to "smile" or "age" the way StyleGAN's \(\mathcal W\) does (Day 251). The semantics live in the diffusion model and the text encoder, not in the autoencoder; viewers routinely assume otherwise.

Evidence

Downsampling factor. Training class-conditional ImageNet models with \(f \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}\), the authors report that small factors (\(f = 1, 2\); \(f = 1\) is just pixel diffusion) train slowly, while \(f = 32\) stagnates early because the first stage has thrown away too much; \(f\) from 4 to 16 strikes a good balance. Plotting sampling speed against FID on CelebA-HQ and ImageNet, \(f = 4\) and \(f = 8\) give the best tradeoffs. Regularization. Both KL- and VQ-regularized latents work; the authors note VQ-regularized latents sometimes gave better samples despite slightly worse reconstructions. Tasks. One framework covers unconditional faces and scenes, class-conditional ImageNet, text-to-image (with classifier-free guidance, Day 263), layout-to-image, semantic synthesis, super-resolution and inpainting. The authors report state-of-the-art inpainting and class-conditional results and competitive numbers elsewhere, at a fraction of pixel-space compute.

Limits & what broke
  • The decoder sets a ceiling. Small text, fine faces in crowds and thin structures degrade in the autoencoder, and no diffusion model can recover them. Later systems raised the latent channel count for this reason.
  • Hidden constants. Latents must be rescaled to roughly unit variance before diffusion (a fixed factor of about 0.18 in Stable Diffusion v1); forgetting it silently breaks training.
  • Data. Training on web-scraped LAION brought copyright, consent and content problems, and Carlini et al. (2023) extracted near-copies of some training images from diffusion models.
  • Text understanding is borrowed. Compositional prompts ("a red cube on a blue sphere") fail in ways traceable to the text encoder, the subject of Day 266.

Bridge

"Compress, then model" (Day 243, Day 244) became the default pipeline. Tomorrow (Day 265) is the road not taken: DALL·E, which generated images from text with no diffusion at all. Later, DiT (Day 270) keeps this latent space and replaces the U-Net with a transformer.

Zero-Shot Text-to-Image Generation (DALL·E)

40m videoLevel 3

Ramesh et al., ICML 2021 (arXiv:2102.12092)

Text-to-image as plain language modelling. A discrete VAE turns each 256×256 image into a 32×32 grid of tokens from an 8,192-entry codebook; up to 256 text tokens and the 1,024 image tokens are concatenated into one sequence; and a 12-billion-parameter decoder-only transformer is trained to predict the next token. There is no diffusion here at all.

Cold open

"An armchair in the shape of an avocado." "An illustration of a baby daikon radish in a tutu walking a dog." The samples in the paper and its accompanying blog post combined concepts that never appear together in training data. Trained on 250 million image–text pairs from the internet and evaluated zero-shot on MS-COCO captions, the model was preferred by human raters over the best GAN of the time in a large majority of comparisons, for both realism and caption match, the authors report.

The problem

Text-to-image models in 2020 were GANs (AttnGAN, DM-GAN, DF-GAN) trained on MS-COCO or CUB birds: small datasets, elaborate architectures, auxiliary losses and extra supervision such as object parts or segmentation masks. They did not generalize to novel compositions. GPT-3 had just suggested that a large enough autoregressive transformer on enough data does many tasks at once. The bet: treat an image as more tokens in the sequence.

The method: stage 1, a discrete VAE

A transformer cannot model 256×256×3 = 196,608 pixel values per image. The dVAE compresses each image to a 32×32 grid of indices (downsampling \(f = 8\)), each one of \(K = 8192\) codes, shrinking the transformer's context by a factor of 192 with, the authors report, little loss in visual quality (⟵ Day 243). The encoder outputs a categorical distribution over the codes at each grid position; training maximizes an evidence lower bound with a uniform prior over codes.

The problem is the familiar one: the ELBO's expectation is over a discrete distribution, so the reparameterization trick of Day 241 does not apply. VQ-VAE used nearest-neighbour assignment with the straight-through estimator. DALL·E instead uses the Gumbel-softmax relaxation.

Derivation · the Gumbel-max trick and its relaxation

If \(g_1, \dots, g_K\) are i.i.d. Gumbel(0, 1) and \(\pi\) is a probability vector, then \(\argmax_k(\log\pi_k + g_k)\) is distributed exactly as \(\mathrm{Categorical}(\pi)\). Sampling becomes a deterministic function of \(\pi\) and independent noise, the same move as \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\), except that argmax has zero gradient. Replace it with a softmax at temperature \(\tau\):

$$y_k = \frac{\exp\big((\log\pi_k + g_k)/\tau\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\big((\log\pi_j + g_j)/\tau\big)}.$$

\(y\) is differentiable in \(\pi\), and approaches a one-hot sample as \(\tau \to 0\). For \(\tau > 0\) the relaxed ELB is a biased surrogate, but its gradients are low-variance, and the bias shrinks as \(\tau\) is annealed.

The paper anneals \(\tau\) down to 1/16 and raises the KL weight to \(\beta = 6.6\), which the authors found improved codebook usage and ultimately reconstruction error. They state the Gumbel choice rather than arguing for it at length. Read against Day 243, it keeps a genuine probabilistic objective: the encoder outputs a full distribution over codes, so the KL to the uniform prior is not a constant and actually regularizes, and there is no separate codebook or commitment loss to balance.

The method: stage 2, one transformer for text and image

Each caption is encoded as up to 256 BPE tokens (vocabulary 16,384) and followed by the 1,024 image tokens. Text tokens attend causally to text; every image token attends to all text tokens and, through sparse row, column or convolutional attention masks, to earlier image tokens. The cross-entropy losses are normalized per modality and weighted 1/8 for text and 7/8 for image, since the image is what we want to model well. Training a 12B-parameter model in 2020 also needed engineering the paper documents in detail: mixed-precision training with per-block gradient scaling to avoid underflow, parameter sharding across GPUs, and PowerSGD gradient compression to cut communication.

Reranking. At test time the authors draw many samples per caption (512 in the main experiments) and rerank them with a pretrained contrastive image–text model, CLIP (⟶ Day 271), keeping the best. Their figure on the effect of reranking shows sample quality improving steadily with the number of candidates. When looking at the paper's showcase images, state it plainly: these are the best of hundreds of draws, selected by a second model.

Evidence

On MS-COCO, zero-shot, the authors report FID and IS competitive with the best domain-specific GAN trained on MS-COCO, and a best-of-five human vote against DF-GAN in which their samples were usually chosen as more realistic and as better matching the caption. On CUB birds, a narrow, specialized domain, the zero-shot model does much worse, which the authors attribute to the domain gap. The qualitative results are what made the paper: combining unrelated concepts, rendering text, and rudimentary zero-shot image-to-image translation when prompted with the top half of an image and an instruction.

Limits & what broke
  • Reranking did substantial work. Quality of a single unreranked sample is much lower than the showcase suggests.
  • Attribute binding. With several objects, attributes attach to the wrong one ("a red cube and a blue sphere"), and errors vary from resample to resample. This compositional weakness persists in later diffusion models (Day 266).
  • Low resolution and blur. 256×256 output, with fine detail limited by the dVAE.
  • Slow and expensive. 1,024 sequential transformer steps per image at 12B parameters, times 512 candidates. Within about a year, diffusion-based systems (GLIDE, then DALL·E 2) gave better images for less sampling compute.

Bridge

The dVAE tokenizer had a second life: BEiT used it as the prediction target for masked image modelling (⟵ Day 119). Tomorrow (Day 266), two 2022 diffusion systems disagree about what the text encoder should be: CLIP's image-aligned embedding, or a frozen language model.

Hierarchical Text-Conditional Image Generation (unCLIP/DALL·E 2) + Imagen

45m videoLevel 3

Ramesh et al. 2022 (arXiv:2204.06125); Saharia et al. NeurIPS 2022 (arXiv:2205.11487)

Two contemporaneous diffusion systems disagree about how text should reach the image. unCLIP first generates a CLIP image embedding from the caption, then decodes that embedding into pixels. Imagen conditions a cascade of pixel-space diffusion models directly on a frozen T5-XXL language model, and finds that scaling the text encoder helps more than scaling the image model.

Cold open

Give unCLIP a photograph and ask for variations: out come images with the same objects and much of the same style, but a different arrangement and different details. Imagen's abstract reports a zero-shot COCO FID of 7.27 without ever training on COCO, and human raters preferring it over other models on a new benchmark of hard prompts. Both arrived within weeks of each other in spring 2022.

The problem

GLIDE (Nichol et al. 2022) had shown that a text-conditioned pixel diffusion model with classifier-free guidance (Day 263) could beat DALL·E (Day 265). The open question was the interface. Should text be represented by an embedding trained to align with images (CLIP, ⟶ Day 271), or by a large language model that has only ever read text?

The method: unCLIP

unCLIP writes the generative model as \(P(x\mid y) = P(x\mid z_i, y)\,P(z_i\mid y)\), where \(z_i\) is the CLIP image embedding of \(x\). This is exact, not an approximation, because \(z_i\) is a deterministic function of \(x\): \(P(x\mid y) = P(x, z_i\mid y)\). Two models are learned.

  • The prior \(P(z_i\mid y)\) maps a caption (and its CLIP text embedding) to a CLIP image embedding. The authors compare an autoregressive prior, which predicts a reduced, quantized version of \(z_i\) token by token, with a diffusion prior: a decoder-only transformer that denoises \(z_i\) and is trained to predict the clean embedding directly with an MSE loss. They report the diffusion prior was more compute-efficient and gave higher-quality samples.
  • The decoder \(P(x\mid z_i, y)\) is a GLIDE-style diffusion model conditioned on \(z_i\) (projected into its timestep embedding and into extra context tokens). For classifier-free guidance, the CLIP embedding is dropped 10% of the time and the caption 50% of the time during training. Two diffusion upsamplers take 64×64 to 256×256 and then 1024×1024.

What the embedding buys. Because the decoder reconstructs from \(z_i\), it is invariant to whatever CLIP does not encode. Encode an image to \(z_i\) and decode it with different noise and you get variations: semantics and much of the style preserved, non-essential details (exact layout, minor objects) changed. Spherical interpolation between two images' embeddings gives smooth semantic blends, and moving \(z_i\) along the difference between two caption embeddings ("text diffs") edits an image toward a new description.

The method: Imagen

Imagen's text encoder is T5-XXL (⟵ Day 142), about 4.6 billion parameters, pretrained only on text and kept frozen. Its embeddings condition a 64×64 base diffusion model and two text-conditional super-resolution diffusion models (64 → 256 → 1024), through pooled embeddings and cross-attention.

Central finding · scale the text encoder

Comparing text encoders and model sizes, the authors report that increasing the size of the frozen language model improves both sample fidelity and image–text alignment much more than increasing the size of the image diffusion model. T5-XXL and CLIP's text encoder score similarly on MS-COCO metrics, but human raters prefer T5-XXL on the harder DrawBench prompts for both alignment and fidelity. A language-only encoder beating a vision-language one is genuinely surprising, and it is the paper's main claim.

Algorithm · dynamic thresholding
  1. At each sampling step, compute the model's prediction \(\hat x_0\) of the clean image.
  2. Let \(s\) be a high percentile of \(|\hat x_0|\) over pixels. If \(s > 1\), clip \(\hat x_0\) to \([-s, s]\) and divide by \(s\); otherwise leave it.
  3. Continue the sampling step from the thresholded \(\hat x_0\).

This fixes the saturation artifact of high guidance weights (Day 263): large \(w\) pushes \(\hat x_0\) far outside the training range \([-1, 1]\). Static clipping to \([-1, 1]\) keeps values legal but flattens them into saturated blocks; dynamic thresholding rescales instead, so Imagen can use large guidance weights for alignment without washing images out. The super-resolution stages use noise conditioning augmentation: the low-resolution input is corrupted with noise of a known level during training, which makes each stage robust to its predecessor's artifacts.

Cascades let each stage specialize: the base model decides content at 64×64, later stages add detail. It is the same idea as progressive growing (⟵ Day 250), with separately trained models instead of one growing network.

Evidence

unCLIP is compared with GLIDE in human evaluations: the authors report comparable photorealism, somewhat lower caption similarity, and a strong preference for unCLIP on diversity, and they illustrate how guidance reduces GLIDE's diversity more than unCLIP's. Imagen reports the COCO FID above and introduces DrawBench, about 200 prompts in 11 categories (counting, spatial relations, rare words, rendering text, unusual compositions), on which raters preferred Imagen over DALL·E 2, GLIDE, latent diffusion and VQGAN+CLIP in side-by-side comparisons. Ablations support each component: text-encoder scaling, dynamic thresholding at high guidance, noise conditioning augmentation, and an efficient U-Net for the upsamplers.

Limits & what broke
  • unCLIP binds attributes poorly. The authors show it confusing which object gets which colour more often than GLIDE, plausibly because a single CLIP embedding does not represent binding precisely. It also renders text badly.
  • Human preference is fragile evidence. DrawBench is small and was designed by the Imagen authors; rater instructions, prompt choice and cherry-picking all move the outcome. COCO FID, meanwhile, barely measures compositional correctness.
  • Not released. Imagen's authors cited risks from web-scraped training data and social biases and did not release the model, so its claims were hard to verify independently.
  • Pixel cascades are expensive. Three diffusion models at increasing resolution; latent diffusion (Day 264) reached the public instead.

Bridge

By mid-2022 text-to-image worked; what it lacked was control. A caption cannot specify the exact pose of a person or the outline of a building. Tomorrow (Day 267) adds spatial conditions to a frozen Stable Diffusion without retraining it.

Adding Conditional Control to Text-to-Image Diffusion Models (ControlNet)

35m videoLevel 2

Zhang, Rao & Agrawala, ICCV 2023 (arXiv:2302.05543)

To give a large pretrained text-to-image model spatial control (edges, depth, pose, segmentation, scribbles), freeze it, train a copy of its encoder on the new condition, and connect the copy back through 1×1 convolutions initialized to zero. At the first training step the combined model is exactly the original, so learning the condition cannot damage what the original already knows.

Cold open

A rough scribble of a house becomes a photograph of that house. A stick-figure pose skeleton dictates exactly how a generated person stands. Edges traced from a photo are re-rendered as a painting with the same composition. All from a frozen Stable Diffusion (Day 264), with one extra network per kind of condition.

The problem

A caption cannot say where things go. Spatially precise control needs an image-shaped condition, but the datasets for any one condition (pose–image pairs, say) are tiny compared with the billions of images behind the base model. Fine-tuning the whole model on them risks overfitting and catastrophic forgetting; training a conditional model from scratch is out of the question. The base model's prior has to be preserved while something new is learned beside it.

The method: lock, copy, connect through zeros

For a block \(\mathcal F(\cdot\,; \Theta)\) mapping features \(x\) to \(y\), ControlNet locks \(\Theta\), clones it into trainable \(\Theta_c\), and computes

$$y_c = \mathcal F(x; \Theta) + \mathcal Z\Big(\mathcal F\big(x + \mathcal Z(c; \Theta_{z1});\ \Theta_c\big);\ \Theta_{z2}\Big),$$

where \(\mathcal Z\) is a 1×1 convolution with weights and bias initialized to zero. The condition image (for example 512×512) is first mapped to the 64×64 latent resolution by a small convolutional encoder. The copy starts as a duplicate of a network that already understands images, which is why so little data suffices.

Derivation · why zeros do not block learning

Write a zero convolution at one position as \(\mathcal Z(I) = W I + B\) with \(W = 0\), \(B = 0\). At initialization its output is 0, so both added terms vanish and \(y_c = y\): the first forward pass is exactly the unmodified model, and no random noise is injected into the deep features of a good checkpoint. Gradients still exist: \(\partial\mathcal Z/\partial W = I\), which is nonzero whenever the incoming features are nonzero, and \(\partial\mathcal Z/\partial B = 1\). After the first update \(W \ne 0\), and gradient begins to flow back into the trainable copy. A zero-initialized layer is silent, not dead.

The cross-month connection. This is the same trick as LoRA's \(B = 0\) initialization (⟵ Day 204) and the near-identity initialization of adapters (⟵ Day 201): add a new pathway that starts as a no-op, so fine-tuning begins at the pretrained model and moves away from it only as far as the data requires. DiT's adaLN-Zero (Day 270) is a fourth instance.

Training details. The loss is the usual noise-prediction loss with both the text prompt and the condition as inputs. Half the time the text prompt is replaced by an empty string, which forces the ControlNet to read semantics from the condition image itself.

Evidence

The authors train ControlNets for Canny edges, straight-line (Hough/M-LSD) maps, soft HED edges, user scribbles, human pose keypoints, ADE20K-style segmentation maps, depth, surface normals and cartoon line drawings. The abstract states that training is robust with both small (under 50k) and large (over 1 million) datasets: a new condition does not need billions of examples, which is the practical headline. They report a sudden convergence phenomenon: rather than improving gradually, the model abruptly starts following the condition, usually in fewer than 10,000 optimization steps. Ablations replace the zero convolutions with ordinary randomly initialized ones, or shrink the copy to a light network, and both degrade results; the authors attribute the first to the copy's pretrained features being damaged in early training. User studies and segmentation-conditioned metrics favour ControlNet over the baselines they test. Multiple ControlNets compose by simply adding their outputs, for example pose plus depth.

Limits & what broke
  • Extra compute at inference. A second copy of the encoder runs at every denoising step.
  • One network per condition type. Composition by addition works but can conflict when two conditions disagree.
  • Only as good as the extractor. Pose, depth and edge maps come from separate models; their errors become constraints.
  • Sudden convergence is observed, not explained. The paper offers no theory for why learning switches on abruptly.
  • Lighter alternatives. T2I-Adapter (Mou et al. 2023) trains a much smaller adapter that injects condition features into the frozen U-Net, trading some fidelity for cost.

Bridge

This is the paper that turned diffusion models from slot machines into controllable tools. Speed remains: dozens of denoising steps per image. Tomorrow (Day 268) asks whether one step can be enough.

Consistency Models

40m videoLevel 4

Song, Dhariwal, Chen & Sutskever, ICML 2023 (arXiv:2303.01469)

Every point on a probability-flow ODE trajectory belongs to exactly one clean image, the trajectory's origin. Learn a function that jumps from any point straight to that origin, build the boundary condition into its parameterization, and train it either by distilling a diffusion model or from scratch. Sampling then takes one network evaluation, or a few if you want more quality.

Prerequisite

The probability-flow ODE of Day 262 is a hard prerequisite: this whole paper is about its trajectories. Without it, the method looks like an arbitrary loss.

Cold open

The abstract reports one-step FIDs of 3.55 on CIFAR-10 and 6.20 on ImageNet 64×64 by distillation, a new state of the art for single-step generation at the time, with no adversarial training. The same model also does inpainting, colourization and super-resolution zero-shot.

The problem

Diffusion samples well but slowly: tens to thousands of sequential network calls (Day 260, Day 261). GANs sample in one step but bring adversarial training back. Progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho 2022) halves a sampler's steps per distillation round, which needs many rounds to get to one step. Consistency models aim for one step directly, from a single training procedure.

The method: the self-consistency property

The paper uses the EDM formulation (Karras et al. 2022): noise level equals time, \(\sigma(t) = t\), and the probability-flow ODE is \(dx_t/dt = -t\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x_t)\) for \(t \in [\epsilon, T]\), with \(\epsilon\) a small positive number and \(x_T\) approximately \(\mathcal N(0, T^2 I)\). Each trajectory runs from a nearly clean point \(x_\epsilon\) to noise.

Definition · consistency function

\(f: (x_t, t) \mapsto x_\epsilon\) maps every point of a trajectory to its origin. Equivalently, it satisfies self-consistency, \(f(x_t, t) = f(x_{t'}, t')\) for all \(t, t'\) on the same trajectory, and the boundary condition \(f(x_\epsilon, \epsilon) = x_\epsilon\).

The boundary condition must be built in, not learned. Self-consistency alone has a trivial solution: any constant function satisfies \(f(x_t, t) = f(x_{t'}, t')\). It is the boundary condition that ties \(f\) to the data. If it were only encouraged by a loss term, training could drift toward the collapse. So the paper enforces it exactly through the parameterization:

$$f_\theta(x, t) = c_{\text{skip}}(t)\,x + c_{\text{out}}(t)\,F_\theta(x, t),\qquad c_{\text{skip}}(\epsilon) = 1,\ c_{\text{out}}(\epsilon) = 0.$$

They use \(c_{\text{skip}}(t) = \sigma_{\text{data}}^2/\big((t-\epsilon)^2 + \sigma_{\text{data}}^2\big)\) and \(c_{\text{out}}(t) = \sigma_{\text{data}}(t - \epsilon)/\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{data}}^2 + t^2}\), with \(\sigma_{\text{data}} = 0.5\), adapted from EDM's preconditioning. At \(t = \epsilon\) the network's output is multiplied by zero, so \(f_\theta(x, \epsilon) = x\) for every \(\theta\). This is the design subtlety of the paper.

The method: two ways to train

Algorithm · consistency distillation (CD)
  1. Discretize \([\epsilon, T]\) into \(t_1 = \epsilon < t_2 < \dots < t_N = T\). Sample data \(x\), an index \(n\), and \(z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\); set \(x_{t_{n+1}} = x + t_{n+1}z\).
  2. Take one ODE-solver step (for example Heun) from \(x_{t_{n+1}}\) to \(t_n\) using a pretrained diffusion model's score: call the result \(\hat x_{t_n}\). The two points are now (approximately) on the same trajectory.
  3. Minimize \(\lambda(t_n)\,d\big(f_\theta(x_{t_{n+1}}, t_{n+1}),\ f_{\theta^-}(\hat x_{t_n}, t_n)\big)\), with \(d\) a distance (L2 or LPIPS) and \(\theta^-\) an exponential moving average of \(\theta\) with no gradient: a target network, as in DQN.

Consistency training (CT) removes the teacher. The score is the only thing the teacher supplied, and it has an unbiased one-sample estimate: \(\nabla\log p_t(x_t) = -\E\big[(x_t - x)/t^2 \mid x_t\big]\). Using it in the Euler step makes the adjacent point simply \(x + t_n z\) with the same \(z\), so the loss compares \(f_\theta(x + t_{n+1}z, t_{n+1})\) with \(f_{\theta^-}(x + t_n z, t_n)\). The number of discretization steps \(N\) is increased during training. Distillation and from-scratch training are genuinely different regimes: CD inherits a teacher's trajectories and its quality ceiling; CT must discover the trajectories itself, and in this paper it ends up behind CD.

Sampling. One step: \(x = f_\theta(x_T, T)\) with \(x_T \sim \mathcal N(0, T^2 I)\). Multistep: after a one-step estimate, re-noise it to an intermediate time \(\tau\), \(x_\tau = x + \sqrt{\tau^2 - \epsilon^2}\,z\), and apply \(f_\theta(x_\tau, \tau)\) again; repeating over a few decreasing times trades compute for quality. Because \(f_\theta\) maps noisy inputs to clean ones, the same loop with known pixels re-imposed after each step performs inpainting, colourization or super-resolution without retraining.

Evidence

On CIFAR-10, ImageNet 64×64 and LSUN 256×256 (bedrooms, cats), the authors report that CD outperforms progressive distillation at one and two steps, and that CT outperforms existing single-step, non-adversarial generative models. Two steps improve noticeably over one. Neither matches the full multi-step diffusion samplers they distil from: one step still loses to tens of steps of the teacher, and the paper frames multistep consistency sampling as the quality dial between the two.

Limits & what broke
  • One step is not free. One-step samples trail the teacher's many-step samples; the gap shrinks with two to four steps.
  • A metric in the loss. The strongest results used LPIPS, whose ImageNet-trained features overlap with those FID measures. Improved consistency training (Song & Dhariwal 2023) replaced it with a robust pseudo-Huber loss, refined the noise and step schedules, and largely closed the CT–CD gap.
  • Discretization tradeoff. Coarse discretization gives a biased target; fine discretization gives a weak training signal. The \(N\) schedule is a sensitive hyperparameter.
  • Practical descendants. Latent Consistency Models (Luo et al. 2023) distil Stable Diffusion in its latent space for few-step text-to-image generation, and LCM-LoRA packages that acceleration as a LoRA that plugs into fine-tuned checkpoints.

Bridge

This is the paper that made real-time diffusion plausible. It accepts the ODE's curved trajectories and learns to jump across them. Tomorrow (Day 269) takes the opposite route: choose straight paths from the start, so even a plain ODE solver needs few steps. Prerequisite: ⟵ Day 262.

Flow Matching and Rectified Flow

45m videoLevel 4

Lipman et al. ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2210.02747); Liu, Gong & Liu ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2209.03003)

Train a continuous normalizing flow without ever simulating it: regress a network onto the velocity of a simple, known path from noise to each individual data point. A theorem guarantees that this gives the same gradients as regressing onto the intractable velocity field of the whole distribution. With straight-line paths the loss is almost embarrassingly simple, and straighter flows need fewer sampling steps.

Cold open

Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al. 2024) is trained with a rectified-flow objective, and FLUX.1 is reported to use the same formulation; many recent image and video generators have followed. The training loop is shorter than DDPM's: interpolate linearly between noise and an image, and regress onto the difference.

The problem

A continuous normalizing flow (CNF) moves samples with an ODE \(dx/dt = v_t(x)\), the continuous-time version of the flows of Day 258. Training one by maximum likelihood means solving the ODE and estimating divergences during every training step, which is slow and scales badly. Diffusion models train without simulation, but only along the specific, curved paths that come from a noising SDE (Day 262). The question: can you choose any path from noise to data and still train simulation-free?

The method: from marginal to conditional

Notation follows Lipman et al.: \(t = 0\) is noise \(p_0 = \mathcal N(0, I)\), \(t = 1\) is data \(q(x_1)\). Flow matching would regress \(v_\theta\) onto a vector field \(u_t\) that generates a chosen probability path \(p_t\) from \(p_0\) to \(q\):

$$\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \E_{t,\ x\sim p_t}\big\|v_\theta(t, x) - u_t(x)\big\|^2.$$

But neither \(p_t\) nor \(u_t\) is known in closed form. The trick is to build them from per-example pieces. For each data point \(x_1\), pick a conditional path \(p_t(x\mid x_1)\) from \(\mathcal N(0, I)\) at \(t = 0\) to a tight Gaussian around \(x_1\) at \(t = 1\), generated by a known conditional field \(u_t(x\mid x_1)\). The marginal path and field are mixtures,

$$p_t(x) = \int p_t(x\mid x_1)\,q(x_1)\,dx_1,\qquad u_t(x) = \int u_t(x\mid x_1)\,\frac{p_t(x\mid x_1)\,q(x_1)}{p_t(x)}\,dx_1,$$

and the paper's first theorem shows this \(u_t\) does generate \(p_t\): the marginal field is the posterior average of the conditional fields.

Theorem · conditional flow matching (Lipman et al., Theorem 2)

Let \(\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \E_{t,\ x_1\sim q,\ x\sim p_t(\cdot\mid x_1)}\big\|v_\theta(t, x) - u_t(x\mid x_1)\big\|^2\). If \(p_t(x) > 0\) for all \(x\) and \(t\), then \(\mathcal L_{\text{FM}}\) and \(\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}\) differ by a constant independent of \(\theta\), so \(\nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{FM}} = \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{CFM}}\).

Proof · expand both squares

Write \(\|v - u\|^2 = \|v\|^2 - 2\langle v, u\rangle + \|u\|^2\). The \(\|u\|^2\) terms do not involve \(\theta\). The \(\|v_\theta\|^2\) terms agree, because sampling \(x_1 \sim q\) then \(x \sim p_t(\cdot\mid x_1)\) is the same as sampling \(x \sim p_t\). For the cross term, substitute the formula for \(u_t\):

$$\E_{p_t(x)}\langle v_\theta(t,x), u_t(x)\rangle = \int\!\!\int \big\langle v_\theta(t,x), u_t(x\mid x_1)\big\rangle\,p_t(x\mid x_1)\,q(x_1)\,dx_1\,dx = \E_{q(x_1),\,p_t(x\mid x_1)}\big\langle v_\theta(t,x), u_t(x\mid x_1)\big\rangle.$$

The \(p_t(x)\) in the denominator of \(u_t\) cancels the density being averaged over. All \(\theta\)-dependent terms match. This is the whole paper, and it fits on one slide: regressing the conditional field yields the correct marginal field, the same way denoising score matching yields the marginal score.

The method: choosing the path

For Gaussian conditional paths \(p_t(x\mid x_1) = \mathcal N(\mu_t(x_1), \sigma_t(x_1)^2 I)\), the flow \(\psi_t(x_0) = \sigma_t x_0 + \mu_t\) generates them, and the conditional field is \(u_t(x\mid x_1) = \frac{\sigma_t'}{\sigma_t}(x - \mu_t) + \mu_t'\). Two choices matter.

  • Diffusion paths. Taking \(\mu_t, \sigma_t\) from the variance-preserving SDE recovers the probability-flow ODE of diffusion as a special case. Its conditional paths are not straight lines travelled at constant speed, so solvers need more steps.
  • Optimal-transport (straight) paths. \(\mu_t = t\,x_1\), \(\sigma_t = 1 - (1 - \sigma_{\min})t\). Then \(\psi_t(x_0) = (1 - (1-\sigma_{\min})t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1\) moves in a straight line at constant speed, and the regression target is \(u = x_1 - (1 - \sigma_{\min})x_0\). With \(\sigma_{\min} \to 0\): \(x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t\,x_1\), target \(x_1 - x_0\).
import torch

def flow_matching_loss(v_model, x1):
    """Conditional flow matching with straight paths (sigma_min = 0)."""
    x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)                          # noise at t = 0
    t = torch.rand(x1.shape[0], *[1] * (x1.dim() - 1))
    xt = (1 - t) * x0 + t * x1                         # a point on the straight path
    return ((v_model(xt, t.flatten()) - (x1 - x0)) ** 2).mean()

@torch.no_grad()
def sample(v_model, shape, steps=10):
    x = torch.randn(shape)
    for i in range(steps):                             # Euler from t = 0 to t = 1
        x = x + v_model(x, torch.full((shape[0],), i / steps)) / steps
    return x

Why straightness buys speed. On a path with constant velocity, a single Euler step is exact. Solver error comes from curvature, so the straighter the trajectories, the fewer steps you need. This reframes the whole fast-sampling literature (Day 261, Day 268) as a fight against curvature. But note the subtlety in the figure: the conditional paths are straight, while the marginal flow that the network learns is an average of them, and it bends wherever conditional paths cross.

The method: rectified flow and reflow

Liu, Gong and Liu arrive at the same objective from a transport viewpoint: given any coupling of \(X_0 \sim \pi_0\) and \(X_1 \sim \pi_1\), learn \(v\) by minimizing \(\int_0^1 \E\|(X_1 - X_0) - v(X_t, t)\|^2\,dt\) with \(X_t = tX_1 + (1-t)X_0\). They prove that the resulting flow, the rectified flow, has non-crossing paths, preserves the marginals, and does not increase any convex transport cost relative to the original coupling. Reflow then repeats the procedure on the coupling the flow itself produces: draw \(Z_0\), solve the ODE to get \(Z_1\), and retrain on the pairs \((Z_0, Z_1)\). Each round straightens the paths, because pairs generated by a non-crossing flow cross less. After one or two rounds, a few Euler steps, or a distilled single step, suffice.

Evidence

Lipman et al. train the same U-Net on CIFAR-10 and downsampled ImageNet (32, 64, 128) with score matching, with flow matching on diffusion paths, and with flow matching on OT paths. They report that OT-path flow matching gives the best likelihoods and sample quality and needs fewer function evaluations with an adaptive ODE solver, and that flow matching with diffusion paths is itself more stable than score matching. Liu et al. show that reflow straightens trajectories measurably and that 1-rectified and 2-rectified flows give good CIFAR-10 samples with very few steps, especially after distillation, plus applications to image-to-image translation where both endpoints are data distributions.

Limits & what broke
  • "Optimal transport" is conditional. Each conditional path is an OT path, but independent random pairing of noise and data is not the OT coupling, so the marginal flow is not the OT map. Minibatch OT couplings (Tong et al. 2023; Pooladian et al. 2023) pair samples more cleverly to straighten it.
  • Reflow is not free. Generating pairs costs a full sampling pass per example, and errors in the learned flow are baked into the next round's data.
  • Timestep weighting matters. Uniform \(t\) is not optimal; SD3's large study found that sampling \(t\) from a logit-normal distribution, concentrating training on intermediate times, worked better.
  • Equivalences. With Gaussian paths, flow matching, diffusion with ε-prediction and score matching are reparameterizations of one another with different time weightings, so some reported gains are weighting choices in disguise.

Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers (DiT)

40m videoLevel 3

Peebles & Xie, ICCV 2023 (arXiv:2212.09748)

Replace latent diffusion's U-Net with a plain Vision Transformer over patches of the latent, condition it through adaptive layer norm with zero-initialized gates (adaLN-Zero), and sample quality tracks the network's forward-pass compute almost exactly. Diffusion inherits transformer scaling behaviour, and the U-Net's inductive bias turns out to be unnecessary.

Cold open

One plot: FID against Gflops for twelve models, four sizes times three patch sizes, falling along a single clean trend. The abstract reports that the largest, DiT-XL/2, reaches an FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256×256, better than all prior diffusion models on that benchmark.

The problem

Every diffusion model this month used a convolutional U-Net (Day 260, Day 263, Day 264). Meanwhile transformers had taken over vision (ViT, ⟵ Day 115) and come with predictable scaling laws in language (⟵ Day 146). Was the U-Net's inductive bias essential to diffusion, or just the architecture that happened to be there first?

The method: a ViT on latent patches

DiT works in the latent space of Stable Diffusion's pretrained autoencoder (⟵ Day 264): a 256×256×3 image becomes a 32×32×4 latent. Patchify splits the latent into \(p\times p\) patches, linearly embeds each into a token, and adds fixed sine–cosine position embeddings. With input size \(I = 32\) the sequence length is \(T = (I/p)^2\):

patch size ptokens (I/p)²effect
816cheapest, coarse
4644× the tokens of p = 8
22564× again; at least 4× the Gflops of p = 4

Halving \(p\) quadruples the tokens and therefore at least quadruples the compute, while changing the parameter count hardly at all: only the patch-embedding and output layers depend on \(p\). That makes patch size the cleanest experiment in the paper for separating compute from parameters. A stack of transformer blocks follows, then a final layer that decodes each token back into a \(p\times p\) patch of predicted noise and predicted (diagonal) covariance.

The method: four ways to condition

The network must know the timestep \(t\) and class label \(c\). The authors compare four block designs.

  • In-context conditioning: append embeddings of \(t\) and \(c\) as two extra tokens, like ViT's class token. Almost free.
  • Cross-attention: add a cross-attention layer in every block that attends to the \(t, c\) embeddings, as in latent diffusion. The authors report it adds roughly 15% to the Gflops.
  • Adaptive layer norm (adaLN): replace each LayerNorm's learned scale and shift with values \(\gamma, \beta\) regressed from the sum of the \(t\) and \(c\) embeddings: conditioning by modulation, like AdaIN in StyleGAN (Day 251) and AdaGN in ADM (Day 263). The most compute-efficient option.
  • adaLN-Zero: adaLN plus a regressed per-channel gate \(\alpha\) on each residual branch, initialized to zero (defined below).
Definition · the adaLN-Zero block

With \(e = \mathrm{emb}(t) + \mathrm{emb}(c)\) and \((\gamma_1, \beta_1, \alpha_1, \gamma_2, \beta_2, \alpha_2) = \mathrm{MLP}(e)\), a block updates the tokens \(h\) by

$$h \leftarrow h + \alpha_1 \odot \mathrm{Attn}\big((1 + \gamma_1)\odot\mathrm{LN}(h) + \beta_1\big),\qquad h \leftarrow h + \alpha_2 \odot \mathrm{FFN}\big((1 + \gamma_2)\odot\mathrm{LN}(h) + \beta_2\big).$$

The MLP's final layer is initialized to zero, so every \(\alpha\) (and \(\gamma, \beta\)) starts at zero and the block is exactly the identity. The paper describes \(\gamma, \beta\) as scale and shift; writing the scale as \(1 + \gamma\), as implementations do, makes a zero output mean "no modulation". The final output layer is zero-initialized as well.

adaLN-Zero is the zero-init idea again. ControlNet's zero convolutions (⟵ Day 267), LoRA's \(B = 0\) (⟵ Day 204) and adapters' near-identity initialization all start a new pathway as a no-op. The DiT authors cite the older precedent of initializing the final batch-norm scale of each residual block to zero for large-batch ResNet training (Goyal et al. 2017). Here the benefit is optimization: a deep stack that starts as the identity is easy to train.

Evidence

The four-way ablation (on DiT-XL/2) is worth reading in full: adaLN-Zero gives the best FID throughout training, clearly ahead of plain adaLN, cross-attention and in-context conditioning, while also being the cheapest of the strong options. The only difference between adaLN and adaLN-Zero is the initialization, so the gap measures the zero-init effect directly.

Scaling. The authors train models S, B, L and XL (the largest about 675M parameters) at patch sizes 8, 4 and 2. FID improves both with model size and with smaller patches, and across all twelve models FID-50K is strongly correlated with forward-pass Gflops. Models with similar Gflops but different shapes, a small model with small patches against a larger model with larger patches, reach similar FID. Because patch size changes compute while barely changing parameters, the conclusion is specific: FID tracks Gflops almost independently of parameter count. That is the paper's real contribution. Two corollaries: larger models use training compute more efficiently, and spending extra compute on more sampling steps does not let a small model catch up with a larger one. Patch size 2 dominates, and it is the most expensive knob.

Benchmarks. With classifier-free guidance (Day 263), DiT-XL/2 sets the ImageNet 256×256 result above and also beats prior diffusion models at 512×512; the authors note it is more compute-efficient than pixel-space U-Nets such as ADM.

Limits & what broke
  • Class labels, not text. All results are class-conditional ImageNet. Text conditioning at scale needed further design: SD3's MM-DiT runs joint attention over text and image tokens and trains with rectified flow (Day 269).
  • Quadratic in tokens. Patch size 2 on a 32×32 latent is 256 tokens; higher resolutions and video multiply the sequence length, and self-attention cost grows with its square.
  • "The U-Net is unnecessary" needs a qualifier. It is unnecessary given enough compute. This is the ViT-versus-CNN story replayed (⟵ Day 115): inductive bias matters most at small scale.
  • A narrow scaling study. One dataset, one resolution, FID as the only target. It shows a clean trend, not a law with fitted exponents like those of Day 146.

Bridge

DiT is the architecture behind modern video generation: OpenAI described Sora as a diffusion transformer operating on spacetime patches (⟶ Day 298). It also closes the month. Three answers to "how do you learn a distribution you can sample from?" ended up combined: a VAE-style autoencoder for compression, a diffusion or flow objective for generation, and a transformer for the network, with adversarial losses surviving inside the autoencoder. Next month starts with the model whose embeddings condition much of this: CLIP (Day 271).
