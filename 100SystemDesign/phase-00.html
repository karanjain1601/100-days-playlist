D001

What System Design Actually Is

18 min · Phase 0 · Prereq: none · Homework: 20 min

Hook — watch it break A photo-sharing app runs happily on one 4-core box with Postgres and local disk. It has 8,000 users. A creator with 2 million followers posts a link. Within ninety seconds: the connection pool is exhausted, the app returns 502s, the disk fills with un-resized uploads, and the database is CPU-bound on a query that was always slow but never mattered. Nothing was wrong with the system. It was correct at 8,000 users and incorrect at 800,000, and no amount of clean code would have saved it. That gap is the entire subject.

What you'll be able to do

Name the four things people mean when they say “design”, run the seven-step process on a blank page without prompting, and explain to an interviewer why a design question has no answer key.

Choosing Postgres over MySQL is usually not architecture. Choosing “every service owns its own data and nobody shares a database” is.

Deep dive — why there is never one right answer

A system design is a point chosen inside a constraint space. Change any constraint and the optimum moves. Concretely, most designs are dominated by three ratios:

The three ratios that pick your architecture
\[ r_{\text{rw}}=\frac{\text{reads}}{\text{writes}},\qquad r_{\text{fan}}=\frac{\text{deliveries}}{\text{write}},\qquad r_{\text{hot}}=\frac{\text{traffic to top 1\% of keys}}{\text{total traffic}} \]
  • \(r_{\text{rw}} \approx 100\) (URL shortener) → cache aggressively, replicate reads, accept slow writes.
  • \(r_{\text{rw}} \approx 1\) (chat) → the write path is the product; caching buys you little.
  • \(r_{\text{fan}} \approx 10^{8}\) (a celebrity post) → fan-out-on-write is bankrupt; you need a hybrid.
  • \(r_{\text{hot}} \to 1\) (a ticket on sale) → sharding does not help, because every shard but one is idle.

An interviewer who says “design Twitter” has not given you these numbers. That is the test. The first three minutes exist so you can obtain them.

Interview angle Common wrong answers on Day-1 material:
  • “There's no right answer, so anything goes.” There are plenty of wrong answers — a design that cannot meet the stated durability requirement is wrong.
  • Treating the requirements phase as a formality and asking zero questions.
  • Naming technologies (“I'd use Kafka and Cassandra”) before naming access patterns.
What scores instead: state an assumption out loud, mark it as an assumption, and say what you would change if it were false.
Homework Write down three systems you use daily. For each, guess (a) what breaks first at 100× today's traffic, and (b) which of the three ratios above dominates it. Keep the page — you will re-answer it on Day 145 and the difference is the course.
D002

The Vocabulary of Scale

24 min · Phase 0 · Prereq: D001 · Homework: 30 min

Hook — watch it break A team celebrates: average API latency is 84 ms, well under the 200 ms target. Support tickets say the app is unusable. Both are true. The average hides that 3% of requests take 4 seconds, and the users filing tickets are the ones with the most data — the same users who hit the slow path every time. An average is a summary statistic that deletes exactly the information you needed.

Latency, response time, service time — three different numbers

These get used interchangeably and they are not the same. For a single request:

Decomposition of what the user feels
\[ T_{\text{response}} \;=\; T_{\text{network}} \;+\; T_{\text{queue}} \;+\; T_{\text{service}} \]
  • Service time — how long the work actually takes once a worker picks it up. This is what profilers show you.
  • Queueing delay — how long the request waited for a free worker. Invisible in application traces unless you instrument for it. Usually the cause of a p99 blow-up.
  • Response time — what the client measures, including network and serialisation.
  • Latency — loosely, the whole thing; precisely, the delay component (network + queue). If you must use one word, say “response time” and define it.

The important consequence: you can make service time faster and response time get worse, if the extra throughput drives utilisation higher and queueing dominates.

Concurrency is not parallelism

Concurrency is a structure property: how many things are in progress. Parallelism is an execution property: how many things run at the same instant. A single-core machine can hold 10,000 concurrent connections and have parallelism of 1. The relationship is Little's Law, which is the single most useful formula in this course:

Little's Law — holds for any stable system, no assumptions
\[ L \;=\; \lambda W \]

\(L\) = average number of requests in the system (concurrency), \(\lambda\) = arrival rate (throughput), \(W\) = average time in system (response time).

Rearranged, it sizes thread pools, connection pools, and queue depths. If your service handles \(\lambda = 2{,}000\) req/s and each takes \(W = 50\) ms, then \(L = 2000 \times 0.05 = 100\) requests are in flight on average. You need at least 100 units of concurrency. Provision 100 threads and you are at 100% utilisation with zero headroom — which brings us to the danger line.

Deep dive — utilisation and why 80% is the cliff

Model a single server as an M/M/1 queue: Poisson arrivals at rate \(\lambda\), exponential service at rate \(\mu\), utilisation \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\). The expected time in system is:

M/M/1 response time
\[ W \;=\; \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} \;=\; \frac{1/\mu}{1-\rho} \;=\; \frac{T_{\text{service}}}{1-\rho} \]

That \(\frac{1}{1-\rho}\) term is the whole story. It is a hyperbola with a vertical asymptote at \(\rho = 1\). Read the multiplier off it:

Utilisation ρLatency multiplier 1/(1−ρ)Queue length ρ/(1−ρ)Feels like

Going from 80% to 90% utilisation buys you 12.5% more throughput and costs you 2× the latency. That is why capacity planning targets 60–70% and why a CPU dashboard reading “80% — fine” is a dashboard lying to you.

Deep dive — percentiles, and why averages lie

Latency distributions are right-skewed and heavy-tailed: a long tail of slow requests caused by GC pauses, cache misses, lock contention, retries, and noisy neighbours. Mean and median can differ by an order of magnitude.

  • p50 (median) — the typical experience. Optimising it is marketing.
  • p90 / p95 — the edge of normal. Where regressions first show.
  • p99 — the users with the most data, most followers, most items in cart. Usually your best customers.
  • p99.9 — where systemic faults hide. At 10,000 QPS this is still 10 requests per second.

You cannot average percentiles. The mean of two servers' p99 values is not the fleet p99. To aggregate, you must merge the underlying histograms (or use a mergeable sketch such as t-digest / DDSketch). Averaging p99s across shards is one of the most common metrics bugs in production, and it always makes things look better than they are.

Deep dive — tail latency amplification

This is the reason p99 dominates in microservice architectures. If a single request fans out to \(n\) independent backend calls and must wait for all of them, then the probability that the request is slow is the probability that at least one backend is slow:

Fan-out tail amplification
\[ P(\text{request slow}) \;=\; 1-(1-p)^{n} \qquad\text{where } p = P(\text{one call slow}) \] \[ \text{With } p = 0.01,\; n = 100:\quad 1-(0.99)^{100} \approx 0.634 \]

A backend whose p99 is 1 second — meaning it is “only slow 1% of the time” — makes 63% of requests slow once you fan out to 100 services. Your service's p50 is now your dependency's p99. This single equation justifies hedged requests, tied requests, request cancellation, and a large part of Google's The Tail at Scale paper.

War story Google's The Tail at Scale (Dean & Barroso, CACM 2013) reports the shape of this problem from production: in a service where a single server has a 99th-percentile latency of 10 ms, a request that must gather results from 100 such servers ends up with a 99th-percentile latency far above 10 ms — the paper's headline example puts it at 140 ms, with 63% of requests exceeding the single-server p99. Their mitigations — hedged requests, tied requests, micro-partitioning, selective replication — are all attempts to defeat \(1-(1-p)^n\).
Interview angle Expect: “Your service p99 is 2 s. Where do you look first?” Strong answers separate the three components of response time before touching code — is it queueing (check concurrency limits and pool saturation), service time (profile), or fan-out amplification (check the trace's critical path).
  • “I'd add more servers.” Only helps if the bottleneck is utilisation, and makes fan-out worse.
  • “Our average is fine.” Never say this out loud.
  • Quoting p99 without saying over what window. p99 over 24 h and p99 over 1 min are different metrics.
Homework Pick any API you can hit. Fire 100 sequential requests, record every duration, sort them, and read off index 50, 90, 95 and 99. Then compute the mean. Write down the ratio p99/mean — that number is your first real feel for tail behaviour.

Deep dive — the estimation chain

Six steps, always the same order. Each one feeds the next.

Step 1 — average QPS from users
\[ \text{QPS}_{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{\text{DAU}\times\text{actions per user per day}}{86{,}400} \]
Step 2 — peak, which is what you must provision for
\[ \text{QPS}_{\text{peak}} \;=\; k\cdot \text{QPS}_{\text{avg}},\qquad k \in [2,5] \]

\(k\approx2\) for a global product with users spread across time zones; \(k\approx5\) for a single-country product with a strong evening peak; \(k\) can exceed 50 for event-driven traffic (ticket sales, live sport, flash sales) — those are not modelled by a multiplier at all, they are modelled as a spike.

Step 3 — storage growth
\[ S_{\text{year}} \;=\; \text{writes/day}\times \text{bytes/write}\times 365 \times R \]

\(R\) is the replication + overhead factor. Use \(R=3\) for naive replication; \(R\approx1.5\) for erasure coding; add ~30% on top for indexes, and a further margin for backups if backups live in the same budget.

Step 4 — bandwidth
\[ B_{\text{egress}} \;=\; \text{QPS}_{\text{peak}}\times \text{bytes per response} \]
Step 5 — machine count
\[ N_{\text{servers}} \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{\text{QPS}_{\text{peak}}}{\text{QPS per server}\times \rho_{\text{target}}} \right\rceil + N_{\text{spare}} \]

Use \(\rho_{\text{target}}=0.6\) from Day 2, and add spares for \(N+2\) redundancy per availability zone.

Step 6 — cache size, from the working set
\[ M_{\text{cache}} \;\approx\; 0.2 \times S_{\text{daily active}} \times (1+\text{overhead}) \]

The 80/20 rule: 20% of the data serves 80% of requests. Redis overhead for small objects is significant — budget 50–100 bytes per key on top of the value.

22 min · Phase 0 · Prereq: D003 · Homework: 15 min

Hook — watch it break An engineer moves a service from one region to another to be “closer to the database team”. The code is unchanged. p99 goes from 60 ms to 780 ms. Deep dive — the speed of light is a design constraint

Light in vacuum: \(c = 3\times10^{8}\) m/s. In optical fibre the refractive index is about 1.47, so signals travel at roughly \(2\times10^{8}\) m/s — about two-thirds of \(c\).

Minimum round-trip time over fibre
\[ \text{RTT}_{\min} \;=\; \frac{2d}{v},\qquad v \approx 2\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \] \[ \Rightarrow\quad \text{RTT}_{\min} \approx 10\ \text{ms per } 1{,}000\ \text{km} \]

Worked: Mumbai → Northern Virginia is about 13,000 km great-circle. So \(\text{RTT}_{\min} = 2\times13{,}000{,}000 / 2\times10^{8} = 0.13\) s = 130 ms. Real-world measurements land near 180–220 ms because cables do not follow great circles and every router, amplifier and switch adds queueing. There is no engineering, no protocol, no amount of money that gets you below 130 ms on that path.

What this forbids
  • Synchronous cross-continent quorum writes with a <100 ms SLO. Impossible. Not hard — impossible.
  • Chatty protocols over WAN. Nine sequential round trips at 150 ms is 1.35 s of pure waiting. Batch, pipeline, or move the computation.
  • “Just replicate synchronously to another continent for safety.” Every write now costs a full RTT. Choose async and accept an RPO, or choose a nearby region.

The corollary that matters: when latency is bounded by distance, the only remaining lever is moving the data closer to the user. That is the entire justification for CDNs (Day 26), edge compute (Day 104), and regional write partitioning (Day 66).

War story High-frequency trading firms have spent hundreds of millions of dollars building microwave and millimetre-wave links between Chicago and New Jersey, because microwaves travel through air at essentially \(c\) while light in fibre travels at \(0.67c\) — and the fibre route is longer than the straight line. The payoff is a few milliseconds. When an industry spends that much money to buy milliseconds, it is confirming that the physics constant in the denominator is real.
Interview angle The question is usually implicit: “would you replicate synchronously across regions?” The scoring answer computes the RTT floor first.
  • “Network latency is negligible.” Inside a rack, sometimes. Across a WAN, never.
  • Quoting “disk is slow” without distinguishing sequential from random, or HDD from NVMe — a 100× difference.
  • Treating cross-AZ as free. It is ~1 ms and it is charged per GB in most clouds.

Write down the ratio real/theoretical. That ratio (usually 1.4–1.8) is your personal fudge factor for every future estimate.
D005

Non-Functional Requirements

26 min · Phase 0 · Prereq: D002, D003 · Homework: 25 min

Hook — watch it break A payments team ships a service with 99.99% availability and a beautiful autoscaling story. Six months in, an engineer discovers that the "durable" write path acknowledged the client after writing to a single replica's page cache, before fsync. A power event loses 40 seconds of transactions. The service was highly available the entire time. It was never durable. Those are different words and the team had been using them as one.

Core concept — the nine NFRs and how each is measured

A functional requirement says what the system does. A non-functional requirement says how well. Every one of these must be attached to a number and a measurement method, or it is a wish.

Deep dive — durability is not availability

They fail independently and are engineered with different mechanisms.

Durability, expressed honestly
\[ \text{"11 nines"} \;=\; 99.999999999\% \;\Rightarrow\; P(\text{loss per object-year}) = 10^{-11} \] \[ \text{Store } 10^{7}\text{ objects} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{expected losses/year} = 10^{7}\times10^{-11}=10^{-4} \]

One object lost every 10,000 years. That number is a model output, not a measurement — it assumes independent device failures. It says nothing about correlated failures: a bad deploy that deletes data, an operator error, ransomware, or a region-wide event. Which is why backups (Day 116) are a separate control from replication.

Deep dive — extracting NFRs from a vague prompt

"Design a ride-hailing app" contains no NFRs. Here is the interrogation sequence that produces them, in the order you should ask:

  1. Who is the user and what is the worst moment? (Rider stranded at 2 a.m. → availability is the top NFR.)
  2. What is the unit of loss? (A lost location ping: fine. A lost payment: not fine. Two different durability tiers in the same system.)
  3. What is the read:write ratio and the fan-out? (Sets the shape.)
  4. What is the acceptable staleness, per data type? (Driver location: 4 s. Account balance: 0.)
  5. What is the regulatory surface? (PII, residency, retention — these change the architecture, not the config.)
  6. What is the budget per unit? (If nobody will say, assume it matters and design for cost visibility.)

The single most valuable habit: tier your NFRs per data type rather than per system. Almost every real system has three tiers — money-grade, user-content-grade, and telemetry-grade — with wildly different durability and consistency needs. Designing all three to the strictest tier is how budgets die.

Hook — watch it break A platform team promises 99.99% availability to every internal consumer. Their service is a thin wrapper over six dependencies, each of which promises 99.9%. Deep dive — error budgets

Error budget
\[ \text{Budget} \;=\; 1 - \text{SLO} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{Budget}_{99.9\%} = 0.1\% \text{ of requests} \] \[ \text{At } 10^{9}\ \text{requests}/28\text{d}:\quad 10^{6}\ \text{failures allowed} \]

The budget converts reliability from a moral argument into an accounting one. The policy that makes it work:

  • Budget remaining → ship features, take risks, deploy on Friday if you like.
  • Budget exhausted → feature freeze; the only merges allowed are reliability fixes. Automatic, not negotiated.
  • Budget consistently unspent → your SLO is too loose or you are over-investing in reliability. Loosen it, or ship faster.
Burn rate — how fast you are spending it
\[ \text{burn rate} \;=\; \frac{\text{observed error rate}}{1-\text{SLO}} \]

A burn rate of 1 exhausts the budget exactly at the end of the window. A burn rate of 14.4 exhausts a 30-day budget in ~2 days — the standard threshold for a page. Multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting is Day 112.

  • The user's path includes their Wi-Fi, their ISP, their DNS resolver and their phone. If the end-to-end path is 99.5%, your 100% is invisible to them and you paid for it anyway.
  • Cost of the next nine is roughly an order of magnitude each time. Going 99.9 → 99.99 typically means multi-region active-active, which can double infrastructure spend.
  • A 100% target forbids all change, and change is where the value is. The budget exists to license risk-taking.
War story Google's SRE book describes the error-budget mechanism as the resolution to a structural conflict: product wants launch velocity, SRE wants stability, and without a shared number the argument is political. Making the budget an explicit, agreed quantity turns "should we ship this?" into "do we have budget?" — a question with an answer. The organisational insight is bigger than the maths.

Scale up first. Seriously. A modern single server can have 128+ cores, 2 TB of RAM and 100 Gbps of network. Deep dive — the ceiling on scale-out

Adding machines does not add throughput linearly forever, because some fraction of the work is serial and some is coordination. Amdahl gives the first bound:

Amdahl's Law — serial fraction \(s\) caps speedup
\[ \text{Speedup}(N) \;=\; \frac{1}{s + \frac{1-s}{N}} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \lim_{N\to\infty}\text{Speedup} = \frac{1}{s} \]

With just 5% serial work, the maximum possible speedup is 20×, no matter how many machines you buy. The Universal Scalability Law adds a second, worse term for coordination — cross-talk between nodes — and it does not merely plateau, it declines:

Universal Scalability Law (Gunther)
\[ C(N) \;=\; \frac{N}{1+\sigma(N-1)+\kappa N(N-1)} \]

\(\sigma\) = contention (serialisation, like Amdahl's \(s\)); \(\kappa\) = coherency cost (nodes must agree). When \(\kappa > 0\), throughput peaks at \(N^{*}=\sqrt{(1-\sigma)/\kappa}\) and then falls. This is why adding database replicas can make a system slower.

Session affinity (routing a user to the same backend every time) makes the statefulness invisible rather than fixing it. Deep dive — Conway's Law is a design constraint, not a proverb

Conway's 1968 observation: organisations design systems that mirror their own communication structure. The practical version for architects is the inverse manoeuvre — choose the team structure you want, and the architecture follows. Two teams that cannot get a meeting will build two services with a hard interface, whether you planned it or not.

Why team size caps service count
\[ \text{communication paths} = \binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2} \]

10 people → 45 pairwise channels. 30 people → 435. Splitting into teams of 5–8 with explicit interfaces is a coordination-cost optimisation, and microservices are the architectural expression of it. If you have one team, you are paying the cost and getting none of the benefit.

The default recommendation, stated plainly

Start with a modular monolith. One deploy artifact, one database, but hard internal module boundaries: each module owns its tables, exposes an interface, and cross-module imports are blocked at build time. This buys you the thing that actually matters — the ability to find the seams later — without paying for the network. When a module genuinely needs to scale, deploy or fail independently, extract it. The seam is already there.

Migration triggers — extract a service when at least two are true
  • A distinct team owns it and is blocked by the shared release train.
  • Its scaling profile is materially different (10× the traffic, or GPU-bound, or memory-bound).
  • Its failure should not take down the rest (payment processing, third-party integrations).
  • It has a genuinely independent data lifecycle and no need for cross-module transactions.
  • Its compliance boundary differs (PCI, PHI) and isolation reduces audit scope.

Notice what is not on the list: "the codebase is big", "microservices are best practice", and "we want to use Kubernetes". None of those are triggers.

Why thread-per-request stops working
\[ \text{CPU spent switching} \approx \frac{N_{\text{switches/s}} \times t_{\text{switch}}}{1} \] \[ 10^{5}\ \text{switches/s} \times 2\,\mu s = 0.2 = \mathbf{20\%\ of\ one\ core\ doing\ nothing\ useful} \]

Core concept — user space, kernel space, syscalls

Your code runs in user space and cannot touch hardware. Every network read, disk write, timer and memory map goes through the kernel via a syscall — a controlled trap that switches privilege level.

  • Syscall cost: roughly 100–500 ns on modern hardware, and it got worse after the Spectre/Meltdown mitigations added page-table isolation.
  • Consequence: batch your syscalls. Writing 1 byte 1,000 times is ~1,000 syscalls; writing 1,000 bytes once is one. This is the entire reason buffered I/O exists.
  • io_uring exists to amortise this further — submission and completion queues shared with the kernel, so many operations cost one syscall or none.

Deep dive — file descriptors and the C10K problem

In Unix, everything is a file descriptor: sockets, pipes, files, timers, epoll instances. Limits stack, and you must raise all of them:

C10K (Dan Kegel, 1999) asked how one server handles ten thousand concurrent connections. The answer was not faster hardware — it was replacing \(O(n)\) readiness scanning (select/poll, which walk the entire fd set on every call) with \(O(1)\) event notification (epoll, kqueue). Today the interesting number is C10M, and the answer involves kernel bypass. The structural lesson survives: cost per idle connection must be near zero.

Deep dive — memory: virtual, paging, swap, OOM

  • Virtual memory means a process's addresses are not physical. Allocating 100 GB of virtual address space on a 16 GB box succeeds — pages are only backed when touched. This is why RSS matters and VSZ mostly does not.
  • Page faults: minor (page is in memory, just not mapped — cheap) vs major (must read from disk — ~100 μs on NVMe, ~10 ms on HDD). A service that suddenly slows with normal CPU is often taking major faults.
  • Swap trades latency for capacity, and for a latency-sensitive service that trade is almost always wrong. Swapping a hot page turns a 100 ns access into a 100 μs one — a 1,000× cliff. Most production services disable swap and let the process die instead, because a dead process fails over and a swapping process just poisons the load balancer's health checks.
  • The OOM killer picks a victim by oom_score, and it frequently picks your main application because it is the largest consumer. In containers, hitting the cgroup memory limit kills the process with exit code 137 — which shows up as a mysterious restart, not an error message.

Design consequence: memory limits must be set below the point where the kernel intervenes, and your service should shed load (D063) before it allocates its way into a kill. Failing fast with a 503 is a better outcome than being terminated mid-request.

CPU scheduling, briefly but usefully

Linux's CFS gives each runnable thread a fair share of CPU time. Two consequences for design:

  • Container CPU limits are enforced by throttling, not by slowing the clock. A container limited to 0.5 CPU that tries to use a full core gets stopped for half of every 100 ms period. Result: p99 latency spikes of ~50 ms with CPU utilisation showing a comfortable 50%. Check nr_throttled before you believe a CPU graph.
  • Noisy neighbours are real. Co-tenant workloads evict your L3 cache lines and saturate memory bandwidth. This is a large part of why p99 varies between identical instances.
War story The "container CPU throttling" pattern has been documented repeatedly across the industry: teams set conservative CPU limits, latency gets dramatically worse, and CPU dashboards look fine because average utilisation is well under the limit. The fix is usually to raise or remove the limit while keeping the request for scheduling, and to alert on throttled periods rather than on utilisation. It is the clearest example of an OS mechanism producing a latency problem that no application profiler can explain.
Interview angle This material shows up as diagnosis questions: "the service returns errors but CPU is low", "latency spikes every 30 seconds", "it works with one client and fails with a thousand".
  • Jumping to "add more replicas" when the limit is per-process and will be hit on every replica.
  • Not knowing that a socket is a file descriptor, and therefore that connection count has an OS ceiling.
  • Blaming the network for what is an accept-queue overflow.
  • Assuming GC pauses are the only source of latency spikes. Throttling, major faults and TCP retransmits all look identical from the application's side.

Most "async" runtimes (Node, Netty, asyncio, Tokio) are technically readiness-based multiplexing, not completion-based async I/O. The distinction matters for disk I/O: epoll does not work on regular files, so file reads in these runtimes are usually delegated to a background thread pool. That thread pool is a queue, and queues have depth limits.

Deep dive — sizing a thread pool with Little's Law

The most common production question this answers: "how many threads should my pool have?" Not "more". The formula:

Pool size from utilisation target
\[ N_{\text{threads}} \;=\; N_{\text{cores}} \times U_{\text{target}} \times \left(1+\frac{W}{C}\right) \]

\(W\) = wait time (blocked on I/O), \(C\) = compute time, \(U_{\text{target}}\) = target CPU utilisation (use 0.7–0.8).

Worked: 8 cores, target 0.75, a request that computes for 5 ms and waits 45 ms on the database. \(N = 8\times0.75\times(1+45/5) = 8\times0.75\times10 = \mathbf{60}\) threads. Note what happens if you tune the query so \(W\) drops to 5 ms: \(N = 8\times0.75\times2 = 12\). Making the dependency faster shrinks the correct pool size by 5×. Pools sized once and never revisited are one of the most common sources of unexplained latency.

Cross-check with Little's Law: at 2,000 req/s and 50 ms per request, \(L = 100\) in flight. If the pool is 60, the other 40 are queueing — and queueing delay is what your p99 is made of. Either raise the pool, reduce \(W\), or shed load (D063). Raising the pool is the option that stops working first.

Deep dive — the concurrency hazards you will actually hit

  • Race condition — two threads read-modify-write the same location. Fix: atomics for single words, locks for invariants spanning multiple locations, or don't share (partition by key so one thread owns one key — see D076).
  • Lock contention — the lock itself becomes the bottleneck. An uncontended mutex is ~17 ns; a contended one costs a syscall and a context switch, ~1–10 μs. Contention makes throughput decrease as you add threads (this is the \(\kappa\) term from D007).
  • False sharing — two threads write different variables on the same 64-byte cache line, and the line ping-pongs between cores. Symptom: adding threads makes it slower, with no lock in sight. Fix: pad to cache-line boundaries.
  • Deadlock — circular wait. Prevent by ordering lock acquisition globally (D034).
  • Async coloured functions — in Node/Python, one blocking call anywhere in an async call chain blocks the loop, and the type system usually will not tell you.
Why "async everywhere" is not free
  • Stack traces become useless; a callback's stack does not include who scheduled it. You need explicit context propagation (D111).
  • Debuggers and profilers show the loop, not the logical request.
  • Every await point is an interleaving point — a place where shared state can change under you. Async code has races too; they are just harder to see.
  • CPU-bound work must be moved to a worker pool explicitly, which means you now have two concurrency models in one process.

Java 21's virtual threads are the current best answer to this tension: blocking-style code, coroutine economics, real stack traces. If you are choosing a runtime for a new I/O-bound service today, "coroutines with blocking-looking syntax" (Go, virtual threads, Kotlin) gives you most of the event loop's efficiency without the colour problem.

War story Redis is single-threaded for command execution, by design, and is one of the fastest datastores in existence. The design works because every command is O(1) or O(log n) and returns in microseconds — so no command monopolises the loop. It also means a single KEYS * on a large database, or a big ZRANGE, blocks every other client for the duration. That is the event-loop tradeoff in one product: extraordinary throughput, on the condition that nothing takes long. Redis 6+ added threaded I/O for reading and writing sockets, but kept command execution single-threaded — because the single-threaded model is what makes its atomicity guarantees free.
Interview angle Expect: "how many concurrent users can one server handle?" The right move is to ask what they are doing — idle WebSockets, short CPU-light requests, or heavy computation — and then apply the model.
  • "Async is faster." Async is more efficient per connection. For CPU-bound work it is slower, because of scheduling overhead.
  • Sizing a thread pool at "2 × cores" for an I/O-bound workload. That formula is for CPU-bound work only.
  • Unbounded thread pools or unbounded queues. Both convert an overload into a memory exhaustion (D063).
  • Not knowing that their runtime's file I/O is served by a hidden thread pool with a default size of 4.

Storage Hardware & the OS Page Cache

28 min · Phase 0 · Prereq: D004, D010 · Homework: 30 min · Phase 0 finale

Hook — watch it break A database benchmark shows 40,000 writes per second on a laptop and 900 on the production server with enterprise SSDs. The laptop is not faster. The laptop is lying: its disk cache acknowledges writes before they reach media, and the production server is honouring fsync. One of those machines will lose your data on a power cut, and it is the fast one.

Core concept — HDD physics

Time to read a random block from a spinning disk
\[ T_{\text{access}} \;=\; T_{\text{seek}} \;+\; T_{\text{rotation}} \;+\; T_{\text{transfer}} \] \[ T_{\text{rotation}}^{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{60}{2\cdot\text{RPM}}\ \text{seconds} \]

For a 7,200 RPM drive: average rotational latency \(= 60/(2\times7200) = 4.17\) ms. Average seek is about 4–9 ms. Transfer of 4 KB at ~200 MB/s is 0.02 ms — negligible. So a random 4 KB read costs roughly 9 ms, of which 99.8% is mechanical positioning.

The consequence, in IOPS
\[ \text{IOPS}_{\text{random}} \approx \frac{1}{9\ \text{ms}} \approx 110\ \text{per drive} \] \[ \text{vs sequential: } \frac{200\ \text{MB/s}}{4\ \text{KB}} = 51{,}200\ \text{IOPS} \]

A 465× difference between random and sequential on the same device. This single ratio explains: why B-Trees exist (minimise seeks by maximising fanout), why LSM trees exist (turn random writes into sequential ones), why Kafka is fast (append-only log), and why "just add an index" can make a table scan slower than no index at all.

Core concept — SSD internals, and why they wear out

You cannot overwrite a page in place. To change one 4 KB page inside a 4 MB block, the controller must copy the live pages elsewhere, erase the whole block, and rewrite. That amplification is the central fact of SSD behaviour:

Write amplification factor
\[ \text{WAF} \;=\; \frac{\text{bytes written to NAND}}{\text{bytes written by the host}} \]
  • Sequential, large writes: WAF ≈ 1.0–1.1.
  • Random 4 KB writes on a nearly-full drive: WAF can reach 3–10, occasionally worse.
  • Consequences: throughput drops, latency becomes bimodal (some writes wait for garbage collection), and endurance is consumed at WAF× the expected rate.

TRIM / discard tells the SSD which blocks the filesystem no longer needs, so garbage collection can skip copying dead data. Over-provisioning (leaving 10–25% unallocated) has the same effect and is why "enterprise" and "consumer" drives with identical NAND behave so differently under sustained random write.

Endurance, in numbers you can use: a drive rated at 1 DWPD (drive writes per day) over 5 years with 2 TB capacity tolerates \(2\ \text{TB}\times365\times5 = 3.65\) PB of host writes. At WAF 4, your effective budget is ~0.9 PB. A write-heavy LSM workload can burn that in under two years — which is why compaction tuning (D037) is a hardware-lifetime decision, not just a performance one.

Deep dive — the page cache and how databases achieve durability

The page cache in one paragraph

Linux uses all free RAM as a cache for file data. Reads that hit it cost ~100 ns instead of ~100 μs. Writes land in dirty pages, flushed by the pdflush/writeback threads according to vm.dirty_ratio and vm.dirty_background_ratio. Two consequences for design: (1) "free memory" on a database server should be near zero — that is the cache working, not a leak; (2) a large dirty_ratio means a flush storm can stall all writers at once, which shows up as periodic multi-second latency spikes with no application cause.

Filesystems and journaling
  • ext4 journal modes: data=ordered (default — metadata journaled, data written before metadata commits), data=journal (both, safest, ~2× write cost), data=writeback (fastest, can expose stale data after a crash).
  • XFS is the common choice for large-file, high-throughput workloads; ZFS/Btrfs add checksums and snapshots at the cost of extra write amplification (copy-on-write).
  • Databases usually want journaling for metadata only — they have their own WAL, and double-journaling is wasted work.

IOPS vs throughput vs latency — pick the right one

MetricDefinitionBottleneck forHow to improve

They are linked — this is Little's Law again
\[ \text{Queue depth} \;=\; \text{IOPS} \times \text{latency} \]

Pushing queue depth up raises IOPS and raises latency. A device advertising 600,000 IOPS is quoting it at queue depth 256 — where per-operation latency is far above its idle figure. Benchmark at the queue depth your workload actually produces.

War story Algolia published a post-mortem in 2015 after a batch of enterprise SSDs corrupted data under a specific access pattern. The root cause was traced to a firmware bug interacting with how the filesystem issued TRIM commands — the drives would zero out ranges that were still in use. It is a reminder that the storage stack has at least five layers of code beneath your database (filesystem, block layer, driver, HBA firmware, drive firmware), any of which can violate the guarantees the layer above assumes. This is the real argument for checksums at the application level and for restore drills (D116).
Interview angle Storage hardware shows up indirectly: "why is this database slow?", "why do we use an append-only log?", "how would you make writes durable?"
  • "SSDs are fast" without distinguishing read from write, or fresh from full.
  • Treating RAID as a backup strategy.
  • Ignoring that cloud block storage has a provisioned IOPS ceiling — the most common capacity-planning miss.
  • Not knowing what fsync costs, and therefore proposing "just commit every write" at 50k writes/s.
  • Assuming the page cache means reads are free. It means hot reads are free; the working set still has to fit.
