How to read these notes

Start here · 4 min

Each phase is one self-contained HTML file. Open it, work down the lesson cards, and use the left rail to jump. Every card carries the same nine parts in the same order, so after three or four lessons you stop reading the headings and just read the content.

The ribbon under every title

The coloured bar under each lesson title is that lesson's timeline, drawn to scale from its real runtime. Hover any segment for its start timecode and duration. It is not decoration — it is the chapter list, and it is the same nine-part shape in all 145 lessons, which is the whole point: repetition is the teaching device.

Standard lesson shape at 30 min — hover a band. Case studies (Days 121–140) use a different ten-part shape.

The five callout types

Hook — watch it breakOpens every lesson. The failure comes before the fix, because a mechanism you have not seen fail is a mechanism you have only memorised.
War storyA named outage, paper, or engineering-blog post. If a claim in these notes has no source, treat it as a heuristic, not a fact.
Interview angleHow the topic actually gets asked, plus the wrong answers that sound right. The wrong answers are marked ✕.
A quiet aside — a definition, a caveat, or a number worth memorising.
HomeworkAlways hands-on, always the last thing in the card. The notes are the cheap part; the homework is where the learning happens.

Notation used throughout

Maths renders live. Latency is \(L\), arrival rate \(\lambda\), service rate \(\mu\), utilisation \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\), replica count \(N\), read quorum \(R\), write quorum \(W\). Storage uses binary prefixes when talking about memory (\(1\,\text{GiB} = 2^{30}\) bytes) and decimal prefixes when talking about disks and networks (\(1\,\text{GB} = 10^{9}\) bytes), which is exactly the ambiguity that makes every capacity estimate 7% wrong. Say which one you mean.

Production notes

If you are recording this, not just studying it

Consistency rules that make a playlist bingeable

  • The same seven-step design template in every case study. Viewers should be able to predict your next move by Day 125 — that predictability is the curriculum working.
  • One recurring example system that evolves across phases (the notes use a fictional chat product, Chatterbox) so each new concept lands on ground the viewer already knows.
  • Every video ends with homework and a one-line teaser. No exceptions, including the recaps.
  • Numbered thumbnails D001–D145 with a phase colour. The playlist grid becomes readable at a glance, which is most of what makes people return.

Make once, reuse 145 times

  • A phase cheat-sheet PDF (ten of them).
  • The seven-step design template card, the latency-numbers card, the consistency-model comparison chart, and a blank estimation worksheet.
  • An SVG component kit: box, cylinder (datastore), queue, client, region boundary, arrow with label. Draw every diagram from the same twelve shapes and the playlist looks authored rather than assembled.

Scope control

If 145 is too many to commit to, ship Days 1–66 as Season 1. It stands alone as a complete beginner-to-intermediate course. Season 2 (Days 67–145) is where the depth and the case studies live, and it is the half that gets people hired.

Review checkpoints

Publish a ten-minute recap after Days 12, 26, 48, 56, 66, 84, 96, 108, 120 and 140. Recaps are where you correct mistakes from earlier videos — do it openly, on camera. It is the single cheapest trust-building move available to a technical channel.
