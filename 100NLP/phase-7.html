Day 109

Multilingual & Cross-Lingual NLP

24 min

One model, a hundred languages, and a tax on every one of them that is not English.

The models

ModelLanguagesSetup
mBERT104MLM on Wikipedia, shared 110k WordPiece vocabulary, no parallel data at all
XLM-R100RoBERTa recipe on 2.5 TB CommonCrawl, 250k SentencePiece. The practical default.
mT5 / mBART101 / 25Seq2seq — needed when you must generate
LaBSE / mUSE109 / 16Sentence embeddings aligned across languages — for bitext mining and cross-lingual retrieval

Why zero-shot transfer works at all

Train a classifier head on English NER, evaluate on Swahili, and it works far above chance — with no parallel data and no shared script. The leading explanations: shared subword tokens act as anchor points (numbers, names, borrowings, punctuation); shared structural regularities in the objective push languages into overlapping representation subspaces; and joint training on a shared vocabulary forces a common space even without alignment supervision. Pires et al. found transfer degrades with typological distance but does not require lexical overlap, which rules out the simplest "shared wordpieces do everything" story.

The curse of multilinguality

$$\text{per-language quality} \;\propto\; \frac{\text{model capacity}}{\text{number of languages}}\quad\text{(past a threshold)}$$

Conneau et al. measured it directly: adding languages helps low-resource ones up to a point (positive transfer), then starts hurting all of them as capacity is diluted. Raising model size pushes the threshold out. Practical consequence: a 100-language model is worse at French than a French-only model of the same size, and you should check whether a monolingual or regional model exists before defaulting to XLM-R.

The tokenizer tax

LanguageTypical fertility on a multilingual vocabEffect

This is not only an efficiency issue Higher fertility means: the same document costs more, fits worse in the context window, and is represented by more, less meaningful units — so quality drops too. It is a compounding disadvantage that falls precisely on the languages with the least data. When you report multilingual results, report fertility per language alongside accuracy.

Low-resource strategies

  • Continued pretraining on target-language text before fine-tuning — usually the largest single gain.
  • Vocabulary extension — add target-language tokens and initialize their embeddings from the mean of their subword pieces.
  • Language adapters (MAD-X) — a language adapter plus a task adapter, composed; lets you add a language without retraining.
  • Translate-train (MT the training data into the target) vs translate-test (MT the test input into English). Translate-train usually wins; both depend on MT quality.
  • Few-shot in the target language beats zero-shot by a lot — even 50 examples.

Code-switching ("mera phone kharab ho gaya, can you help?") breaks language ID, breaks monolingual tokenizers, and is under-represented in every benchmark. If your users code-switch, you need code-switched evaluation data; there is no substitute.

Exercise Fine-tune XLM-R on English NER and evaluate zero-shot on three typologically different languages. Report F1 and fertility for each. Then add 100 target-language examples and re-measure — the jump is usually large.
Day 110

Speech Meets NLP

22 min

Your NLP model is about to receive text nobody wrote. It will not behave as you expect.

The pipeline

Audio → resample to 16 kHz mono → features → acoustic model → text. Classic features: mel spectrogram (STFT magnitudes mapped onto the mel scale, which spaces frequency bands the way human hearing does) and MFCC (a DCT of the log-mel spectrogram, which decorrelates the bands — essential for GMM-era systems, largely unnecessary for neural ones, which prefer raw log-mel).

CTC

The alignment problem: 1,000 audio frames, 20 output characters, no per-frame labels. CTC introduces a blank symbol and sums over every alignment that collapses to the target:

$$P(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{B}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}\prod_{t=1}^{T}P(\pi_t\mid\mathbf{x})$$

where \(\mathcal{B}\) removes repeats then blanks (so hh-e-ll-lo → hello, and the blank between the two ls is what preserves the double letter). The sum is computed with the forward algorithm — the same dynamic program as Day 29 and Day 63. CTC assumes conditional independence between output frames, which is why CTC systems benefit so much from an external language model at decoding time.

Whisper

An encoder–decoder transformer trained on ~680k hours of weakly-supervised multilingual audio scraped from the web. Its design choice is to make the task itself a prompt: special tokens select transcribe vs translate, the language, and whether to predict timestamps. So one model does ASR in 99 languages, speech translation into English, language ID and voice-activity detection. Robustness to accents, background noise and domain shift comes from data scale rather than architecture.

Word error rate, and its blind spots

$$\mathrm{WER}=\frac{S+D+I}{N}$$

Substitutions, deletions and insertions over reference words — Levenshtein at the word level (Day 14). It can exceed 1.0. What it misses: all errors are weighted equally, so mistranscribing "not" as "now" costs the same as "the" as "a" while being catastrophic for downstream meaning; it ignores punctuation and casing unless you evaluate them separately; and it is not comparable across languages with different word-segmentation conventions (use CER for Chinese and Japanese).

What ASR output does to your NLP model No punctuation or casing (unless restored) · no paragraph structure · homophone substitutions · disfluencies ("um", "you know", restarts, repairs) · speaker turns interleaved · numbers written as words. A sentiment or NER model trained on clean text typically loses 5–15 points on transcripts. Mitigations: run punctuation and casing restoration first (a token classifier predicting the punctuation that follows each token); fine-tune your downstream model on transcript-like text; or train on synthetically degraded text (lowercase, strip punctuation, inject homophone errors).

Diarization and downstream

Diarization answers "who spoke when": VAD → segmentation → speaker embeddings (x-vectors or ECAPA-TDNN) → clustering. Errors compound with ASR errors, so evaluate the joint system, not each part alone. For meeting or call analytics, speaker-attributed transcripts are what your NLP consumes, and a diarization error silently reassigns a sentiment or an action item to the wrong person.

End-to-end SLU skips text entirely and predicts intents or slots directly from audio, avoiding error propagation and preserving prosody — which carries sarcasm, question intonation and emphasis that text loses. It needs task-specific audio data, which is why the cascaded pipeline still dominates in practice.

Exercise Transcribe an hour of audio with Whisper. Run your Phase 2 classifier on both the transcripts and the reference text and report the accuracy drop. Then add punctuation restoration and measure again.
Day 111

Multimodal & Document AI

24 min

Most real business text arrives as a scanned PDF with a table in it.

CLIP

Train an image encoder and a text encoder jointly so that matching pairs have high cosine similarity and mismatched pairs low — a symmetric InfoNCE over the batch, which is the Day 87 objective applied across modalities:

$$\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\text{i}\to\text{t}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{t}\to\text{i}}\bigr),\qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{i}\to\text{t}}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i}\log\frac{e^{\cos(\mathbf{v}_i,\mathbf{t}_i)/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{\cos(\mathbf{v}_i,\mathbf{t}_j)/\tau}}$$

Trained on 400M image-caption pairs, this yields zero-shot classification: embed the class names as "a photo of a {label}", embed the image, take the argmax cosine. No labelled classification data at all. The prompt template matters — an ensemble of 80 templates was worth several points in the original paper.

Vision-language models

The dominant recipe is: frozen vision encoder → projector → LLM. The projector (a linear layer, an MLP, or a Q-Former that compresses to a fixed set of query tokens) maps image patches into the LLM's token embedding space, so the LLM literally reads the image as tokens. Training is two-stage: align the projector on caption data with everything else frozen, then instruction-tune on visual QA data. LLaVA showed this works with a surprisingly small amount of instruction data.

Document understanding

Text alone loses layout, and layout is meaning: in an invoice, the number to the right of "Total" is the total. LayoutLM adds 2D positional embeddings (the normalized bounding box \((x_0,y_0,x_1,y_1)\) of each token) to the text embeddings; v2 and v3 add the image patch as a third modality and align text with image patches during pretraining.

$$\mathbf{h}_i = E_{\text{tok}}(w_i)+E_{\text{1D}}(i)+\underbrace{E_x(x_0)+E_x(x_1)+E_y(y_0)+E_y(y_1)+E_w(w)+E_h(h)}_{\text{2D layout}}$$

Measure OCR quality before blaming the model A 5% character error rate can mean a 30% drop in downstream extraction F1, because errors concentrate in exactly the rare tokens you care about — names, amounts, part numbers. Always: report OCR CER on a sample; check reading order manually on 10 pages; and validate extracted numbers against checksums or totals where the document provides them.

End-to-end document VLMs (Donut, Nougat, and modern general VLMs) skip OCR entirely and generate structured output directly from the page image. They avoid error propagation and handle tables better; they are slower, harder to debug, and can hallucinate plausible values into fields — which is worse than an OCR error, because it is not detectably wrong.

Exercise Build a document Q&A system over scanned PDFs. Compare the OCR+LayoutLM pipeline against an end-to-end VLM on the same 50 documents. Report accuracy per field type and count hallucinated values separately from missed ones.
Day 112

Information Extraction & Knowledge Graphs

24 min

Turning prose into rows you can query. The oldest applied NLP goal, and still hard.

Relation extraction

ApproachHowCost / quality
SupervisedMark the entity pair with special tokens, classify with an encoderBest quality; needs labelled data per relation type
Distant supervisionAlign a KB to text: if (Musk, founded, SpaceX) is in the KB, treat every sentence containing both as a positiveFree labels, noisy — most co-occurrences do not express the relation. Multi-instance learning mitigates it.
LLM-basedPrompt for a JSON list of triples with a fixed schemaZero-shot, flexible schema; hallucinates relations; expensive at corpus scale
OpenIEExtract (subject, relation, object) with no fixed schemaBroad coverage, unnormalized relations that need clustering afterwards

The standard supervised trick: insert entity markers into the input ([E1] Elon Musk [/E1] founded [E2] SpaceX [/E2]) and classify from the concatenation of the two entity-start representations. Simple and strong.

Event extraction

Two stages: find the trigger (a word that evokes an event and its type — acquired → BusinessAcquisition) and fill its arguments with roles (Acquirer, Acquired, Price, Date). Both are sequence-labelling or span-classification problems. The hard parts are events split across sentences and arguments realized only implicitly.

Entity linking

Three steps: mention detection (Day 32), candidate generation (an alias table plus fuzzy or dense retrieval), and disambiguation — score candidates by context similarity, prior popularity \(P(e\mid m)\), and coherence with other entities linked in the same document. The classic ambiguity is Washington: person, state, city, university, or football team. Coherence is what resolves it: if the document already linked Seattle, the state becomes far more likely.

Coreference

Detect mentions, then cluster those referring to the same entity. Modern systems score span pairs end-to-end (e2e-coref) or, in the higher-performing formulation, treat it as a word-level linking problem. Evaluation uses three metrics because none alone is adequate — MUC (link-based, insensitive to singletons), B³ (mention-based), CEAF (entity alignment) — and the reported score is their average, CoNLL F1, currently around 80–83 on OntoNotes. Coreference is what turns "The company said it would…" into a usable fact.

Building the graph

from neo4j import GraphDatabase

Three design rules that save the project (1) Canonical IDs, not surface strings — link to Wikidata QIDs or your own registry, or the graph fills with duplicate nodes. (2) Store provenance on every edge — document ID, character offsets, extractor version, confidence. Without it you cannot debug, audit, or retract. (3) Keep a confidence threshold and a review queue — a knowledge graph silently accumulating wrong edges is worse than no graph.

KG + RAG: use the graph for multi-hop and aggregate questions ("which suppliers are exposed to both X and Y?") that chunk retrieval cannot answer, and use vector retrieval for the descriptive text. Retrieve a subgraph, serialize it into the prompt, and cite the source documents on the edges.

Exercise Extract entities and relations from a corpus, link them to canonical IDs, and build a queryable graph. Then write five questions answerable only by traversal and check whether your Day 102 RAG system can answer them. It usually cannot — that gap is the argument for the graph.
Day 113

Domain-Specific NLP

22 min

Three domains, three sets of constraints that have nothing to do with model architecture.

Biomedical and clinical

UMLS is the backbone: a metathesaurus unifying 200+ vocabularies (SNOMED CT, ICD-10, RxNorm, LOINC, MeSH) under Concept Unique Identifiers. Normalizing a mention to a CUI is the domain's version of entity linking, and tools like scispaCy and QuickUMLS do it.

Negation and assertion are the whole task "No evidence of pneumonia" contains pneumonia. A naive extractor records the diagnosis. Clinical text needs an assertion classifier over each detected concept: present / absent / hypothetical / conditional / historical / experienced by someone other than the patient (family history). NegEx is the classic rule-based approach — trigger terms with a scope window — and it is still a strong baseline. Roughly half of all clinical concept mentions are negated or otherwise non-affirmed; getting this wrong inverts your output.

De-identification is a legal prerequisite. HIPAA Safe Harbor names 18 identifier categories that must be removed; the alternative is expert determination. Practically: high-recall NER plus regex plus a manual review pass, and surrogate replacement (swap real names for realistic fake ones, shift all dates by a consistent per-patient offset) rather than blanket redaction, so downstream models still see well-formed text. Note that recall matters far more than precision here, and residual risk is never zero.

Legal

Contract clause extraction, obligation and deadline detection, precedent retrieval. Characteristics: extremely long documents (a credit agreement can be 300 pages), deeply nested cross-references ("subject to Section 7.3(b)(ii)"), defined terms that override ordinary meaning, and a very low tolerance for false negatives — missing an indemnity clause is a materially different failure from missing a topic label. Practical approaches: hierarchical chunking that follows the document's own section structure, a defined-terms glossary extracted first and injected into context, and always returning the source span for human verification.

Financial

Earnings-call transcripts, filings, news. The twist is that sentiment depends on numeric context: "revenue grew 3%" is negative if guidance was 8%. So the useful pipeline extracts the number, the metric, the period and the comparison basis, then judges. Domain-specific difficulties: heavy hedging language, forward-looking-statement boilerplate that dominates by volume, and strict timing constraints (a signal is worthless late).

Adapting a model

$$\text{general LM}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{DAPT: domain corpus}\ }\ \text{domain LM}\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{TAPT: task's unlabelled text}\ }\ \xrightarrow{\ \text{fine-tune}\ }\ \text{task model}$$

Gururangan et al. showed both stages help and that they stack, with TAPT being remarkably cheap (it uses the training inputs you already have, just without labels). Add vocabulary extension when the domain has many terms that fragment badly — measure fertility on domain terms before and after.

Regulatory constraints are design constraints Clinical decision support may be a regulated medical device depending on jurisdiction and claim. Automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects trigger explanation and human-review obligations under GDPR Article 22. Financial advice is regulated. The EU AI Act assigns risk tiers with corresponding documentation duties. Find out which of these apply before you design the system, because "add a human in the loop" is cheap at design time and expensive after launch.
Exercise Take a general model and a specialist corpus. Measure the task metric with: (a) no adaptation, (b) TAPT only, (c) DAPT + TAPT. Report all three deltas and the compute each cost.
Day 114

Data Annotation & Dataset Construction

22 min

Your model's ceiling is your annotation guidelines. Nothing else in the pipeline matters as much.

Guidelines that survive contact with annotators

Structure: a one-paragraph task definition · a precise definition of each label with positive and negative examples · explicit rules for the boundary cases you will actually meet · a decision tree for ambiguity · instructions for what to do when the answer is genuinely unclear (an explicit "unclear" option is better than forcing a guess) · a changelog. Then pilot: three annotators, 50 items, measure agreement, and rewrite every rule that produced a disagreement. Expect 2–3 pilot rounds. Guidelines written without a pilot are always wrong.

Agreement, and how to read it

$$\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e}$$

\(p_o\) is observed agreement, \(p_e\) the agreement expected by chance. Cohen's \(\kappa\) is for two annotators; Fleiss' for many; Krippendorff's \(\alpha\) handles any number of annotators, missing data, and ordinal or interval labels — use it by default.

Kappa calculator — and the prevalence paradox

Adjudication

Options: majority vote (needs ≥3 annotators); an expert adjudicator resolving only disagreements (cheapest per item, and the disagreements are the interesting ones); or MACE/Dawid-Skene, which model annotator reliability and infer labels probabilistically — worth it when annotator quality varies. Always keep the raw individual annotations, not just the resolved label; disagreement is signal about item difficulty and is needed for any later re-analysis.

Reducing how much you must label

Active learning
Label the items the model is most uncertain about (entropy, least-confidence, or margin) or that are most representative (core-set). Typically reaches a target accuracy with 30–60% of the labels. Caveats: the resulting dataset is biased toward the decision boundary, so it is a poor test set and it does not transfer to a different model.
Weak supervision
Write labelling functions — regexes, keyword lists, an existing model, a knowledge-base lookup — each noisy and each covering part of the data. Snorkel's generative model estimates each function's accuracy from their agreements and disagreements (no ground truth needed) and produces probabilistic labels to train on.
LLM labelling
Fast and cheap. Works well for coarse, well-defined categories. Fails quietly on subtle or subjective distinctions, and imports the labelling model's biases into your dataset permanently. Always validate on a human-labelled sample, report the agreement, and keep the provenance flag on every row.
The self-fulfilling loop If you label with an LLM and then evaluate the fine-tuned student against those same labels, you are measuring distillation fidelity, not accuracy. Your test set must be human-labelled, always, no exceptions.

Datasheets for datasets (Gebru et al.) — document motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, uses, distribution and maintenance. Half a day of work; it is what makes a dataset reusable and auditable, and it is what a reviewer looks for first.

Exercise Write guidelines for a task you care about. Annotate 200 examples with a partner, independently. Compute \(\kappa\). Then rewrite the guidelines based on the disagreements and re-annotate 50 — the improvement in \(\kappa\) is the deliverable.

28 min

Throughput and latency are different problems with different solutions. Know which you have.

Continuous batching and PagedAttention

Static batching waits for the whole batch to finish, so one long generation blocks nine short ones. Continuous batching evicts finished sequences and admits new ones at every decoding step, raising GPU utilization several-fold. PagedAttention (vLLM) then fixes the memory side: instead of pre-allocating a contiguous block for each sequence's maximum possible length — which wastes 60–80% of KV memory to internal fragmentation — it allocates fixed-size pages like an operating system's virtual memory, and lets sequences share pages for a common prompt prefix. Together these are typically a 5–20× throughput improvement over naive serving.

Speculative decoding

A small draft model proposes \(k\) tokens; the large model verifies all \(k\) in one forward pass and accepts the longest prefix consistent with its own distribution. The acceptance test is constructed so the output distribution is provably identical to sampling from the large model alone — it is a pure latency optimization, not an approximation. Typical speedup 2–3×, depending on how often the draft agrees. Self-speculative variants (Medusa, EAGLE, n-gram lookup) avoid needing a separate draft model.

Serving cost and latency calculator

Caching, streaming, autoscaling

Caching: exact (hash of the full request), semantic (embedding nearest-neighbour above a threshold — tune it carefully, a false hit is a wrong answer), and prefix/KV (shared system prompt, which mainly cuts TTFT). Streaming via SSE turns a 4-second wait into a 300 ms wait plus a readable stream, which is a bigger perceived-quality win than most model upgrades. Cold starts are the autoscaling problem: loading a 14 GB model takes 30–120 seconds, so keep a warm floor of replicas and scale on queue depth rather than CPU.

Day 116

MLOps for NLP

24 min

Reproducibility, versioning and drift. The unglamorous work that decides whether the model still works in six months.

Experiment tracking

Log for every run: the git commit, the full config, the data version hash, every hyperparameter, all metrics per step, the environment (pip freeze), the hardware, and the artifacts. MLflow (self-hosted, open) or Weights & Biases (hosted, better UI, sweep support). The rule: if it is not logged, the run did not happen — six weeks later you will not remember which of forty runs produced the checkpoint you shipped.

Versioning the whole chain

$$\text{model} = f\bigl(\text{code},\ \text{data},\ \text{config},\ \text{environment},\ \text{seed}\bigr)$$

Git handles code. DVC (or LakeFS, or dataset versions in the tracker) handles data — it stores a hash pointer in git and the bytes in object storage, so git checkout plus dvc checkout restores an exact state. Hydra handles config composition and records the resolved config with each run. A model registry handles the promotion path: staging → production → archived, with lineage back to the run.

CI/CD with eval gates

  • On every PR: lint, unit tests, and a fast smoke eval on 20 items.
  • On merge to main: the full golden-set eval; fail the build if any headline metric regresses beyond a stated tolerance, and fail on any regression in a protected slice.
  • Before promotion: latency and cost benchmarks; a bias audit; a check that the model card is updated.
  • On deploy: shadow traffic first, then a canary percentage, then full — with an automatic rollback trigger.

Retraining triggers: scheduled (monthly), performance-based (metric below threshold on the audit sample), volume-based (N new labels available), or event-based (a known upstream change). Day 117

Ethics, Bias, Privacy & Law

24 min

Not a compliance chore. Every item here is a design constraint that is cheap now and expensive later.

Two kinds of harm

Representational
The system demeans, stereotypes, or erases a group. Toxicity classifiers over-flagging African-American English; translation defaulting gender-neutral pronouns to stereotyped genders; an image model that renders "CEO" as one demographic.
Allocational
The system distributes an opportunity or resource unequally. Résumé ranking, loan text screening, content-moderation strikes. These are the ones with legal exposure, and they are the ones to prioritize.

Fairness metrics are mathematically incompatible

$$\underbrace{P(\hat Y{=}1\mid A{=}a)}_{\text{demographic parity}}\qquad \underbrace{P(\hat Y{=}1\mid Y{=}1,A{=}a)}_{\text{equal opportunity}}\qquad \underbrace{P(Y{=}1\mid \hat Y{=}1,A{=}a)}_{\text{predictive parity}}$$

Chouldechova and Kleinberg et al. proved these cannot all hold simultaneously unless base rates are equal across groups or the classifier is perfect. So choosing a fairness metric is choosing a value judgement, not finding the correct one. What you owe your reviewers is: which metric you optimized, why, what it costs on the others, and who was consulted. Report the disaggregated confusion matrix per group and let people see the trade.

Memorization and extraction

Carlini et al. showed training data can be extracted verbatim from LLMs by sampling and filtering by membership-inference signals, and that memorization grows with model size, with duplication in the training set, and with the length of context you condition on. Practical consequences: deduplicate aggressively (Day 90), scrub secrets and PII before training rather than after, and test your own model for regurgitation of known training strings before release.

Differential privacy gives a formal bound: a mechanism is \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP if no single training example can change the output distribution by more than \(e^{\varepsilon}\). DP-SGD achieves it by clipping per-example gradients and adding calibrated noise. It works and it costs — meaningful utility loss at useful \(\varepsilon\), and much slower training. Federated learning keeps data on-device and shares only updates, which is a different (weaker) property: updates can leak, so it is usually combined with DP and secure aggregation.

This is a summary for engineers, not legal advice. Jurisdictions differ, the rules are moving quickly, and the details matter. If your system touches employment, credit, healthcare, education, insurance or law enforcement, get an actual lawyer involved before launch, not after.

Environmental cost

Report it where you can: GPU-hours × TDP × PUE × grid carbon intensity. The tooling exists (codecarbon, ML CO2 Impact). Inference at scale typically dominates training over a model's lifetime, which is another argument for the distillation and quantization work in Days 96–97. Reporting it is now standard practice at the major venues.

Documentation as the deliverable

Model card (Day 36) · datasheet (Day 114) · impact assessment for high-risk uses: who could be harmed, how, how likely, what mitigations, what residual risk remains, and who is accountable. Write the residual-risk section honestly — a document that claims no residual risk is a document nobody believes.

Exercise Write a full model card for your Day 108 project including a bias audit with disaggregated metrics, the fairness metric you chose and why, the red-team results from Day 107, and a stated residual risk. This is the single most differentiating item in an NLP portfolio.
Day 118

How to Read NLP Research

22 min

Reading fast and sceptically is a skill. Here is the procedure.

The three-pass method (Keshav)

  1. Five minutes — title, abstract, section headings, figures, conclusion, references you recognize. Decide: what category, what is the claimed contribution, is it relevant to me? Most papers stop here.
  2. One hour — read for the argument, ignoring proofs and implementation detail. Study every figure and table; check axes, error bars, and baselines. You should be able to summarize the evidence to someone else.
  3. Several hours — reimplement the core idea mentally (or actually). Challenge every assumption. This is how you find the weakness.

The scepticism checklist

Look forRed flag
BaselinesIs the strongest simple baseline present? TF-IDF+LR for classification, BM25 for retrieval, lead-3 for summarization, head+tail truncation for long documents. Its absence is the most common flaw in the literature.
Tuning parityWas the baseline tuned as hard as the proposed method? Usually not, and it is rarely stated.
VarianceSingle seed? No confidence intervals? A 0.3-point gain with no variance reported is not a result.
AblationsWhich component actually causes the gain? If there is no ablation, the paper does not know either.
Benchmark selectionFive datasets in the main table, twelve in the appendix, and the method loses on seven.
ContaminationFor anything LLM-based: was the benchmark in the training data? Usually unanswerable and usually unaddressed.
Cost+1 point for 10× the compute is a different claim from +1 point for free. Very often omitted.
Metric choiceDoes the headline metric match the claimed capability? Accuracy on imbalanced data, BLEU for paraphrase-heavy tasks, ROUGE for factuality.

Keeping up without drowning

arXiv cs.CL is thousands of papers a month; you cannot and should not read them. A workable routine: follow the ACL Anthology for peer-reviewed work (ACL, EMNLP, NAACL, EACL, COLING, TACL, CL) and treat arXiv as preprints requiring more scepticism; subscribe to two or three curated newsletters; skim titles for ten minutes daily and queue three for pass 2 weekly; read one paper properly (pass 3) per month. Depth on a few beats skimming many.

A ~25-paper reading list, mapped to this playlist Foundations — Bengio et al. neural LM (Day 43); Mikolov word2vec + Levy & Goldberg's analysis (43–45); Pennington GloVe (45); Bojanowski fastText (46); Blei LDA (41). Sequence models — Hochreiter & Schmidhuber LSTM (61); Sutskever seq2seq (64); Bahdanau attention (65); Lample BiLSTM-CRF (63); Holtzman nucleus sampling (66). Transformers — Vaswani (71–77); Sennrich BPE (78); Kudo SentencePiece/subword regularization (79); Devlin BERT (81); Liu RoBERTa (84); Clark ELECTRA (84); Raffel T5 (86); Reimers Sentence-BERT (87); Su RoPE (74). LLMs — Kaplan and Hoffmann scaling laws (89); Ouyang InstructGPT (94); Rafailov DPO (95); Hu LoRA + Dettmers QLoRA (96); Dao FlashAttention (92); Lewis RAG (102); Wei chain-of-thought + Wang self-consistency (99); Liu lost-in-the-middle (92). Exercise Pick one paper and reproduce a single number from its main table. Write up what replicated, what did not, and what the paper omitted that you needed. That write-up is a portfolio piece and a genuinely useful public artifact.

Five projects, five write-ups, and a clear answer to "what did you actually build?"

Turning the five projects into a portfolio

Each project needs: a one-paragraph problem statement a non-specialist can follow · the approach with one architecture diagram · results with baselines and confidence intervals · a limitations section · reproduction instructions that work · and a demo link where the project supports one. The limitations section is what separates a portfolio from a tutorial-follow — it is evidence that you evaluated your own work.

The README standard Line 1: what this does, in one sentence. Then: a screenshot or a code block showing usage · installation in one command · the headline result with its baseline · how to reproduce · the project structure · limitations · licence. If someone cannot get a result in five minutes, they will not try. Pin your best four repos on GitHub and delete or archive the rest.

Framing your work

Weak: "Built an NLP model for text classification." Strong: "Cut manual triage time 40% by shipping a hybrid retrieval + classification service handling 12k tickets/day at p95 < 300 ms; macro-F1 0.87 ± 0.02 against a 0.71 keyword baseline." Every line should carry a number, a comparison, and a consequence. If you do not have production numbers, use benchmark numbers with baselines — the discipline is what is being assessed.

NLP-specificWhy subword tokenization (78) · MLM vs causal LM and what each is good for (81, 85) · how would you fix a RAG system that returns irrelevant chunks (103) · how do you detect hallucination (105)

The system-design answer that lands Start with the requirements and the metric, propose the simplest baseline first and say what it would score, then add complexity only where you can name the failure it fixes. Interviewers are checking whether you reach for BERT before TF-IDF. Finish with monitoring and failure modes — almost nobody does, and it is what distinguishes a senior answer.
Exercise Publish a portfolio site with one technical write-up per project. Then do a mock system-design interview out loud with a timer. The gap between what you know and what you can explain under time pressure is usually large and closes fast with practice.
Day 120

🛠 Capstone & Where to Go Next

30 min

One system that uses everything, then an honest account of what this playlist did not teach you.

The capstone spec

Combine retrieval, fine-tuning, evaluation and deployment in one system on a domain you know something about. A workable shape:

  1. Ingest a real corpus with the Day 16 preprocessing library and the Day 13 cleaning funnel — report the funnel.
  2. Index with hybrid retrieval and re-ranking (Days 52, 101, 103).
  3. Fine-tune one component that measurably beats the off-the-shelf option — a domain bi-encoder (87), a classifier for routing (82), or a LoRA-adapted generator (96). Show the before/after.
  4. Ground generation with citations, an abstention path, and citation verification (102, 105).
  5. Evaluate against a committed golden set with CI gating and the four RAG metrics (103, 106).
  6. Harden with red-teaming and a tool allow-list (107).
  7. Deploy with streaming, caching, and measured p95 and cost (115).
  8. Document with a model card, a datasheet, and an honest limitations section (36, 114, 117).
The rule that makes it a capstone Every claim gets a number and a baseline. "Fine-tuning helped" is not a result; "domain bi-encoder lifted Recall@10 from 0.61 to 0.78 on the 42-query golden set, at 4 GPU-hours and +8 ms p95" is.

The 120-day arc, on one slide

What this playlist did not cover in depth

  • Distributed training — FSDP, tensor/pipeline/sequence parallelism, DeepSpeed ZeRO. Needed the moment a model does not fit on one GPU.
  • CUDA and kernel-level work — Triton, custom kernels, profiling. Where the real inference wins live.
  • Formal semantics and computational linguistics — type theory, categorial grammar, semantic parsing to logical forms.
  • Speech beyond ASR — TTS, voice conversion, audio LMs.
  • Reinforcement learning proper — we used PPO and DPO as recipes, not as RL theory.
  • Interpretability — probing beyond the basics, circuits, sparse autoencoders, activation steering.
  • Efficient long-context research — SSMs, linear attention, hybrid architectures, beyond the survey on Day 92.

How to keep learning after the series ends Build something you will use yourself, so the feedback loop is real. Reproduce one paper a quarter. Write about what you learn — the write-up is what converts reading into knowledge, and it is also your portfolio. Contribute to an open-source project you already depend on; a good bug report and a small PR are worth more than a hundred stars on a tutorial repo. And keep the habit from Day 28: every claim gets a number and a baseline.
Deliverable Ship the capstone. Record a five-minute demo. Write the retrospective: what worked, what did not, what you would do differently, and what you now want to learn. Then go build the next thing.
