Day 89

What Actually Changes at Scale

22 min

Two papers, one correction, and a budget calculator you should run before any training job.

Kaplan scaling laws (2020)

Test loss follows smooth power laws in each of parameters \(N\), data \(D\) and compute \(C\), over many orders of magnitude, provided the other two are not bottlenecks:

$$L(N)=\left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N},\qquad L(D)=\left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D},\qquad L(C)=\left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{\alpha_C}$$

with \(\alpha_N\approx0.076\), \(\alpha_D\approx0.095\), \(\alpha_C\approx0.050\). The exponents are small — a 10× increase in parameters buys roughly a 16% loss reduction — but the curves are remarkably straight on log-log axes, which is what made them useful for planning.

The Chinchilla correction (2022)

What Kaplan got wrong Kaplan's sweeps used a fixed LR schedule that was not re-tuned per model size, which systematically penalized the smaller, longer-trained runs. Hoffmann et al. re-ran with properly tuned schedules and found \(N\) and \(D\) should scale roughly equally: \(N\propto C^{0.5}\), \(D\propto C^{0.5}\). The compute-optimal ratio is about 20 tokens per parameter. GPT-3 (175B params, 300B tokens ≈ 1.7 tokens/param) was therefore badly undertrained; Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) beat it while being 2.5× smaller.
$$C\approx 6ND \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad N^{*}\approx\sqrt{\frac{C}{120}},\quad D^{*}\approx 20N^{*}$$

The factor 6 in \(C=6ND\) is the standard estimate of FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass multiply-accumulate and 4 for the backward pass.

Compute budget calculator — Chinchilla-optimal vs inference-optimal

Emergent abilities, and the counter-argument

Wei et al. reported capabilities that appear abruptly past a scale threshold — multi-step arithmetic, word unscrambling, some reasoning benchmarks. Schaeffer et al.'s rebuttal: the abruptness is largely an artifact of discontinuous metrics. Exact-match on a 5-digit addition problem is all-or-nothing, so a smoothly improving per-digit accuracy produces a step function in exact-match. Switch to a continuous metric (per-token edit distance, log-likelihood of the correct answer) and the curve becomes smooth. The honest position: capabilities do improve with scale; whether any of them are discontinuous depends on how you choose to measure, and you should report both a hard and a soft metric.

Why bigger stopped being the only lever

Inference cost dominates over a model's lifetime. A model serving 10⁹ requests pays its training cost back many times over in serving. So the field moved to: over-training smaller models, distillation, mixture-of-experts (more parameters, same active FLOPs), better data quality, and spending compute at inference time via extended reasoning instead of at training time.

Pretraining Data

24 min

The least glamorous and most consequential part of building an LLM.

The corpora

Quality filtering

  1. Heuristics — mean word length in [3,10], fraction of lines ending in punctuation, symbol-to-word ratio, fraction of duplicate lines, bullet-point fraction, stop-word presence (a page with no stop words is not prose). Gopher's rule set is the standard reference.
  2. Classifier-based — train a fast classifier (fastText) to distinguish a high-quality reference set (Wikipedia, books, upvoted links) from random crawl, then keep documents above a threshold. FineWeb-Edu instead used an LLM to score 500k samples for educational value and distilled that into a small classifier.
  3. Perplexity-based — score with a KenLM model trained on clean text; drop very high perplexity (garbage) and very low perplexity (boilerplate, repetition).

Deduplication

The most reliable single improvement. Lee et al. found web corpora contain substantial near-duplicate content, and that removing it lets models reach the same loss with less training, reduces memorization of training text by an order of magnitude, and reduces train/test overlap. Two levels: exact (suffix-array or hash on documents and long substrings) and fuzzy (MinHash LSH from Day 13, typically 5-grams, ~0.8 Jaccard threshold).

Mixtures

Typical ratios: 60–70% filtered web, 5–15% code (which improves reasoning even on non-code tasks), 5–10% books and papers, 3–5% Wikipedia, 2–5% dialogue/forum, plus maths. Sources are upsampled by epoch count rather than duplicated blindly — high-quality small corpora get 2–4 epochs while web text gets one. Muennighoff et al. found repeating data up to ~4 epochs is nearly as good as fresh data, and beyond ~16 epochs the returns vanish entirely.

Benchmark contamination If a test set appears in the training data, your evaluation is meaningless. Detect it by: n-gram overlap between benchmark items and training documents (13-gram is the common threshold); comparing the model's likelihood on the canonical ordering of a dataset's examples against shuffled orderings; or simply asking the model to complete a benchmark item from its first few words. Report the contamination check alongside the benchmark score — a benchmark number without one is uninterpretable for any model whose training data you cannot inspect.

Licensing, consent, and litigation

Live and unsettled. The live questions: whether training on copyrighted text is fair use (litigated in several jurisdictions, with outcomes still emerging); whether robots.txt and site terms bind crawlers used for model training; whether output that reproduces training text is infringement; and what obligations attach to personal data under GDPR when it has been absorbed into weights. Practical guidance: record your data provenance in a datasheet, honour opt-out signals, keep the exclusion lists you used, and prefer explicitly licensed corpora where you can. Day 117 covers this properly.

Modern LLM Architecture Choices

26 min

Every change since 2017 has been an efficiency argument. Here is the account.

Grouped-query attention

MHA gives every query head its own K and V. MQA gives all heads one shared K and V — an enormous cache saving but a measurable quality drop and training instability. GQA interpolates: \(g\) groups of query heads share one KV head.

$$\text{KV cache bytes}=2\cdot L\cdot n\cdot \underbrace{h_{kv}}_{\text{KV heads}}\cdot d_k\cdot b, \qquad h_{kv}=\frac{h}{\text{group size}}$$
KV cache calculator — the number that decides your serving cost

Mixture of experts

Replace the FFN with \(E\) parallel expert FFNs and a router that sends each token to the top-\(k\) experts (usually \(k=1\) or 2):

$$\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{TopK}(\mathbf{g})}g_i\cdot \mathrm{FFN}_i(\mathbf{x}),\qquad \mathbf{g}=\softmax(W_r\mathbf{x})$$

Total parameters scale with \(E\); active parameters per token do not. Mixtral 8×7B has ~47B total but ~13B active. The problems: experts collapse (the router sends everything to a few), so you add an auxiliary load-balancing loss \(\alpha E\sum_i f_i P_i\) where \(f_i\) is the fraction of tokens routed to expert \(i\) and \(P_i\) the mean router probability; and all experts must be resident in memory even though most are idle, so MoE saves compute, not memory.

Exercise Download an open model's config.json and account for every parameter — embeddings, per-layer attention, per-layer FFN, norms — until your total matches the published count within 1%. Then explain each config field in one sentence. This is the single best way to be sure you understand the architecture.
Day 92

Efficient Attention & Long Context

24 min

The \(O(n^2)\) wall is a memory wall, not a FLOP wall. That distinction is the whole lesson.

Where the cost actually is

Standard attention materializes the \(n\times n\) score matrix in HBM. For \(n=8192\), one head, fp16: \(8192^2\times2 = 134\) MB — times 32 heads times 32 layers, written and read several times per forward pass. GPUs are far faster at arithmetic than at moving data (an A100 does ~312 TFLOP/s but only ~2 TB/s of HBM bandwidth), so attention is IO-bound.

FlashAttention

Exact, not approximate FlashAttention computes the same result as standard attention, bit-for-bit up to floating-point associativity. It tiles Q, K and V into blocks that fit in on-chip SRAM, computes the softmax with the online (streaming) algorithm so it never needs the full row at once, and never writes the \(n\times n\) matrix to HBM at all. For the backward pass it recomputes the scores from the saved statistics rather than storing them — trading FLOPs for bandwidth, which is the right trade on modern hardware. Result: 2–4× faster and memory linear in \(n\) instead of quadratic. Use it always; there is no accuracy reason not to.
$$m^{(j)}=\max\bigl(m^{(j-1)},\ \tilde m_j\bigr),\quad \ell^{(j)}=e^{m^{(j-1)}-m^{(j)}}\ell^{(j-1)}+e^{\tilde m_j-m^{(j)}}\tilde\ell_j$$

That rescaling recursion is the online softmax: keep a running max and a running normalizer, correct previously accumulated output when the max changes.

Sparse patterns

MethodPatternComplexity
Longformersliding window + dilated window + global tokens on a few positions\(O(n\cdot w)\)
BigBirdwindow + random + global; proven to be a universal approximator and Turing complete\(O(n)\)
Mistral SWAsliding window \(w=4096\); stacking \(L\) layers gives an effective receptive field of \(L\cdot w\)\(O(n\cdot w)\)

The attention-sink finding is worth remembering: models dump excess attention mass onto the first tokens, so evicting them from the cache destroys generation quality even though those tokens carry no semantic content.

Linear attention and state-space models

$$\softmax(QK^\top)V \;\approx\; \phi(Q)\bigl(\phi(K)^\top V\bigr)$$

Reassociating the matmuls gives \(O(n d^2)\) instead of \(O(n^2 d)\). Quality has historically lagged. Mamba takes a different route: a selective state-space model whose parameters are input-dependent, giving RNN-like \(O(n)\) inference with constant memory and parallel training via a scan. Competitive with transformers at small-to-mid scale; weaker at exact recall from long context, which is why hybrid architectures (a few attention layers among many SSM layers) are the current practical answer.

Extending a trained context window

Position interpolation: to go from \(L\) to \(L'\), rescale positions by \(L/L'\) so the model sees only angles it was trained on. Needs a short fine-tune, and it compresses fine-grained position information. NTK-aware / YaRN: instead of scaling all frequencies equally, scale the RoPE base so high-frequency dimensions (which carry local ordering) are barely touched while low-frequency ones stretch. YaRN adds a temperature correction to the attention logits and reaches long contexts with ~10× less fine-tuning data than plain interpolation.

"Lost in the middle" Liu et al. showed retrieval accuracy from long contexts is U-shaped: information at the beginning and end is found reliably, information in the middle is often missed — sometimes performing worse than not providing the document at all. Practical consequences for RAG: put the most relevant chunk first and repeat key material at the end; do not assume a 128k context window means 128k of usable attention; and always test with a needle-in-a-haystack probe at your actual context length before designing around it.

Day 93

Instruction Tuning / Supervised Fine-Tuning

24 min

Base models complete text. Making one follow instructions takes surprisingly little data — if the data is good.

Why base models do not follow instructions

A base model is trained to continue text. Given "Write a poem about autumn.", the most likely continuation in the pretraining distribution might be "Write a poem about winter. Write a poem about spring." — a list of homework prompts, which is a perfectly good completion and a useless response. SFT reshapes the distribution so that instruction-shaped inputs are followed by answer-shaped outputs.

Building the dataset

SourceHowCaveat
Human-writtenAnnotators write prompt/response pairsExpensive, best quality, slow
Existing NLP datasetsReformat with templates (FLAN, Super-NaturalInstructions)Cheap and broad; responses are terse and unnatural
Self-InstructSeed with ~175 tasks, have the model generate new instructions + responses, filter by ROUGE similarityAmplifies the seed model's biases and errors
DistillationGenerate responses with a stronger modelCheck the licence. Many provider terms forbid training competing models on their outputs.
The LIMA finding Zhou et al. fine-tuned a 65B base model on 1,000 carefully curated examples and got a model competitive with ones trained on 50k+ noisy examples. Their interpretation ("superficial alignment hypothesis"): almost all knowledge is learned in pretraining, and SFT mainly teaches format and style — which subdistribution of its existing knowledge to sample from. Practical consequence: curate ruthlessly. 1,000 excellent examples beat 50,000 mediocre ones, and one bad pattern repeated 500 times will be learned faithfully.

Chat templates and loss masking

messages = [
    {"role": "system",    "content": "You are a careful technical assistant."},

Mask the loss to completions only. Training on the prompt tokens teaches the model to generate instructions, which is not the task and wastes capacity:

from trl import SFTTrainer, DataCollatorForCompletionOnlyLM

collator = DataCollatorForCompletionOnlyLM(
    response_template="<|assistant|>", tokenizer=tok)   # everything before this -> -100

SFTTrainer(model=model, train_dataset=ds, data_collator=collator,
           max_seq_length=2048, packing=False,   # packing conflicts with completion masking
           args=TrainingArguments(learning_rate=2e-5, num_train_epochs=3,
                                  per_device_train_batch_size=4,
                                  gradient_accumulation_steps=8, bf16=True,
                                  warmup_ratio=0.03, lr_scheduler_type="cosine")).train()

For multi-turn conversations, mask every assistant turn's tokens as targets and every user/system turn as ignored — a single training example can then supply several supervised turns.

What SFT costs you

Catastrophic forgetting is real: heavy SFT on a narrow instruction distribution measurably degrades general capabilities and can flatten output diversity. Mitigations: keep the LR low (1e-5 to 2e-5), 2–3 epochs maximum, mix in a small fraction of pretraining data, or use LoRA (Day 96) so the base weights are untouched. Always evaluate on a general benchmark before and after, not only on your target task.

Day 94

RLHF

28 min

Optimizing for what humans prefer, because you cannot write the loss function for "helpful".

The reward model

Humans are bad at absolute ratings and good at comparisons, so collect pairwise preferences. Under the Bradley–Terry model:

$$P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\frac{e^{r_\phi(x,y_w)}}{e^{r_\phi(x,y_w)}+e^{r_\phi(x,y_l)}}=\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)$$
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}=-\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\log\sigma\bigl(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\bigr)\Bigr]$$

Architecture: the SFT model with the LM head replaced by a scalar head reading the final token. Only differences are identified — the reward scale has an arbitrary offset, so normalize it. Annotator agreement on preference pairs is typically only 60–75%, which puts a hard ceiling on reward-model accuracy of roughly the same magnitude.

PPO for language

$$\max_{\pi_\theta}\ \E_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,y\sim\pi_\theta}\Bigl[r_\phi(x,y)\Bigr]-\beta\,\KL\bigl(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)$$
$$\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}=\E_t\Bigl[\min\bigl(\rho_t A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_t\bigr)\Bigr],\qquad \rho_t=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}$$

The mapping to language: state = prompt + tokens generated so far, action = the next token, reward = 0 at every step except the last, where it is \(r_\phi(x,y)\) minus the per-token KL penalty. Advantages are computed with GAE over the value head's estimates. Clipping stops any single update from moving the policy too far.

What happens without the KL term The policy finds whatever maximizes the reward model, which is not the same as maximizing human preference. Documented outcomes: outputs collapse to a single high-reward phrase; the model emits fluent nonsense that happens to score well; length explodes. The KL penalty anchors the policy near the SFT model, so \(\beta\) is the dial between "stays sane" and "actually improves". Monitor the KL during training — if it climbs past ~10 nats, you are off the rails.

Reward hacking and verbosity bias

Human annotators prefer longer, more confident, better-formatted answers, so reward models learn length as a proxy for quality — and PPO then exploits it. Fixes: control for length in the reward (subtract a length term, or train the RM on length-matched pairs), report win-rate against a length-matched baseline, and include an explicit "concise" instruction in some of your preference data. Related failures: sycophancy (agreeing with the user's stated view because annotators rated agreement highly) and confident hedging removal.

from trl import RewardTrainer, RewardConfig
# dataset columns: input_ids_chosen / attention_mask_chosen / input_ids_rejected / ...
trainer = RewardTrainer(model=rm, args=RewardConfig(
    output_dir="rm", per_device_train_batch_size=8, learning_rate=1e-5,
    num_train_epochs=1, max_length=1024, bf16=True), train_dataset=prefs)
trainer.train()

# afterwards, ALWAYS inspect what it rewards:
import numpy as np
scores  = np.array([score(p, r) for p, r in samples])
lengths = np.array([len(r.split()) for _, r in samples])
print("corr(reward, length) =", np.corrcoef(scores, lengths)[0,1])
# above ~0.4 and your reward model is substantially a length detector

Day 95

Preference Optimization Beyond PPO

22 min

DPO's derivation is short, elegant, and removes two entire training stages.

The derivation

Start with the KL-constrained reward objective. Its optimal policy has a closed form:

$$\pi^{*}(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\right)$$

Rearrange for the reward:

$$r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x)$$

Substitute into Bradley–Terry. Since \(Z(x)\) depends only on \(x\), it cancels in the difference:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}=-\E\left[\log\sigma\!\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}\right)\right]$$
What just happened No reward model. No sampling from the policy. No value head. No RL loop. It is a binary classification loss on preference pairs, computed with two forward passes (policy and frozen reference) over text you already have. Memory drops from four models to two; training becomes as simple as SFT. The language model is its own implicit reward model, with \(\hat r(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\).

The variants

MethodChangeUse when
IPOReplaces the log-sigmoid with a squared loss around a target marginDPO is overfitting — it can drive \(\pi(y_l)\to0\) without bound

RLAIF and Constitutional AI

Replace the human labeller with a model. Constitutional AI: (1) the model generates a response, critiques it against a written set of principles, and revises it — those revisions become SFT data; (2) the model then ranks pairs of responses according to the same principles, producing preference data for RLAIF. The point is that the values become an explicit, auditable document rather than being implicit in annotator behaviour. Lee et al. found RLAIF roughly matches RLHF on helpfulness while being dramatically cheaper. The obvious limitation: the labeller model's blind spots become the trained model's blind spots.

Online vs offline

The distribution-shift catch DPO is offline: the preference pairs were generated by some other policy, so as \(\pi_\theta\) moves away from that distribution, the pairs become less informative about the states the policy now visits. PPO is online — it samples from the current policy, so the signal stays on-distribution. This is the main reason careful comparisons still often favour PPO at the top end, and why iterative DPO (generate with the current policy, label, retrain, repeat) closes much of the gap.
from trl import DPOTrainer, DPOConfig
# dataset columns: prompt / chosen / rejected
DPOTrainer(model=policy, ref_model=None,      # None -> LoRA adapters disabled = reference
           args=DPOConfig(beta=0.1, learning_rate=5e-7, num_train_epochs=1,
                          max_length=1024, max_prompt_length=512, bf16=True,
                          loss_type="sigmoid"),   # "ipo" | "kto_pair" | "hinge"
           train_dataset=prefs, processing_class=tok).train()

Note the learning rate: 5e-7, roughly 40× smaller than SFT. DPO is extremely sensitive to it, and too high a rate collapses the model within a few hundred steps.

Day 96

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

26 min

Train 0.1% of the parameters, get most of the quality, and fit a 7B model on a gaming GPU.

Why full fine-tuning is impractical

A 7B model with AdamW needs weights (28 GB fp32) + gradients (28) + Adam \(m\) and \(v\) (56) = 112 GB before activations. Two 80 GB GPUs minimum, and you get one fine-tuned copy per task.

LoRA

The hypothesis: the update \(\Delta W\) during fine-tuning has low intrinsic rank, even though \(W\) does not. So parameterize it as a product of two thin matrices:

$$W' = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}BA,\qquad B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k},\ r\ll\min(d,k)$$

\(A\) is initialized Gaussian, \(B\) is initialized to zero — so \(\Delta W=0\) at step 0 and training starts exactly at the pretrained model. \(W_0\) is frozen. The \(\alpha/r\) scaling means changing \(r\) does not require retuning the learning rate.

LoRA / QLoRA memory calculator

QLoRA

  1. NF4 — a 4-bit "NormalFloat" data type whose quantization levels are the quantiles of a normal distribution, which is information-theoretically optimal for normally-distributed weights (which neural network weights approximately are).
  2. Double quantization — the quantization constants themselves are quantized, saving a further ~0.37 bits per parameter.
  3. Paged optimizers — optimizer state is paged to CPU memory on gradient-checkpointing spikes, preventing OOM crashes.

Gradients flow through the frozen 4-bit weights (dequantized on the fly to bf16 for the matmul) into the LoRA adapters. Dettmers et al. showed QLoRA matches 16-bit full fine-tuning quality while fine-tuning a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU.

Merging and serving

$$W_{\text{merged}}=W_0+\frac{\alpha}{r}BA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{zero added inference latency}$$

Merge for a single-task deployment. Do not merge when you want to serve many adapters — keep them separate and use S-LoRA or vLLM's multi-LoRA support to batch requests across adapters against one shared base.

from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model, prepare_model_for_kbit_training
from transformers import BitsAndBytesConfig

bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(load_in_4bit=True, bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",
                         bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
                         bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, quantization_config=bnb,
                                             device_map="auto")
model = prepare_model_for_kbit_training(model, use_gradient_checkpointing=True)
model = get_peft_model(model, LoraConfig(
    r=16, lora_alpha=32, lora_dropout=0.05, bias="none", task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
    target_modules=["q_proj","k_proj","v_proj","o_proj",
                    "gate_proj","up_proj","down_proj"]))
model.print_trainable_parameters()
Other PEFT methods, and why LoRA won Adapters insert small bottleneck layers between sublayers — they work, but add sequential depth and therefore inference latency. Prefix/prompt tuning prepends learned virtual tokens — very few parameters, but it consumes context length and is unstable to train. LoRA wins because it merges away to zero overhead.

Quantization, Distillation & Compression

24 min

Making the model smaller without making it worse — and knowing exactly how much worse.

The number formats

FormatBitsExponent / MantissaWhere
fp32328 / 23Master weights, accumulation
fp16165 / 10Older GPUs; narrow range, needs loss scaling
bf16168 / 7Training default on Ampere+. Same range as fp32, so no overflow.
fp884/3 or 5/2H100 training and inference
int8 / int48 / 4integer + scaleInference

bf16 trades precision for range, which is the right trade for training: gradients span many orders of magnitude, and you care about their scale far more than their fifth significant figure.

How quantization works

$$s=\frac{\max(|W|)}{2^{b-1}-1},\qquad W_q=\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{W}{s}\right),\qquad \hat W = s\cdot W_q$$

Per-tensor scaling uses one \(s\) for a whole matrix; per-channel uses one per row or column and is much more accurate; group-wise (e.g. 128 weights per group) is the usual compromise in 4-bit methods.

The outlier problem Dettmers et al. found that past ~6.7B parameters, a small number of feature dimensions develop activations 20–100× larger than the rest, and these outliers are essential to the model's function. A single per-tensor scale chosen to cover them destroys precision for everything else, which is why naive int8 collapses on large models. LLM.int8() decomposes the matmul: outlier dimensions in fp16, everything else in int8. GPTQ quantizes weights one column at a time, using second-order (Hessian) information to update the remaining weights to compensate for the error just introduced. AWQ observes that only ~1% of weights matter and scales the salient channels before quantizing, protecting them without mixed precision.

General rule from the literature: for a fixed memory budget, a larger model quantized to 4 bits beats a smaller model at 8 or 16 bits. 4-bit is the sweet spot; below it, returns fall off sharply.

GGUF and CPU inference

llama.cpp's GGUF format packs weights, tokenizer and metadata in one file with many quantization variants. Read the names: Q4_K_M = 4-bit, K-quant, medium — mixed bit-widths that spend more bits on the layers that matter. Q4_K_M and Q5_K_M are the usual recommendations. It runs on CPU with memory mapping, which is what makes local inference on a laptop practical.

Distillation and pruning

Response distillation trains the student on the teacher's soft output distribution (Day 84). Feature distillation also matches intermediate hidden states and attention maps (TinyBERT, MiniLM). Self-distillation uses the model's own earlier or ensembled predictions.

Unstructured pruning zeroes individual weights by magnitude — high compression ratios, but no speedup without sparse kernel support. Structured pruning removes whole heads, channels or layers — smaller ratios, but real wall-clock gains on ordinary hardware. SparseGPT and Wanda prune LLMs to ~50% sparsity in one shot without retraining, using activation statistics.

Prompt Engineering Fundamentals

24 min

The discipline is not the tricks. It is refusing to evaluate by eyeballing three examples.

What actually matters

Specificity
"Summarize this" vs "Summarize this in 3 bullet points, each under 15 words, focusing on financial risk." The second is testable; the first is not.
Structure
Delimit the sections. XML-style tags, markdown headers, or triple backticks all work; being consistent matters more than which you choose. Put the long document before the instruction for long inputs — it reduces the lost-in-the-middle effect and improves prefix caching.
Roles
System — persistent behaviour, constraints, output format. User — the task and its data. Assistant — prior turns, or a partially pre-filled response to force a format.
Output format
Show the exact shape you want, including an example. "Return JSON" is weaker than showing the JSON.
Give it an out
"If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: NOT_FOUND." Without this the model will invent something, because nothing in its training rewards silence.

Few-shot: what the examples actually do

Min et al.'s finding is the important one: randomizing the labels in your demonstrations barely hurts performance on many classification tasks. What the examples convey is the input distribution, the label space, and the output format — not the input-to-label mapping. Practical implications: choose examples that cover the range of inputs and every label; use 4–8 (returns flatten fast); order matters, so if you can, permute and average, and put the most similar example last.

The part people skip: a test harness

import json, itertools, statistics
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

def evaluate(prompt_template, examples, call, scorer, n_trials=3):
    """Score one prompt variant on a fixed labelled set, with repeats."""
    def one(ex):
        outs = [call(prompt_template.format(**ex)) for _ in range(n_trials)]
        return [scorer(o, ex["gold"]) for o in outs]
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(8) as pool:
        results = list(pool.map(one, examples))
    flat = [s for r in results for s in r]
    consistency = statistics.mean(len(set(r)) == 1 for r in results)
    return {"mean": statistics.mean(flat),
            "stdev": statistics.stdev(flat) if len(flat) > 1 else 0.0,
            "self_consistency": consistency,
            "failures": [e["id"] for e, r in zip(examples, results) if statistics.mean(r) < 0.5]}

for name, tpl in VARIANTS.items():
    print(name, evaluate(tpl, dev_set, call_model, exact_match))
Prompt sensitivity is larger than you expect Reordering few-shot examples, changing "Question:" to "Q:", or adding a trailing space can move accuracy by several points. Two consequences: (1) you cannot compare two prompts on five hand-picked examples; you need a labelled dev set of at least ~50 items and repeated trials; (2) any published prompt-technique result without variance reporting should be treated as provisional.

Day 99

Advanced Prompting & Reasoning

24 min

Spend compute at inference time instead of training time. Sometimes it works spectacularly; often it does nothing.

Chain of thought

Ask for intermediate steps before the answer. Wei et al. found this only helps at scale — below roughly 10B parameters CoT often makes things worse, because the model produces plausible-looking but invalid reasoning and then commits to its conclusion. Kojima et al.'s zero-shot version is the famous one-liner: appending "Let's think step by step." lifted GSM8K accuracy from ~18% to ~79% on a large model.

$$P(a\mid q)=\sum_{z}P(a\mid z,q)P(z\mid q)\ \approx\ P(a\mid \hat z,q)P(\hat z\mid q)$$

Read the rationale \(z\) as a latent variable. CoT samples one \(\hat z\) and conditions on it, which converts a single hard step into several easy ones — and gives the model more forward passes (more serial compute) to spend on the problem.

Self-consistency

Sample \(k\) reasoning paths at temperature ~0.7, then take the majority vote over final answers (not over reasoning text). Reliably adds 5–20 points on arithmetic and commonsense reasoning.

$$\hat a=\argmax_{a}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{1}\bigl[a_i=a\bigr]$$

Cost is linear in \(k\); gains flatten by \(k\approx20\text{–}40\). It only works when answers are comparable — exact-match numbers or labels. For free-form text you need a different aggregator, such as picking the sample with the highest average pairwise similarity to the others.

The rest of the toolkit

TechniqueMechanismCost
Least-to-mostAsk the model to decompose into subproblems, solve in order, feeding each answer forward2 calls + \(n\) subcalls; strong on compositional generalization
ReActInterleave Thought → Action → Observation, where actions call real toolsGrounds reasoning in retrieved facts; the basis of Day 104
Tree of ThoughtsGenerate several candidate steps, self-evaluate each, search with BFS/DFS and backtracking10–100× the tokens. Only worth it for genuine search problems (puzzles, planning)
PAL / PoTEmit Python instead of prose reasoning; execute it for the answerArithmetic becomes exact rather than predicted. Large gains on maths.

Self-correction without external signal mostly does not work Huang et al. found that when a model critiques and revises its own answer with no external feedback, performance often drops — the model changes correct answers to incorrect ones about as often as the reverse. Self-refine works when the critique has something real to check against: a test suite, a compiler, a retrieved document, a schema validator. Build the loop around a verifier, not around the model's opinion of itself.

Reasoning-trained models

Models trained specifically to produce long internal reasoning before answering (via RL on verifiable outcomes) change the calculus. On these, explicit "think step by step" prompting is redundant or harmful — the model already does it, and instructing it again can truncate or distort the process. The general rule: check whether your model is reasoning-trained before applying 2022-era prompting advice, and prefer stating the goal and constraints clearly over prescribing a method.

Structured Output & Tool Use

22 min

Getting a machine-readable answer out of a text generator, reliably enough to build on.

Three levels of reliability

Constrained decoding

Compile the JSON schema (or any grammar) into a finite-state machine. At each generation step, compute the set of tokens that could legally continue, and set the logits of all others to \(-\infty\) before sampling. Invalid output becomes impossible, not merely unlikely.

$$\tilde z_v = \begin{cases} z_v & v\in \mathcal{A}(s_t)\\ -\infty & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

Libraries: outlines, guidance, lm-format-enforcer, XGrammar; vLLM and llama.cpp both expose grammar modes. One caveat worth knowing: heavy constraints can hurt content quality, because forcing the model onto a rigid path prevents it from "thinking" in prose first. The usual fix is a two-field schema — a free-text reasoning field before the structured fields.

from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, field_validator
from typing import Literal
import json

class Extraction(BaseModel):
    reasoning: str = Field(description="Brief justification. Fill this FIRST.")
    company:   str
    amount_usd: float | None
    sentiment: Literal["positive", "negative", "neutral"]
    confidence: float = Field(ge=0.0, le=1.0)

    @field_validator("company")
    @classmethod
    def not_placeholder(cls, v):
        if v.strip().lower() in {"", "n/a", "unknown", "none"}:
            raise ValueError("company must be an actual name or the record dropped")
        return v.strip()

def extract(text, call, max_retries=3):
    schema = json.dumps(Extraction.model_json_schema(), indent=2)
    prompt = f"Extract into JSON matching this schema:\n{schema}\n\nTEXT:\n{text}"
    err = None
    for attempt in range(max_retries):
        raw = call(prompt if err is None else
                   prompt + f"\n\nYour previous output failed validation:\n{err}\nFix it.")
        try:
            return Extraction.model_validate_json(raw)
        except Exception as e:
            err = str(e)[:400]
    raise ValueError(f"failed after {max_retries} attempts: {err}")

Tool calling

The protocol is the same across providers: you send a list of tool schemas; the model returns a structured call rather than text; you execute it and return the result as a message; the model continues. Parallel calls are supported by most current models, so independent lookups happen in one round trip.

Tool descriptions are prompts The model chooses tools based entirely on their name, description and parameter docs. Write them for a competent stranger: say what the tool does, when to use it, when not to, what it returns, and what happens on failure. A vague description is the single most common cause of wrong tool selection — far more common than model capability limits.

Handling hallucinated arguments

Models invent parameter values, especially IDs and dates. Defences: validate every argument against a schema before execution; make tools return actionable errors ("user_id 'abc' not found; call search_users first") rather than stack traces; require a lookup step for anything the model could not know; and cap retries so a bad call cannot loop forever.

When not to use an LLM If the task is a fixed-label classification with more than ~200 labelled examples, a fine-tuned encoder (Day 82) is typically cheaper by two or three orders of magnitude, faster by one or two, deterministic, and often more accurate. Use the LLM to bootstrap labels, then distil into the small model. Measure both before committing.

ANN indexes

$$\text{recall@k}=\frac{|\text{ANN top-}k\ \cap\ \text{exact top-}k|}{k}$$
Flat
Exhaustive. 100% recall, \(O(N)\) per query. Perfectly fine below ~100k vectors — and it is the ground truth you measure the others against.
HNSW
A multi-layer navigable small-world graph; search descends from a sparse top layer to a dense bottom one. Query \(O(\log N)\). Knobs: M (edges per node, 16–64) and efConstruction / efSearch (candidate list size — the direct recall/latency dial). High memory, excellent recall, slow to build.
IVF-PQ
Cluster into nlist Voronoi cells, search nprobe of them; compress vectors with product quantization (split into \(m\) subvectors, quantize each to 256 centroids). 10–50× memory reduction at some recall cost. The choice at 100M+ vectors.

Day 102

RAG I: Building the Core Pipeline

28 min

Six stages, and a failure mode at every one of them.

Why RAG exists

Four reasons, in rough order of importance: private data the model was never trained on; freshness beyond the training cutoff; citations, so a human can verify a claim; and cost, since retrieving 2k relevant tokens is cheaper than fine-tuning or than stuffing 100k tokens of context. Note that reducing hallucination is a consequence of grounding, not a guarantee — a model can still misread a correctly retrieved document.

The prompt

SYSTEM = """Answer using ONLY the numbered context below.
Cite every claim with the source number in square brackets, like [2].
If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly: NOT_FOUND
Do not use prior knowledge. Do not speculate."""

def build_prompt(question, chunks):
    ctx = "\n\n".join(
        f"[{i+1}] source: {c.source} · section: {c.section}\n{c.text}"
        for i, c in enumerate(chunks))
    # most relevant FIRST and the question repeated at the END (lost-in-the-middle, Day 92)
    return [{"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM},
            {"role": "user", "content": f"CONTEXT\n{ctx}\n\nQUESTION: {question}"}]

Context budgeting

$$n_{\text{chunks}}=\left\lfloor\frac{L_{\max}-L_{\text{system}}-L_{\text{question}}-L_{\text{answer}}}{L_{\text{chunk}}+L_{\text{metadata}}}\right\rfloor$$

Reserve output tokens explicitly, or a long retrieval silently truncates the answer. Count tokens with the model's own tokenizer, never with len(text)/4.

Handling "not found"

Three layers, all needed: a score threshold (if the best retrieval score is below \(\tau\), do not call the model at all); the explicit NOT_FOUND instruction; and a post-check that every citation index actually exists and that cited text supports the sentence. Then design the UI to show "I could not find this in your documents" as a first-class, non-embarrassing outcome — otherwise every incentive pushes toward answering anyway.

RAG II: Making It Actually Good

The gap between a weekend demo and something people trust is entirely in this lesson.

Re-ranking — the highest-return single change

Retrieve 50 with the cheap bi-encoder, re-score all 50 with a cross-encoder, keep the top 5. The cross-encoder sees query and passage together with full attention, so it can judge relevance rather than similarity. Typical gain: +10–20% NDCG@10 for ~50–200 ms of added latency.

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder
ce = CrossEncoder("BAAI/bge-reranker-base", max_length=512)

def retrieve(query, k=5, pool=50):
    cands = hybrid_search(query, k=pool)                    # Day 52
    scores = ce.predict([(query, c.text) for c in cands])
    ranked = [c for _, c in sorted(zip(scores, cands), key=lambda p: -p[0])]
    return ranked[:k], sorted(scores, reverse=True)[:k]

HyDE's logic is worth stating explicitly: query and passage live in different distributions (questions vs statements), so query-passage cosine is comparing across a domain gap. Generating a pseudo-answer moves the query into the passage distribution. It fails when the model has no idea about the topic — the hallucinated answer then retrieves the wrong region.

Multi-hop and agentic retrieval

"Which university did the author of the 2019 report attend?" needs two retrievals in sequence: find the author, then find their biography. Implement as a loop: retrieve → check sufficiency → generate a follow-up query → retrieve again, with a hard step budget. GraphRAG instead pre-extracts an entity/relation graph, clusters it into communities, and summarizes each — which answers global questions ("what are the main themes?") that chunk retrieval structurally cannot.

LLM Agents

26 min

A loop, some tools, and a great deal of error handling. The realistic assessment matters more than the architecture.

The loop

$$s_{t+1}=f\bigl(s_t,\ \underbrace{\pi(s_t)}_{\text{action}},\ \underbrace{\mathrm{env}(a_t)}_{\text{observation}}\bigr)\quad\text{until } \mathrm{done}(s_t) \text{ or } t>T_{\max}$$

Observe → plan → act → reflect. The state is the conversation history plus a scratchpad; the policy is the model; the environment is your tools. Everything hard is in the details of that env function.

class Agent:
    def __init__(self, tools, max_steps=10, max_cost_usd=0.50):
        self.tools = {t.name: t for t in tools}
        self.max_steps, self.max_cost = max_steps, max_cost_usd

    def run(self, task):
        msgs = [{"role": "user", "content": task}]
        cost, seen = 0.0, set()
        for step in range(self.max_steps):
            resp = call(msgs, tools=[t.schema for t in self.tools.values()])
            cost += resp.cost
            if cost > self.max_cost:
                return {"status": "budget_exceeded", "spent": cost, "partial": msgs}
            if not resp.tool_calls:
                return {"status": "ok", "answer": resp.content, "steps": step, "cost": cost}
            msgs.append(resp.message)
            for call_ in resp.tool_calls:
                key = (call_.name, json.dumps(call_.arguments, sort_keys=True))
                if key in seen:                       # loop detector
                    result = "You already made this exact call. Try a different approach."
                else:
                    seen.add(key)
                    result = self._execute(call_)
                msgs.append({"role": "tool", "tool_call_id": call_.id, "content": result})
        return {"status": "max_steps", "partial": msgs, "cost": cost}

    def _execute(self, call_):
        tool = self.tools.get(call_.name)
        if tool is None:
            return f"No tool named {call_.name}. Available: {list(self.tools)}"
        try:
            return json.dumps(tool.run(**call_.arguments))[:4000]   # truncate: context is finite
        except ValidationError as e:
            return f"Invalid arguments: {e}. Fix them and retry."
        except Exception as e:
            return f"Tool failed: {type(e).__name__}: {e}. Try a different approach."
Four guardrails that are not optional A step budget, a cost budget, a loop detector on repeated identical calls, and truncated tool output. Without all four, a single agent run can consume an unbounded amount of money on a task it will never finish.

Memory

Short-term is the context window: keep recent turns verbatim, summarize older ones, and always keep the original task statement pinned (agents drift off-task as history grows). Long-term is a vector store of past episodes, plus a structured store for facts and preferences. Retrieve into context at the start of each run rather than trying to keep everything resident.

Multi-agent patterns

Generator–criticOne produces, one reviews against criteriaThe critic has a real checklist or a verifier

The honest assessment Multi-agent systems multiply cost and latency and add failure modes (agents talking past each other, error propagation, deadlock). They help when subtasks genuinely need different tools or different context — not merely because a task "has several parts". If you can write the steps down in order, write them down in order: a deterministic pipeline with an LLM at each node is cheaper, faster, debuggable and usually more accurate. Reserve agency for cases where the next step genuinely depends on what the previous step found.

Sandboxing

Any tool that executes code, writes files, sends messages or spends money needs: a container or VM with no host access, network egress allow-listing, read-only mounts by default, resource limits, and a human approval gate for irreversible actions. Treat tool output as untrusted input — it can contain prompt injection (Day 107).

Hallucination, Grounding & Uncertainty

22 min

The model was trained to produce likely text, not true text. Everything follows from that.

The taxonomy

AxisTypeExample
SourceIntrinsicContradicts the provided context
ExtrinsicAdds content not in the context — may even be true, but unverifiable from what you gave it
TargetFactualityContradicts the world
FaithfulnessContradicts the source

For RAG, faithfulness is the metric you can actually compute, because you have the source. Factuality needs an external ground truth you usually do not have.

Why next-token training produces confident errors

Three compounding causes. (1) The objective rewards plausible continuations; a fluent wrong answer and a fluent right answer are locally indistinguishable to the loss. (2) There is no representation of "I do not know" unless the training data contained refusals in the same contexts — and RLHF often penalizes hedging because annotators preferred confident answers. (3) Once a wrong token is generated, everything after it is conditioned on the error and the model commits.

Calibration

$$\mathrm{ECE}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\frac{|B_m|}{N}\bigl|\mathrm{acc}(B_m)-\mathrm{conf}(B_m)\bigr|$$

Base models are reasonably calibrated on multiple-choice log-probabilities. RLHF degrades this measurably. And verbalized confidence ("I'm 90% sure") is much worse calibrated than the underlying token probabilities — models say 90% for almost everything. If you need a confidence number, derive it from log-probabilities or from sampling agreement, not from asking.

Detection

def selfcheck(question, n=5, ctx=None):
    """SelfCheckGPT: sample several times; inconsistency implies fabrication."""
    answers = [call(question, ctx=ctx, temperature=0.8) for _ in range(n)]
    main    = call(question, ctx=ctx, temperature=0.0)
    scores  = []
    for claim in split_claims(main):
        # what fraction of independent samples ENTAIL this claim?
        support = sum(nli(premise=a, hypothesis=claim) == "entailment" for a in answers)
        scores.append(support / n)
    return main, list(zip(split_claims(main), scores))

def verify_citations(answer, chunks, threshold=0.5):
    """Every cited sentence must be entailed by the chunk it cites."""
    bad = []
    for sent, cite_ids in sentences_with_citations(answer):
        if not cite_ids:
            bad.append((sent, "uncited claim")); continue
        prem = "\n".join(chunks[i].text for i in cite_ids)
        if nli_score(premise=prem, hypothesis=sent) < threshold:
            bad.append((sent, f"not supported by {cite_ids}"))
    return bad

The SelfCheckGPT intuition: knowledge the model actually has is stable across samples; fabrications vary. It needs no external resource, costs \(n\) extra generations, and detects the confident-fabrication case reasonably well.

Grounding techniques

  • Quote-then-answer — require verbatim supporting quotes before the answer, then verify each quote appears in the source with exact string matching. Catches a large share of extrinsic hallucination for one cheap check.
  • Sentence-level citations — cite per sentence rather than per answer, so verification is localized.
  • Abstention — an explicit NOT_FOUND path plus a retrieval score threshold (Day 102).
  • NLI fact-checking — run a trained NLI model over (context, claim) pairs and flag anything not entailed.

$$\mathrm{pass}@k = \E_{\text{problems}}\left[1-\frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right]$$

where \(n\) samples are generated and \(c\) pass. Use this unbiased estimator rather than generating exactly \(k\) — it has far lower variance.

Three biases you must control for Position bias — judges prefer the first option. Fix: run both orders and require consistency, or average. Verbosity bias — judges prefer longer answers regardless of content. Fix: report length alongside win-rate, and include length-matched pairs. Self-preference — a judge prefers text from its own family. Fix: use a different model family as judge, or an ensemble. Also: always validate the judge against ~100 human labels and report their agreement. An unvalidated judge is an opinion, not a measurement.
JUDGE = """Compare two answers to the same question against the provided context.
Judge ONLY on: factual support from the context, completeness, and directness.
Explicitly IGNORE length, formatting, and confidence of tone.

Question: {q}
Context: {ctx}
Answer A: {a}
Answer B: {b}

Respond with JSON: {{"reasoning": "...", "winner": "A"|"B"|"tie"}}"""

def judge_pair(q, ctx, a, b, call):
    r1 = json.loads(call(JUDGE.format(q=q, ctx=ctx, a=a, b=b)))
    r2 = json.loads(call(JUDGE.format(q=q, ctx=ctx, a=b, b=a)))   # swapped
    flip = {"A": "B", "B": "A", "tie": "tie"}
    if r1["winner"] != flip[r2["winner"]]:
        return "inconsistent"                 # position bias detected — count these
    return r1["winner"]

Your own eval set

The minimum viable eval 50–200 items drawn from real usage, not invented. Each with: input, reference output or a checkable assertion, and a category tag. Include: the common happy path, known hard cases, adversarial inputs, out-of-scope requests that should be refused, and a few that should return "not found". Score with a mix of deterministic checks (regex, schema validity, exact match, unit tests) and a validated judge. Run it in CI on every prompt or model change, and fail the build on regression. This file is the single most valuable artifact in an LLM application repo.

Cost and latency are first-class metrics

$$\text{cost/request}=\frac{n_{\text{in}}\cdot p_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}\cdot p_{\text{out}}}{10^6},\qquad \text{latency}=\underbrace{\mathrm{TTFT}}_{\text{prefill}}+n_{\text{out}}\cdot\underbrace{\mathrm{TPOT}}_{\text{decode}}$$

Report p50/p95/p99, not the mean. Time-to-first-token dominates perceived responsiveness for streaming UIs; time-per-output-token dominates total duration for long answers. A model that is 2 points better and 5× more expensive is a business decision, and you should present it as one.

Day 107

Safety, Alignment & Red-Teaming

24 min

Prompt injection is the central unsolved security problem of every RAG and agent system you will build.

The alignment problem, stated plainly

We can specify what we can measure, and we optimize what we specify. The gap between "what we measured" and "what we wanted" is where the failures live — reward hacking (Day 94), sycophancy, specification gaming. This is an engineering problem before it is a philosophical one: your preference data is your specification, and it is always incomplete.

Harm taxonomy

Misuse — weapons, cyber-attacks, fraud, targeted harassment, CSAM. Malfunction — false medical or legal information, discriminatory outputs, confident errors in high-stakes settings. Systemic — labour effects, information ecosystem degradation, concentration of capability. Different mitigations for each; conflating them produces bad policy and bad engineering.

Why jailbreaks work (conceptually)

Safety training is a thin behavioural layer over a model that has the underlying capability. Broadly, jailbreak families exploit: distribution shift (the refusal behaviour was trained on prompts that look unlike the attack — unusual formats, encodings, languages, or fictional framings); objective conflict (helpfulness and harmlessness training pull in different directions and the attack maximizes the former); and long-context dilution (safety conditioning weakens far from the system prompt). Automated methods search the prompt space directly for suffixes that flip the refusal.

What follows from that Refusal behaviour is not a property of the weights in the way capability is; it is a learned surface behaviour. So (1) safety fine-tuning can be substantially undone by a small amount of contrary fine-tuning, which matters if you accept user-supplied adapters; (2) defences that pattern-match on the input will always be one reformulation behind; and (3) the durable controls are architectural — what the system is permitted to do, not what it can be persuaded to say.

Prompt injection — the one that matters for you

Say the limitation out loud There is currently no complete defence against indirect prompt injection. Published guidance from every major vendor and from OWASP's LLM Top 10 says the same. Design as if injection will succeed: assume the model can be made to attempt any action its tools permit, and make sure the permitted set is one you can live with. Then document the residual risk in your model card.

Incremental ingestion

def sync(source, store, index):
    seen = set()
    for doc in source.list():
        seen.add(doc.id)
        h = hashlib.sha256(doc.bytes).hexdigest()
        if store.hash_of(doc.id) == h:
            continue                                  # unchanged: skip embedding entirely
        store.delete_chunks(doc.id); index.remove_ids(store.chunk_ids(doc.id))
        chunks = enrich(chunk(parse(doc)), title=doc.title, section_path=True)
        vecs   = embed([c.text for c in chunks])
        ids    = store.upsert(doc.id, chunks, h)
        index.add_with_ids(vecs, ids)
    for gone in store.doc_ids() - seen:               # deletions matter for correctness
        index.remove_ids(store.chunk_ids(gone)); store.delete_doc(gone)
