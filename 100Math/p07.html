Phase 7 · Days 82–91 · Lessons 96–108

Probability

Phase 7 builds probability from Kolmogorov's three axioms up to the limit theorems, deriving every distribution, expectation and inequality rather than memorising it. It reuses the sets and counting of Phase 2, the integrals of Phases 4–5 and the eigenvectors of Phase 3, and it is the language in which Phase 8 states every estimator, interval and test.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Derive complement rules, inclusion–exclusion and Bayes' theorem from the axioms, and compute a positive predictive value from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence.
  • State the story, pmf or density, mean and variance of the standard discrete and continuous distributions, and derive the Poisson limit of the binomial.
  • Find the distribution of a transformed variable by the CDF method, the Jacobian formula or convolution, and approximate its variance by the delta method.
  • Use linearity, indicators, the tower property and the law of total variance to compute expectations without finding full distributions.
  • Compute a covariance matrix, prove it is positive semidefinite, and derive the conditional distribution of a multivariate Gaussian with the Schur complement.
  • Choose and apply Markov, Chebyshev, Jensen, the union bound or Hoeffding, and size a sample from a concentration bound.
  • Prove the weak law of large numbers, apply the CLT with a continuity correction, and find a Markov chain's stationary distribution as a left eigenvector.

L96 · DAY 82

Sample Spaces, Events & the Axioms of Probability

34m video45m practice

Probability is a function defined on sets. Fix a sample space, decide which subsets count as events, impose Kolmogorov's three axioms, and every rule you use afterwards (complements, inclusion–exclusion, the birthday computation) is a theorem you can prove, not a convention to memorise.

Experiments, outcomes and events

A random experiment has a set \(\Omega\) of possible outcomes \(\omega\), the sample space: finite (two dice, \(\{1,\dots,6\}^2\), 36 outcomes), countably infinite (tosses until the first head, \(\{1, 2, \dots\}\)) or uncountable (a point in \([0,1]^2\)). Choosing \(\Omega\) is a modelling decision: recording only the sum of two dice, \(\{2, \dots, 12\}\), throws away the equally-likely structure of \(\{1,\dots,6\}^2\).

An event is a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\); it happens when the outcome lands in it. Every set operation of L25 now has a meaning:

In wordsAs sets

Which subsets get a probability: σ-algebras

Definition · σ-algebra

A collection \(\mathcal F\) of subsets of \(\Omega\) with (i) \(\Omega \in \mathcal F\); (ii) \(A \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow A^c \in \mathcal F\); (iii) \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow \bigcup_i A_i \in \mathcal F\). By De Morgan it is closed under countable intersections too.

For countable \(\Omega\), take all subsets. For \(\Omega = [0,1)\) we want \(P(\text{interval}) = \text{length}\), invariant under shifts mod 1, and countably additive. Vitali showed this is impossible on all subsets: pick one point from each class of \(x \sim y \iff x - y \in \Q\) to form \(V\). The shifts \(V + q \pmod 1\), \(q \in \Q \cap [0,1)\), are countably many disjoint copies of \(V\) covering \([0,1)\), so \(1 = m + m + \cdots\) for one number \(m\), which fails for \(m = 0\) and for \(m > 0\). The fix is to use only the Borel sets, generated by intervals. L121 returns to this.

Kolmogorov's axioms and their consequences

Definition · Probability measure

\(P : \mathcal F \to \R\) with (1) \(P(A) \ge 0\); (2) \(P(\Omega) = 1\); (3) countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint \(A_1, A_2, \dots\), \(P\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big) = \sum_i P(A_i)\).

Theorem · Consequences

(a) \(P(\varnothing) = 0\). (b) Finite additivity. (c) \(P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)\). (d) \(A \subseteq B \Rightarrow P(B \setminus A) = P(B) - P(A)\), so \(P(A) \le P(B)\). (e) \(0 \le P(A) \le 1\). (f) \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\).

Proof

(a) \(A_1 = \Omega\), \(A_i = \varnothing\) for \(i \ge 2\) are disjoint with union \(\Omega\), so \(1 = 1 + \sum_{i \ge 2} P(\varnothing)\), which forces \(P(\varnothing) = 0\). (b) Pad \(A_1, \dots, A_n\) with empty sets. (c) \(A \cup A^c = \Omega\) disjointly. (d) \(B = A \cup (B \setminus A)\) disjointly, and \(P(B \setminus A) \ge 0\). (e) Take \(B = \Omega\) in (d). (f) is today's Prove it.

Induction on (f) gives inclusion–exclusion, the twin of the counting formula in L31:

$$P\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\Big) = \sum_i P(A_i) - \sum_{i<j} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \sum_{i<j<k} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots + (-1)^{n+1} P\Big(\bigcap_{i=1}^n A_i\Big).$$

The classical and geometric models

Classical: \(\Omega\) finite with equally likely outcomes, \(P(A) = |A| / |\Omega|\). It satisfies the axioms, and it reduces probability to counting with the tools of L30 and L31. Geometric: a uniform point in a region of finite area, \(P(A) = \operatorname{area}(A)/\operatorname{area}(\Omega)\). Points and segments have area 0, so they have probability 0 without being impossible.

Worked example · Birthdays among 23 people

Birthdays uniform on 365 days, independent. \(|\Omega| = 365^{23}\); the complement “all different” has \(365 \cdot 364 \cdots 343\) outcomes, so

$$P(\text{no match}) = \prod_{k=0}^{22}\Big(1 - \frac{k}{365}\Big) \approx 0.4927, \qquad P(\text{some shared birthday}) \approx 0.5073.$$

The reason: \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\) pairs, and \(1 - x \approx e^{-x}\) gives \(P(\text{no match}) \approx e^{-253/365} \approx e^{-0.693} \approx \tfrac12\).

Worked example · Point in a square

\((X, Y)\) uniform in \([-1, 1]^2\) (area 4); the inscribed disc has area \(\pi\), so \(P = \pi/4 \approx 0.785\). Four times the fraction of random points inside estimates \(\pi\).

Worked example · Three-set inclusion–exclusion

\(N\) is uniform on \(\{1, \dots, 1000\}\); \(A_d\) = “\(d\) divides \(N\)”, \(|A_d| = \lfloor 1000/d \rfloor\), and \(A_2 \cap A_3 = A_6\) etc. The probability that 2, 3 or 5 divides \(N\) is

$$\frac{500 + 333 + 200 - 166 - 100 - 66 + 33}{1000} = 0.734.$$

Odds, and what probability means

Odds are \(o = p/(1-p)\), with \(p = o/(1+o)\): “3 to 1 against” is odds \(1/3\), so \(p = 1/4\). Odds are the currency of Bayes' rule (L97), and log-odds are the linear predictor in logistic regression (L116).

  • Frequentist: long-run relative frequency in repeated trials; the law of large numbers (L108) connects the axioms to it.
  • Bayesian: degree of belief given information, updated by Bayes' rule; it applies to one-off events and unknown parameters.
  • Subjective (de Finetti): your price for a ticket paying 1 if \(A\) occurs. Prices that break the axioms let an opponent build a Dutch book, bets you lose whatever happens.

The axioms say how probabilities combine, not what they mean, so every theorem of this phase holds under every reading. The readings part ways in Phase 8, over whether a parameter may carry a probability.

Common errors
  • Adding overlapping events. \(P(\text{a six on either die}) = \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 - \tfrac1{36} = \tfrac{11}{36}\), not \(\tfrac13\).
  • False equal likelihood. On \(\{2, \dots, 12\}\), \(P(7) = 6/36\), not \(1/11\).
  • Disjoint ≠ independent. Disjoint events of positive probability are never independent: one happening rules out the other.
  • The wrong birthday question. “Someone shares my birthday” has probability \(1 - (364/365)^{22} \approx 0.059\).

L97 · DAY 83

Conditional Probability, Independence & Bayes' Theorem

38m video50m practiceheavy lesson

Conditioning on \(B\) means discarding every outcome outside \(B\) and rescaling what is left so it sums to 1. Bayes' theorem is that rescaling written two ways, and nearly every mistake made with it comes from forgetting how small the prior was.

Conditional probability is renormalisation

Definition

For \(P(B) > 0\), \(\displaystyle P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\). The new sample space is \(B\); \(A\) survives only through \(A \cap B\).

Theorem · \(P(\cdot \mid B)\) is a probability measure

It is non-negative; \(P(\Omega \mid B) = P(B)/P(B) = 1\); and for disjoint \(A_i\) the sets \(A_i \cap B\) are disjoint, so \(P(\bigcup A_i \mid B) = \sum P(A_i \cap B)/P(B) = \sum P(A_i \mid B)\). Every theorem of L96 therefore holds conditionally.

Rearranged, the definition is the multiplication rule \(P(A \cap B) = P(B)\,P(A \mid B)\), and by induction the chain rule

$$P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1)\,P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}).$$

Three aces in a row from a shuffled deck: \(\tfrac4{52} \cdot \tfrac3{51} \cdot \tfrac2{50} = \tfrac1{5525}\). A tree diagram is the chain rule drawn: multiply along a path, add across the leaves you want.

Total probability and Bayes' theorem

Theorem · Total probability and Bayes

If \(B_1, \dots, B_n\) partition \(\Omega\) with every \(P(B_i) > 0\), then

$$P(A) = \sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i), \qquad P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}.$$
Proof

\(A = \bigcup_i (A \cap B_i)\) is a disjoint union, so \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A \cap B_i) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i) P(B_i)\). For Bayes, write \(P(A \cap B_j)\) both ways: \(P(B_j \mid A) P(A) = P(A \mid B_j) P(B_j)\), divide by \(P(A)\), and expand \(P(A)\) by total probability.

With hypothesis \(H\) and data \(D\): \(P(H)\) is the prior, \(P(D \mid H)\) the likelihood, \(P(D)\) the evidence, \(P(H \mid D)\) the posterior. Dividing Bayes for \(H\) by Bayes for \(H^c\) cancels the evidence and gives the odds form:

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid D)}{P(H^c \mid D)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(D \mid H)}{P(D \mid H^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)}}_{\text{prior odds}}.$$

If \(D_1, D_2\) are conditionally independent given \(H\) and given \(H^c\), updating on both multiplies the prior odds by both likelihood ratios: yesterday's posterior is today's prior.

Base rates: the disease test

Worked example · Disease testing

Prevalence \(P(D) = 0.01\), sensitivity \(P(+ \mid D) = 0.99\), specificity \(P(- \mid D^c) = 0.95\). By total probability \(P(+) = 0.99 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99 = 0.0594\), so

$$\text{PPV} = P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} = \frac16 \approx 0.167, \qquad \text{NPV} = P(D^c \mid -) = \frac{0.95 \cdot 0.99}{0.95 \cdot 0.99 + 0.01 \cdot 0.01} \approx 0.9999.$$

Worked example · Two-step sequential update

The likelihood ratio of a positive is \(0.99/0.05 = 19.8\). Prior odds \(1:99\). After one positive, odds \(19.8/99 = 0.2\), so \(p = 0.2/1.2 = 1/6\), matching the PPV. After a second positive from an independent test, odds \(0.2 \times 19.8 = 3.96\) and \(p = 3.96/4.96 \approx 0.80\). This assumes the two results are conditionally independent given disease status; rerunning the same sample usually violates it.

Independence: pairwise, mutual, conditional

Definition

\(A, B\) are independent if \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)\) (equivalently \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) when \(P(B) > 0\)). \(A_1, \dots, A_n\) are mutually independent if \(P(\bigcap_{i \in S} A_i) = \prod_{i \in S} P(A_i)\) for every \(S \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}\). \(A, B\) are conditionally independent given \(C\) if \(P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) P(B \mid C)\).

Pairwise but not mutual. Toss two fair coins; \(A\) = first is heads, \(B\) = second is heads, \(C\) = the two agree. Each has probability \(\tfrac12\) and each pairwise intersection has \(\tfrac14\), but \(A \cap B \cap C = \{HH\}\) has \(\tfrac14 \ne \tfrac18\).

Conditional independence and independence are unrelated in both directions. (i) Choose one of two coins at random, with \(P(H) = 0.9\) or \(0.1\), and flip it twice. Given the coin the flips are independent, yet \(P(H_1 \cap H_2) = \tfrac12(0.81 + 0.01) = 0.41 \ne 0.25 = P(H_1)P(H_2)\): the first flip tells you about the coin. (ii) Two independent fair coins become dependent given \(C\) = “exactly one head”: \(P(H_1 \mid C) = P(H_2 \mid C) = \tfrac12\) but \(P(H_1 \cap H_2 \mid C) = 0\). This is Berkson's paradox, revisited in L117.

Simpson's paradox and Monty Hall

In the kidney-stone data usually quoted from Charig et al. (1986), treatment A beat B on small stones (81/87 = 93% vs 234/270 = 87%) and on large stones (192/263 = 73% vs 55/80 = 69%), yet lost overall (273/350 = 78% vs 289/350 = 83%). By total probability, each overall rate is \(\sum_s P(\text{success} \mid s, T)\,P(s \mid T)\), and A was given mostly the hard large stones. Different weights can reverse a comparison.

Worked example · Monty Hall by conditioning

You pick door 1. The car is behind door \(C\), uniform on \(\{1,2,3\}\). The host opens a goat door you did not pick, choosing at random if he has two options. Let \(H_3\) = “host opens 3”. Then \(P(H_3 \mid C{=}1) = \tfrac12\), \(P(H_3 \mid C{=}2) = 1\), \(P(H_3 \mid C{=}3) = 0\), so \(P(H_3) = \tfrac13(\tfrac12 + 1 + 0) = \tfrac12\) and

$$P(C = 2 \mid H_3) = \frac{1 \cdot \tfrac13}{\tfrac12} = \frac23, \qquad P(C = 1 \mid H_3) = \frac{\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13}{\tfrac12} = \frac13.$$

Switching wins with probability \(\tfrac23\). The host's rule enters through the likelihoods; change the rule and the answer changes.

Common errors
  • Transposing the conditional (the prosecutor's fallacy): \(P(+ \mid D) = 0.99\) says nothing about \(P(D \mid +)\) until you supply the prior.
  • Base-rate neglect: quoting accuracy without prevalence.
  • Pairwise taken for mutual independence, or disjoint taken for independent.
  • Multiplying likelihood ratios for correlated evidence, such as two witnesses who talked to each other.
  • “Two doors left, so 50–50” ignores that the host's choice depends on where the car is.

  1. Prove it. If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, so are \(A\) and \(B^c\). Hint: split \(A\) along \(B\).
    Proof sketch

    \(A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B^c)\) disjointly, so \(P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A)P(B) = P(A)(1 - P(B)) = P(A)P(B^c)\). Applying it twice shows \(A^c, B^c\) are independent too.

    \(P(+) = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094\); PPV \(= 0.00099/0.05094 \approx 0.019\), about 2%.

  3. Three cards: red/red, red/white, white/white. One is drawn at random and a random face shows red. Find the probability the other face is red.
    Answer

    \(\tfrac23\): of the three red faces equally likely to be showing, two belong to the red/red card.

  4. Show that \(P(A \mid B) > P(A)\) implies \(P(B \mid A) > P(B)\) (when both conditionals are defined).

    Both say \(P(A \cap B) > P(A) P(B)\).

    Answer

    \(P(BB)/P(\text{at least one } B) = \tfrac14 / \tfrac34 = \tfrac13\).

L98 · DAY 83

Discrete Random Variables & PMFs

30m video40m practiceheavy lesson

A random variable is not a variable: it is a function \(X : \Omega \to \R\) that turns outcomes into numbers. Its pmf and its CDF are two complete descriptions of the probabilities it induces on \(\R\), and indicator variables turn many expectations into one-line computations.

Random variables are functions

Definition

A random variable is a function \(X : \Omega \to \R\) such that \(\{X \le x\} = \{\omega : X(\omega) \le x\}\) is an event for every \(x\) (the measurability condition of L121). For two dice, \(\Omega = \{1,\dots,6\}^2\) and \(X(\omega_1, \omega_2) = \omega_1 + \omega_2\); the event \(\{X = 4\}\) is the subset \(\{(1,3), (2,2), (3,1)\}\) of \(\Omega\).

\(X\) is discrete if it takes countably many values, continuous if it has a density (L100), and mixed otherwise, such as a censored waiting time with an atom at the cut-off. The support of a discrete \(X\) is the set of values it takes with positive probability.

The probability mass function

Definition · PMF

\(p_X(x) = P(X = x)\). It satisfies (i) \(p_X(x) \ge 0\) and (ii) \(\sum_{x} p_X(x) = 1\), the sum over the support. Conversely, any function with (i) and (ii) on a countable set is the pmf of some random variable. Probabilities of sets follow by additivity: \(P(X \in A) = \sum_{x \in A} p_X(x)\).

The cumulative distribution function

Definition · CDF

\(F_X(x) = P(X \le x)\) for every real \(x\), not only the support. For a discrete \(X\) it is a step function that jumps by \(p_X(x)\) at each support point and is flat in between.

Theorem · Properties of every CDF

(i) \(F\) is non-decreasing; (ii) \(\lim_{x \to -\infty} F(x) = 0\) and \(\lim_{x \to \infty} F(x) = 1\); (iii) \(F\) is right-continuous: \(\lim_{t \downarrow x} F(t) = F(x)\). Conversely, every function with (i)–(iii) is the CDF of some random variable.

The proof (today's Prove it) needs one tool that comes straight from countable additivity.

Lemma · Continuity of probability

If \(A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \cdots\) then \(P(\bigcup_n A_n) = \lim_n P(A_n)\). If \(A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \cdots\) then \(P(\bigcap_n A_n) = \lim_n P(A_n)\).

Proof. Set \(B_1 = A_1\) and \(B_n = A_n \setminus A_{n-1}\). The \(B_n\) are disjoint, \(\bigcup_{k \le n} B_k = A_n\) and \(\bigcup_n B_n = \bigcup_n A_n\), so \(P(\bigcup A_n) = \sum_n P(B_n) = \lim_n \sum_{k \le n} P(B_k) = \lim_n P(A_n)\). For decreasing sets apply this to the complements \(A_n^c\), which increase.

The left limit \(F(x^-) = \lim_{t \uparrow x} F(t) = P(X < x)\), so the pmf is recovered as the jump \(p_X(x) = F(x) - F(x^-)\). For discrete variables endpoints matter:

$$P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a), \quad P(a \le X \le b) = F(b) - F(a^-), \quad P(a < X < b) = F(b^-) - F(a), \quad P(X \ge a) = 1 - F(a^-).$$
Worked example · Sum of two dice

Count the pairs on each anti-diagonal of the \(6 \times 6\) grid: \(p(s) = (6 - |s - 7|)/36\) for \(s = 2, \dots, 12\). Accumulating, \(36\,F(s) = 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 26, 30, 33, 35, 36\). Then \(P(4 < X \le 8) = F(8) - F(4) = \tfrac{26 - 6}{36} = \tfrac59\), while \(P(4 \le X \le 8) = F(8) - F(3) = \tfrac{23}{36}\): the endpoint 4 carries \(\tfrac3{36}\).

Functions of a discrete random variable

If \(Y = g(X)\), then \(Y\) is again a random variable (a composition of functions) with

$$p_Y(y) = P(g(X) = y) = \sum_{x \,:\, g(x) = y} p_X(x).$$
Worked example · The pmf of \(X^2\)

Let \(X\) take \(-1, 0, 1, 2\) with probabilities \(0.2, 0.3, 0.1, 0.4\). Then \(Y = X^2\) takes \(0\) with probability \(0.3\), \(1\) with probability \(0.2 + 0.1 = 0.3\) (two values of \(x\) collapse onto \(y = 1\)), and \(4\) with probability \(0.4\).

Indicator random variables

Definition · Indicator

\(\mathbf 1_A(\omega) = 1\) if \(\omega \in A\), else 0. It is Bernoulli with parameter \(P(A)\), and with \(\E[X] = \sum_x x\,p_X(x)\) (developed in L102), \(\E[\mathbf 1_A] = 1 \cdot P(A) + 0 \cdot P(A^c) = P(A)\). Algebra: \(\mathbf 1_{A \cap B} = \mathbf 1_A \mathbf 1_B\), \(\mathbf 1_{A^c} = 1 - \mathbf 1_A\), \(\mathbf 1_A^2 = \mathbf 1_A\).

The device: write a complicated count as a sum of indicators, then use linearity of expectation, which needs no independence.

Worked example · Expected matches

\(n\) letters go into \(n\) addressed envelopes uniformly at random. Let \(A_i\) = “letter \(i\) is in its own envelope”, so \(P(A_i) = (n-1)!/n! = 1/n\). The number of matches is \(M = \sum_i \mathbf 1_{A_i}\), so \(\E[M] = n \cdot \tfrac1n = 1\) for every \(n\), even though the \(A_i\) are dependent. The full distribution is harder: inclusion–exclusion (L31) gives \(P(M = 0) = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k/k! \to e^{-1} \approx 0.368\).

Common errors
  • Writing \(P(X)\). \(X\) is a function, not an event; write \(P(X = 3)\) or \(P(X \le 3)\).
  • Endpoint slips. For discrete \(X\), \(P(X < 8) = F(7) \ne F(8)\).
  • Using \(p_X(\sqrt y)\) for the pmf of \(X^2\). When \(g\) is not one-to-one, add over every preimage.
  • Defining \(F\) only on the support. \(F(7.5) = F(7)\) is a legitimate and necessary value.
  • Thinking indicator tricks need independence. Linearity of expectation never does; variances of sums do (L104).

Practice · 40 min

  1. Prove it. From the axioms, any CDF is non-decreasing and right-continuous (and has limits 0 and 1). Hint: express each property as a statement about nested events and use the continuity lemma.
    Proof sketch

    If \(x \le y\) then \(\{X \le x\} \subseteq \{X \le y\}\), so monotonicity of \(P\) gives \(F(x) \le F(y)\). For right-continuity, \(\{X \le x + 1/n\}\) decreases to \(\bigcap_n \{X \le x + 1/n\} = \{X \le x\}\), so \(F(x + 1/n) \to F(x)\); monotonicity of \(F\) upgrades this sequence to all \(t \downarrow x\). Similarly \(\{X \le -n\} \downarrow \varnothing\) and \(\{X \le n\} \uparrow \Omega\) give the limits 0 and 1. Left-continuity fails because \(\{X \le x - 1/n\} \uparrow \{X < x\}\), which can miss an atom at \(x\).

  2. \(X\) is the number of heads in three fair tosses. Give its pmf and CDF, and compute \(P(1 \le X < 3)\) from the CDF.
    Answer

    \(p = \tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18\) on \(0,1,2,3\); \(F = \tfrac18, \tfrac48, \tfrac78, 1\). \(P(1 \le X < 3) = F(2) - F(0) = \tfrac68\).

  3. \(F(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\), \(0.3\) on \([0,1)\), \(0.7\) on \([1,3)\), \(1\) for \(x \ge 3\). Find the pmf, \(P(X > 1)\) and \(P(X \ge 1)\).
    Answer

    \(p(0) = 0.3\), \(p(1) = 0.4\), \(p(3) = 0.3\). \(P(X > 1) = 1 - F(1) = 0.3\); \(P(X \ge 1) = 1 - F(1^-) = 0.7\).

  4. A fair die shows \(X\). Find the pmf of \(Y = (X - 3)^2\).
    Answer

    Values \(4, 1, 0, 1, 4, 9\): \(p_Y(0) = \tfrac16\), \(p_Y(1) = \tfrac26\), \(p_Y(4) = \tfrac26\), \(p_Y(9) = \tfrac16\).

  5. Use indicators to find the expected number of distinct faces seen in 6 rolls of a die.
    Answer

    Face \(j\) is seen with probability \(1 - (5/6)^6\), so \(\E = 6\big(1 - (5/6)^6\big) \approx 3.99\).

Bernoulli and binomial

A Bernoulli\((p)\) variable is one trial: 1 with probability \(p\), else 0. Its mean is \(p\) and, since \(X^2 = X\), \(\Var X = p - p^2 = p(1-p)\). A Binomial\((n,p)\) variable counts successes in \(n\) independent trials. Any particular sequence with \(k\) successes has probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\) by independence, and there are \(\binom nk\) such sequences (choose the positions of the successes), which gives the pmf.

Worked example · Binomial mean and variance by indicators

Write \(X = I_1 + \cdots + I_n\) with \(I_j\) the indicator of success on trial \(j\). Linearity gives \(\E X = np\) with no sums of binomial coefficients. The \(I_j\) are independent, so variances add (L102): \(\Var X = \sum_j p(1-p) = np(1-p)\).

Geometric and negative binomial; memorylessness

If \(X\) counts trials up to and including the first success, \(P(X = k) = (1-p)^{k-1}p\) and the tail is \(P(X > k) = (1-p)^k\) (the first \(k\) trials all fail). The other convention counts failures before the first success, \(Y = X - 1\) on \(\{0, 1, \dots\}\), with mean \((1-p)/p\) and the same variance. For any variable on \(\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\), \(\E X = \sum_{k \ge 0} P(X > k)\), so \(\E X = \sum_k (1-p)^k = 1/p\) in one line.

Theorem · Memorylessness

For \(X \sim\) Geometric\((p)\) and integers \(m, n \ge 0\): \(P(X > m + n \mid X > m) = P(X > n)\). Proof: the left side is \((1-p)^{m+n}/(1-p)^m = (1-p)^n\). Having failed \(m\) times, the process starts afresh.

The negative binomial counts trials to the \(r\)-th success. The last trial must be a success and the other \(r - 1\) successes sit somewhere among the first \(k - 1\) trials, hence \(\binom{k-1}{r-1}p^r(1-p)^{k-r}\). It is a sum of \(r\) independent geometrics, so its mean and variance are \(r\) times theirs.

Hypergeometric: sampling without replacement

Draw \(n\) items without replacement from \(N\), of which \(K\) are “successes”. Choosing \(k\) of the \(K\) and \(n - k\) of the other \(N - K\) gives the pmf. By symmetry each draw is a success with probability \(K/N\), so \(\E X = nK/N\) again by indicators; the draws are negatively correlated, which produces the finite-population factor \((N - n)/(N - 1)\) in the variance. As \(N \to \infty\) with \(K/N \to p\), drawing without replacement barely differs from drawing with it, and the pmf tends to Binomial\((n, p)\). A common rule of thumb uses the binomial when \(n \le 0.05N\).

Worked example · Quality control

A lot of 50 items contains 5 defectives. Inspect 5 at random and accept the lot if none is defective:

$$P(X = 0) = \frac{\binom{45}{5}}{\binom{50}{5}} = \frac{1{,}221{,}759}{2{,}118{,}760} \approx 0.577.$$

The binomial approximation gives \(0.9^5 \approx 0.590\), close but too optimistic, because each good item drawn makes the next draw slightly more likely to be defective. A lot this bad still passes more than half the time: the plan needs a larger sample.

Poisson: the law of rare events

The pmf \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\) sums to 1 because \(\sum_k \lambda^k/k! = e^\lambda\).

Worked example · Poisson mean and variance by the series

\(\E X = \sum_{k \ge 1} k\,e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda \sum_{k \ge 1} e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda\). The same shift twice gives \(\E[X(X-1)] = \lambda^2\), so \(\E X^2 = \lambda^2 + \lambda\) and \(\Var X = \lambda\).

Theorem · Poisson limit of the binomial

If \(X_n \sim\) Binomial\((n, \lambda/n)\), then for each fixed \(k\), \(P(X_n = k) \to e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!\).

Proof $$\binom nk \Big(\frac\lambda n\Big)^k \Big(1 - \frac\lambda n\Big)^{n-k} = \underbrace{\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}}_{\to 1} \cdot \frac{\lambda^k}{k!} \cdot \underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac\lambda n\Big)^n}_{\to e^{-\lambda}} \cdot \underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac\lambda n\Big)^{-k}}_{\to 1}.$$
Worked example · The limit at \(n = 1000\)

Binomial\((1000, 0.002)\) against Poisson\((2)\): \(P(X = 0)\) is \(0.998^{1000} = 0.13506\) against \(e^{-2} = 0.13534\); \(P(X = 2)\) is \(0.27094\) against \(0.27067\). The largest gap over \(k\) is about \(3 \times 10^{-4}\).

The Poisson process. If arrivals in disjoint time intervals are independent and the count in any interval of length \(t\) is Poisson\((\lambda t)\), then the first arrival time \(T\) satisfies \(P(T > t) = P(\text{no arrivals in } [0, t]) = e^{-\lambda t}\): an exponential waiting time, the continuous twin of the geometric (L101).

Multinomial, categorical, and choosing a model

The multinomial puts each of \(n\) independent trials into one of \(m\) categories with probabilities \(p_1, \dots, p_m\): \(P(k_1, \dots, k_m) = \frac{n!}{k_1! \cdots k_m!}\prod_i p_i^{k_i}\). Each count is Binomial\((n, p_i)\), and counts compete, \(\Cov(X_i, X_j) = -np_ip_j\). With \(n = 1\) it is the categorical distribution: a one-hot vector \(x\) with \(P(x) = \prod_i p_i^{x_i}\), so \(\log P(x) = \sum_i x_i \log p_i\), the negative of the cross-entropy loss behind every classifier (L118).

A first look at MGFs. \(M_X(t) = \E[e^{tX}]\) packs every moment into one function, since \(M_X'(0) = \E X\). For the binomial, \(M(t) = (1 - p + pe^t)^n\); for the Poisson, \(M(t) = \sum_k e^{-\lambda}(\lambda e^t)^k/k! = e^{\lambda(e^t - 1)}\), and \(M'(0) = \lambda\). L102 develops the tool.

Common errors
  • Mixing geometric conventions. Trials (mean \(1/p\)) or failures (mean \((1-p)/p\)): check the support before quoting a formula.
  • Binomial for draws without replacement from a small population: the variance is overstated by the factor \((N-1)/(N-n)\).
  • Poisson for clustered events. If events trigger one another the variance exceeds the mean (overdispersion), and the Poisson understates tail risk.
  • Dropping \(\binom nk\), or using it when the order of outcomes is fixed.

Continuous Random Variables, PDFs & CDFs

34m video45m practice

For a continuous random variable every single value has probability zero, so probability lives on intervals and is measured as area under a density. The CDF is the bridge: integrate the density to get it, differentiate it to get the density back, and invert it to turn uniform random numbers into draws from any distribution.

Why \(P(X = x) = 0\), and what that does not mean

Definition · Continuous random variable and density

\(X\) is continuous if there is a function \(f \ge 0\), its density, with \(P(a < X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) for all \(a < b\). Taking \(a \to -\infty, b \to \infty\) forces \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f = 1\). Conversely, any \(f \ge 0\) with integral 1 is the density of some random variable.

For every \(x\) and \(h > 0\), \(P(X = x) \le P(x - h < X \le x) = \int_{x-h}^x f \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\). So endpoints never matter: \(P(a \le X \le b) = P(a < X < b)\). What it does not mean is that \(x\) is impossible: some value is always observed, and each one had probability 0. It also means conditioning on \(\{X = x\}\) cannot use the ratio definition of L97; L103 defines conditional densities instead.

Reading a density correctly

A density is not a probability. It is probability per unit of \(x\), so it has units, and it can exceed 1: Uniform\((0, \tfrac12)\) has \(f = 2\) on its support, and \(f(x) = 1/(2\sqrt x)\) on \((0, 1]\) is unbounded yet integrates to 1. The correct reading, where \(f\) is continuous at \(x\), is

$$P(x < X \le x + dx) \approx f(x)\,dx .$$

The support is where \(f > 0\); a mode is a maximiser of \(f\); \(f\) is symmetric about \(c\) if \(f(c + t) = f(c - t)\), in which case \(c\) is a median, and the mean whenever the mean exists (the Cauchy density is symmetric about 0 and has no mean; L101).

The CDF and the density are FTC partners

Theorem · CDF from density and back

\(F(x) = P(X \le x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f(t)\,dt\), so \(F\) is continuous, and by the fundamental theorem of calculus (L70) \(F'(x) = f(x)\) at every point where \(f\) is continuous. Interval probabilities are differences: \(P(a < X \le b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

Worked example · Normalise, then CDF, median, interval

\(f(x) = c\,x^2\) on \([0, 2]\), zero elsewhere. Normalise: \(c \int_0^2 x^2\,dx = 8c/3 = 1\), so \(c = 3/8\). CDF: \(F(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\), \(x^3/8\) on \([0,2]\), 1 for \(x > 2\). Median: \(m^3/8 = \tfrac12\) gives \(m = 4^{1/3} \approx 1.587\). Interval: \(P(1 < X < 1.5) = (1.5^3 - 1)/8 = 2.375/8 \approx 0.297\). The mode is 2 (where \(f = 1.5\)), and the mean is \(\int_0^2 \tfrac38 x^3\,dx = 1.5\), below the median: a long left tail.

Quantiles

Definition · Quantile function

\(F^{-1}(u) = \inf\{x : F(x) \ge u\}\) for \(u \in (0, 1)\). If \(F\) is continuous and strictly increasing this is the ordinary inverse; the infimum handles flat stretches and jumps. The median is \(F^{-1}(\tfrac12)\), the quartiles \(F^{-1}(\tfrac14), F^{-1}(\tfrac34)\), the \(p\)-th percentile \(F^{-1}(p/100)\).

Lemma · Switching between F and its inverse

For every \(u \in (0,1)\) and real \(x\): \(F^{-1}(u) \le x \iff u \le F(x)\).

Proof. (⇐) If \(u \le F(x)\), \(x\) is in the set \(\{t : F(t) \ge u\}\), so its infimum is at most \(x\). (⇒) By right-continuity the set \(\{t : F(t) \ge u\}\) contains its infimum, so \(F(F^{-1}(u)) \ge u\); if \(F^{-1}(u) \le x\), monotonicity gives \(F(x) \ge F(F^{-1}(u)) \ge u\).

Inverse transform sampling

Theorem · Inverse transform

If \(U \sim\) Uniform\((0,1)\) and \(F\) is any CDF, then \(X = F^{-1}(U)\) has CDF \(F\). This is how a computer turns uniform random numbers into draws from other distributions. Conversely, if \(F\) is continuous then \(F(X) \sim\) Uniform\((0,1)\) (the probability integral transform, the reason p-values are uniform under the null in L115).

Worked example · Exponential sampler

\(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x \ge 0\). Solve \(u = F(x)\): \(x = -\ln(1 - u)/\lambda\). Directly, for \(x \ge 0\):

$$P\Big(-\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\ln(1 - U) \le x\Big) = P\big(1 - U \ge e^{-\lambda x}\big) = P\big(U \le 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\big) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}.$$

Because \(1 - U\) is also uniform, \(-\ln(U)/\lambda\) works as well.

Mixed distributions

Some variables are neither discrete nor continuous. A lifetime \(T \sim\) Exp\((\lambda)\) observed only until a study ends at time \(c\) is recorded as \(Y = \min(T, c)\): on \([0, c)\) it has density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\), and it has an atom \(P(Y = c) = e^{-\lambda c}\), a jump in its CDF. Censored survival data, rainfall (an atom at zero) and insurance claims all look like this. The CDF still describes them completely; L121 shows how measure theory treats pmfs, densities and mixtures as one object.

Common errors
  • Reading \(f(x)\) as \(P(X = x)\), and so believing \(f \le 1\).
  • Integrating over the wrong range. Outside the support \(f = 0\); the CDF is 0 to the left and 1 to the right, and must be written for every real \(x\).
  • Sampling with \(F(U)\) instead of \(F^{-1}(U)\).
  • Assuming symmetry gives a mean. It gives a median; the mean must also exist.

    Proof sketch

Uniform and exponential

Uniform\((a,b)\) is “no preference within an interval”; through the inverse transform of L100 it generates everything else. Exponential\((\lambda)\) is the waiting time to the first arrival of a Poisson process of rate \(\lambda\) (L99): \(P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). Its mean \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt = 1/\lambda\) comes from the tail formula \(\E T = \int_0^\infty P(T > t)\,dt\).

Theorem · Memorylessness

For \(s, t \ge 0\), \(P(T > s + t \mid T > s) = P(T > t)\). Among continuous distributions on \((0,\infty)\) only the exponential has this property, just as only the geometric does among discrete ones.

The normal distribution

The constant \(1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})\) is forced by the Gaussian integral \(\int_\R e^{-z^2/2}dz = \sqrt{2\pi}\) (L73) and the substitution \(z = (x - \mu)/\sigma\), \(dx = \sigma\,dz\). The same substitution gives standardisation: if \(X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) then \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)\), since for \(\sigma > 0\), \(P(Z \le z) = P(X \le \mu + \sigma z) = \Phi(z)\). More generally \(aX + b \sim \mathcal N(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)\) for \(a \ne 0\), and sums of independent normals are normal; both get one-line MGF proofs in L102.

The 68–95–99.7 rule: \(P(|Z| \le 1) \approx 0.683\), \(P(|Z| \le 2) \approx 0.954\), \(P(|Z| \le 3) \approx 0.997\). The normal is everywhere for three separate reasons: the central limit theorem makes sums of many small effects approximately normal (L108); it has the largest entropy among densities with a given variance (L119); and it is closed under affine maps, sums, marginals and conditionals (L105), so computations stay in closed form.

Worked example · Normal table by hand

\(X \sim \mathcal N(100, 15^2)\). Standardise, then read \(\Phi\):

  • \(P(X > 130) = P(Z > 2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228\).
  • \(P(85 < X < 115) = P(-1 < Z < 1) = 2\Phi(1) - 1 = 2(0.8413) - 1 = 0.6826\).
  • \(P(X < 110) = \Phi(0.67) \approx 0.749\) (round \(z = 0.667\) to the table's 0.67).
  • 90th percentile: \(\Phi(z) = 0.90\) at \(z \approx 1.28\), so \(x = 100 + 15(1.28) \approx 119.2\).

Gamma, beta and chi-square

Gamma\((n, \lambda)\) with integer \(n\) is the waiting time to the \(n\)-th arrival; for general \(\alpha > 0\) the normaliser is \(\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}dx\) from L73. Beta\((\alpha,\beta)\) lives on \((0,1)\), so it models an unknown probability; Beta\((1,1)\) is uniform, and it is the conjugate prior of L113. If \(X \sim\) Gamma\((\alpha, 1)\) and \(Y \sim\) Gamma\((\beta, 1)\) are independent, \(X/(X+Y) \sim\) Beta\((\alpha, \beta)\). Chi-square \(\chi^2_k\) is the law of \(Z_1^2 + \cdots + Z_k^2\) for independent standard normals; it is Gamma\((k/2, 1/2)\), and it governs sample variances (L110).

Worked example · Sums of exponentials are Gamma

For independent \(X, Y \sim\) Exp\((\lambda)\), the convolution formula (L106) gives, for \(s > 0\),

$$f_{X+Y}(s) = \int_0^s \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,\lambda e^{-\lambda(s-x)}\,dx = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda s}\int_0^s dx = \lambda^2 s\,e^{-\lambda s},$$

the Gamma\((2, \lambda)\) density. Induction: if \(S_n\) has density \(\lambda^n s^{n-1}e^{-\lambda s}/(n-1)!\), convolving with one more exponential gives \(\lambda^{n+1}e^{-\lambda s}\int_0^s x^{n-1}dx/(n-1)! = \lambda^{n+1}s^n e^{-\lambda s}/n!\), which is Gamma\((n+1, \lambda)\).

Worked example · \(Z^2 \sim \chi^2_1\)

For \(y > 0\), \(P(Z^2 \le y) = P(-\sqrt y \le Z \le \sqrt y) = 2\Phi(\sqrt y) - 1\). Differentiate with the chain rule:

$$f_{Z^2}(y) = 2\,\varphi(\sqrt y)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,y^{-1/2}e^{-y/2},$$

which is the Gamma\((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) density, because \((\tfrac12)^{1/2}/\Gamma(\tfrac12) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) using \(\Gamma(\tfrac12) = \sqrt\pi\).

Ratios and heavy tails: t, F, Laplace, Cauchy, log-normal

  • Student's \(t_\nu\) is \(Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}\) with \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\) independent of \(V \sim \chi^2_\nu\). Its polynomial tails are heavier than the normal's; \(t_1\) is Cauchy and \(t_\nu \to \mathcal N(0,1)\) as \(\nu \to \infty\).
  • \(F_{d_1, d_2}\) is \((U/d_1)/(V/d_2)\) for independent \(U \sim \chi^2_{d_1}\), \(V \sim \chi^2_{d_2}\): the law of variance ratios in ANOVA (L115). Note \(t_\nu^2 \sim F_{1,\nu}\).
  • Laplace is the difference of two independent exponentials. Its log-density is \(-|x - \mu|/b\), so maximising a Laplace likelihood minimises \(\sum|x_i - \mu|\) (the median), and a Laplace prior gives the L¹ penalty of the lasso (L113).
  • Cauchy has \(\int |x|/(\pi(1+x^2))\,dx = \infty\), so it has no mean; the average of \(n\) independent Cauchy draws is again standard Cauchy, and never settles down. It is also \(Z_1/Z_2\).
  • Log-normal \(e^Y\) models products of many positive factors (incomes, file sizes). Its median is \(e^\mu\) but its mean is \(e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\), pulled up by the right tail.
Common errors
  • Rate versus scale. Exponential\((\lambda)\) has mean \(1/\lambda\) in the rate convention but mean \(\lambda\) in the scale convention; Gamma has the same split. Check the mean before using a formula.
  • \(\sigma\) versus \(\sigma^2\). \(\mathcal N(0, 4)\) has standard deviation 2; standardise by dividing by \(\sigma\), never by \(\sigma^2\).
  • Quoting \(e^\mu\) as the log-normal mean. It is the median.
  • Averaging heavy-tailed data and trusting it. For Cauchy data the sample mean is no better than a single observation.

Expectation, Variance, Moments & MGFs

36m video50m practiceheavy lesson

Expectation is the balance point of a distribution, and linearity makes it the easiest quantity in probability to compute. Variance and higher moments measure spread and shape, and the moment generating function packs every moment into one function whose product rule makes sums of independent variables easy.

Expectation and when it exists

Definition · Expectation

\(\E X = \sum_x x\,p_X(x)\) (discrete) or \(\E X = \int_\R x\,f_X(x)\,dx\) (continuous), provided \(\sum |x|\,p_X(x) < \infty\), respectively \(\int |x| f_X(x)\,dx < \infty\). Otherwise \(\E X\) does not exist.

Place mass \(p(x)\) at each point \(x\) of a weightless ruler: \(\E X\) is where the ruler balances. Absolute convergence matters. For the Cauchy density (L101) both \(\int_0^\infty x f\) and \(\int_{-\infty}^0 x f\) are infinite; the symmetric limit \(\int_{-M}^M x f = 0\) is not the definition, and averages of Cauchy samples do not converge to 0 or to anything else.

Theorem · Law of the unconscious statistician (LOTUS)

\(\E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)\,p_X(x)\), or \(\int g(x) f_X(x)\,dx\), whenever the sum or integral converges absolutely. You never need the distribution of \(g(X)\).

Proof (discrete). Group the \(x\) by the value \(y = g(x)\): \(\E[g(X)] = \sum_y y\,P(g(X) = y) = \sum_y y \sum_{x : g(x) = y} p_X(x) = \sum_x g(x)\,p_X(x)\).

Linearity, with no independence needed

Theorem · Linearity of expectation

\(\E[aX + bY + c] = a\,\E X + b\,\E Y + c\) whenever \(\E X, \E Y\) exist, whatever the dependence between \(X\) and \(Y\).

Proof (discrete). LOTUS for the pair with \(g(x, y) = x + y\): \(\E[X + Y] = \sum_{x,y}(x + y)\,p(x,y) = \sum_x x \sum_y p(x,y) + \sum_y y \sum_x p(x,y) = \E X + \E Y\), since the inner sums are the marginals. Scaling and constants are immediate; the continuous case replaces sums by integrals.

Worked example · Fixed points of a random permutation

Shuffle \(n\) cards; card \(i\) is a fixed point if it lands in position \(i\). With \(I_i\) its indicator, \(P(I_i = 1) = 1/n\), and the number of fixed points \(N = \sum_i I_i\) has \(\E N = n \cdot \tfrac1n = 1\), for every \(n\). The \(I_i\) are dependent (if \(n - 1\) cards are fixed, so is the last), and linearity does not care.

Variance

Definition and two identities

\(\Var X = \E[(X - \mu)^2]\) with \(\mu = \E X\); the standard deviation \(\sigma = \sqrt{\Var X}\) has the units of \(X\).

(i) \(\Var X = \E[X^2] - (\E X)^2\): expand \(\E[X^2 - 2\mu X + \mu^2] = \E X^2 - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2\) by linearity. (ii) \(\Var(aX + b) = a^2 \Var X\): \(aX + b - (a\mu + b) = a(X - \mu)\), and squaring brings out \(a^2\). Shifting does not change spread.

Worked example · \(\Var(X + Y) = \Var X + \Var Y\) for independent \(X, Y\)

First, independence gives \(\E[g(X)h(Y)] = \sum_{x,y} g(x)h(y)\,p_X(x)p_Y(y) = \E[g(X)]\,\E[h(Y)]\). Now expand:

$$\Var(X+Y) = \E\big[\big((X - \mu_X) + (Y - \mu_Y)\big)^2\big] = \Var X + \Var Y + 2\,\E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)],$$

and the cross term factors into \(\E[X - \mu_X]\,\E[Y - \mu_Y] = 0\). Without independence the cross term is \(2\Cov(X, Y)\) (L104).

Moments and shape

The \(k\)-th moment is \(\E X^k\); the \(k\)-th central moment is \(\E(X - \mu)^k\). Dividing by \(\sigma^k\) makes them unit-free:

  • Skewness \(\E[(X - \mu)^3]/\sigma^3\): its sign points to the longer tail. Exponential: 2. Any symmetric law with a third moment: 0.
  • Kurtosis \(\E[(X - \mu)^4]/\sigma^4\): weight in the tails relative to the shoulders. The normal has 3, so excess kurtosis is kurtosis minus 3: exponential \(+6\), uniform \(-1.2\), \(t_\nu\) \(6/(\nu - 4)\) for \(\nu > 4\) and infinite or undefined below.

Moment generating functions

Definition · MGF

\(M_X(t) = \E[e^{tX}]\), considered only when it is finite for all \(t\) in an open interval around 0.

Theorem · Four properties

(i) \(M_X^{(k)}(0) = \E[X^k]\). (ii) Uniqueness: if \(M_X = M_Y\) on a neighbourhood of 0, \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same distribution. (iii) If \(X, Y\) are independent, \(M_{X+Y} = M_X M_Y\). (iv) \(M_{aX + b}(t) = e^{bt}M_X(at)\).

Proof of (i), (iii), (iv). (i) \(e^{tX} = \sum_k t^k X^k/k!\), so \(M_X(t) = \sum_k \E[X^k]\,t^k/k!\) (the swap of \(\E\) and \(\sum\) is justified when \(M\) is finite near 0), and the \(k\)-th derivative at 0 keeps only \(\E X^k\). (iii) \(\E[e^{tX}e^{tY}] = \E[e^{tX}]\,\E[e^{tY}]\) by independence. (iv) \(\E[e^{t(aX+b)}] = e^{bt}\E[e^{(at)X}]\). (ii) is a deep theorem, used here without proof.

Worked example · The normal MGF

For \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\) complete the square, \(tz - z^2/2 = -(z - t)^2/2 + t^2/2\):

$$M_Z(t) = \int_\R \frac{e^{-(z-t)^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dz \cdot e^{t^2/2} = e^{t^2/2}, \qquad M_X(t) = e^{\mu t}M_Z(\sigma t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2} \text{ for } X = \mu + \sigma Z.$$

Then \(M_X' = (\mu + \sigma^2 t)M_X\), so \(\E X = M_X'(0) = \mu\); \(M_X'' = \sigma^2 M_X + (\mu + \sigma^2 t)^2 M_X\), so \(\E X^2 = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\) and \(\Var X = \sigma^2\).

Sums by multiplication. Independent normals: \(e^{\mu_1 t + \sigma_1^2t^2/2}e^{\mu_2 t + \sigma_2^2t^2/2}\) is the MGF of \(\mathcal N(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)\), and by (iv) \(aX + b \sim \mathcal N(a\mu + b, a^2\sigma^2)\). Independent Poissons: \(e^{\lambda_1(e^t - 1)}e^{\lambda_2(e^t - 1)} = e^{(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)(e^t - 1)}\), so Poisson\((\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\). Independent Exp\((\lambda)\): each has \(M(t) = \lambda/(\lambda - t)\) for \(t < \lambda\), and \((\lambda/(\lambda - t))^n\) is the Gamma\((n, \lambda)\) MGF. Uniqueness turns each product into a distribution.

The characteristic function \(\varphi_X(t) = \E[e^{itX}]\) always exists because \(|e^{itX}| = 1\), and it has the same uniqueness and product properties. The Cauchy distribution has no MGF but \(\varphi(t) = e^{-|t|}\). The CLT proof in L108 runs on it.

Common errors
  • \(\E[g(X)] = g(\E X)\). False for nonlinear \(g\): \(\E[X^2] \ne (\E X)^2\), \(\E[1/X] \ne 1/\E X\). Jensen (L107) says which way the gap goes.
  • \(\Var(X - Y) = \Var X - \Var Y\). For independent variables it is \(\Var X + \Var Y\): noise adds.
  • \(\Var(aX) = a\Var X\). It is \(a^2\Var X\).
  • \(\E[XY] = \E X\,\E Y\) without justification. It needs independence, or at least zero covariance.
  • Assuming an MGF exists. The log-normal has every moment but \(M(t) = \infty\) for all \(t > 0\); then use the characteristic function.

Joint, Marginal & Conditional Distributions

38m video55m practice

A joint distribution tells the whole story about several variables at once. Marginals forget a variable by summing or integrating it out; conditionals take a slice of the joint and renormalise it. The tower property and the law of total variance are what turn conditioning into a computational tool.

Joint pmfs, densities and CDFs

Definition · Joint distribution

Discrete: \(p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y)\) with \(\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) = 1\). Continuous: \(f(x, y) \ge 0\) with \(P((X, Y) \in A) = \iint_A f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\) for regions \(A\), so \(\iint_{\R^2} f = 1\). The joint CDF \(F(x, y) = P(X \le x, Y \le y)\) determines everything, and \(f = \partial^2 F / \partial x\,\partial y\) where \(f\) is continuous.

Probabilities of regions are the double integrals of L84, and most errors come from the limits: draw the support first, then read the limits off the picture.

Marginals and conditionals

Definition · Marginal and conditional densities

\(f_X(x) = \int_\R f(x, y)\,dy\) (sum over \(y\) in the discrete case). For \(f_X(x) > 0\), \(\displaystyle f_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f(x, y)}{f_X(x)}\). As a function of \(y\) it is a genuine density: it is non-negative and \(\int f(x,y)\,dy / f_X(x) = 1\).

The conditional density is the joint density along the vertical line at \(x\), rescaled to area 1. It is the limit of \(P(Y \le y \mid x \le X \le x + h)\) as \(h \to 0\), which sidesteps the fact that \(P(X = x) = 0\). Rearranged, it is the chain rule for densities, \(f(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)\), and for \(n\) variables

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = f(x_1)\,f(x_2 \mid x_1)\,f(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots f(x_n \mid x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}),$$

the factorisation behind every autoregressive model.

Independence and the rectangle test

Definition · Independent random variables

\(X\) and \(Y\) are independent if \(F(x,y) = F_X(x)F_Y(y)\) for all \(x, y\); equivalently \(f(x,y) = f_X(x)f_Y(y)\) (or \(p(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y)\)) everywhere. Factorisation criterion: they are independent if and only if \(f(x,y) = g(x)h(y)\) for all \((x,y)\), including where \(f = 0\), for some functions \(g, h\).

Consequence: the support of independent variables is a product set, a rectangle for intervals. If some \((x_0, y_0)\) has \(f(x_0, y_0) = 0\) while \(f_X(x_0) > 0\) and \(f_Y(y_0) > 0\), they are dependent. Random variables \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are i.i.d. when they are mutually independent with a common marginal \(f\); their joint density is \(\prod_i f(x_i)\), the likelihood of Phase 8.

Worked example · A density on a triangle

\(f(x,y) = 8xy\) on \(0 < y < x < 1\). Normalisation: \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^x 8xy\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 4x^3\,dx = 1\). Marginals:

$$f_X(x) = \int_0^x 8xy\,dy = 4x^3, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 8xy\,dx = 4y(1 - y^2), \qquad 0 < x, y < 1.$$

Conditional: \(f_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = 8xy/4x^3 = 2y/x^2\) on \(0 < y < x\). Independence? The formula \(8xy\) looks like \(g(x)h(y)\), but the full density is \(8xy\,\mathbf 1\{y < x\}\) and the indicator does not factor. Concretely \(f(0.5, 0.75) = 0\) while \(f_X(0.5) f_Y(0.75) = 0.5 \times 1.3125 > 0\). Dependent.

Conditional expectation and the tower property

Definition · Conditional expectation

\(h(x) = \E[Y \mid X = x] = \int y\,f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)\,dy\) is a number for each \(x\). \(\E[Y \mid X] = h(X)\) is a random variable, a function of \(X\).

Theorem · Tower property (law of iterated expectations)

\(\E\big[\E[Y \mid X]\big] = \E Y\). Also, known factors come out: \(\E[g(X)\,Y \mid X] = g(X)\,\E[Y \mid X]\).

Proof. \(\E[h(X)] = \int h(x) f_X(x)\,dx = \iint y\,f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)\,f_X(x)\,dy\,dx = \iint y\,f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \E Y\) by the chain rule for densities.

Worked example · Tower property on the triangle

\(\E[Y \mid X = x] = \int_0^x y \cdot \frac{2y}{x^2}\,dy = \frac{2x}{3}\), so \(\E[Y \mid X] = \tfrac23 X\). Tower: \(\E Y = \tfrac23\E X = \tfrac23 \int_0^1 x \cdot 4x^3\,dx = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac45 = \tfrac{8}{15}\). Directly: \(\int_0^1 y \cdot 4y(1 - y^2)\,dy = 4(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15) = \tfrac8{15}\).

The law of total variance

Theorem · Law of total variance

With \(\Var(Y \mid X) = \E[Y^2 \mid X] - (\E[Y \mid X])^2\),

$$\Var Y = \underbrace{\E\big[\Var(Y \mid X)\big]}_{\text{unexplained: spread within each slice}} + \underbrace{\Var\big(\E[Y \mid X]\big)}_{\text{explained: spread of the slice means}}.$$

The second term is the part of \(Y\)'s variability that knowing \(X\) accounts for; the first is what remains. This split is the population version of \(R^2\) in regression and the ancestor of the bias–variance decomposition of L111.

Worked example · Total variance on the triangle

\(\E[Y^2 \mid X = x] = \int_0^x y^2 \frac{2y}{x^2}dy = \frac{x^2}{2}\), so \(\Var(Y \mid X = x) = \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{4x^2}{9} = \frac{x^2}{18}\). With \(\E X^2 = \int 4x^5 = \tfrac23\) and \(\Var X = \tfrac23 - \tfrac{16}{25} = \tfrac{2}{75}\):

$$\E[\Var(Y \mid X)] = \frac{2/3}{18} = \frac{1}{27}, \qquad \Var\big(\tfrac23 X\big) = \frac49 \cdot \frac{2}{75} = \frac{8}{675}, \qquad \text{sum} = \frac{25 + 8}{675} = \frac{11}{225}.$$

Check: \(\E Y^2 = \int_0^1 4y^3(1 - y^2)\,dy = \tfrac13\), so \(\Var Y = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{64}{225} = \tfrac{11}{225}\). Knowing \(X\) explains \(\tfrac{8/675}{11/225} = \tfrac{8}{33} \approx 24\%\) of the variance.

Conditional independence and graphs

\(X \perp Y \mid Z\) means \(f(x, y \mid z) = f(x \mid z) f(y \mid z)\), equivalently \(f(x \mid y, z) = f(x \mid z)\): once \(Z\) is known, \(Y\) adds nothing about \(X\). The graph \(X \leftarrow Z \rightarrow Y\) encodes \(f(x,y,z) = f(z)f(x \mid z)f(y \mid z)\): ice-cream sales and drownings can both be driven by temperature, dependent marginally and independent given it. A directed graph is a picture of which conditionals appear in the chain rule; that is the first idea of graphical models.

Common errors
  • Rectangle limits on a triangle. \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^1 8xy\) is 2, not 1; the inner limit must be \(y < x\).
  • “The formula factorises, so they are independent.” Only if the support is a rectangle too.
  • Treating \(\E[Y \mid X]\) as a number. It varies with \(X\); \(\E[Y \mid X = x]\) is the number.
  • Forgetting the explained term. \(\Var Y \ne \E[\Var(Y \mid X)]\) unless \(\E[Y \mid X]\) is constant.

Covariance, Correlation & the Covariance Matrix

34m video45m practice

Covariance measures how two variables move together linearly; correlation is its unit-free version, trapped in \([-1, 1]\) by Cauchy–Schwarz. Collect the covariances of many variables and you get a matrix that is always symmetric positive semidefinite, the object that PCA, the multivariate Gaussian and whitening are built on.

Covariance and its algebra

Definition · Covariance

\(\Cov(X, Y) = \E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)] = \E[XY] - \E X\,\E Y\) (expand and use linearity).

It is symmetric, \(\Cov(X, X) = \Var X\), constants drop out (\(\Cov(X + c, Y) = \Cov(X, Y)\)), and it is bilinear: \(\Cov\big(\sum_i a_i X_i, \sum_j b_j Y_j\big) = \sum_{i,j} a_i b_j \Cov(X_i, Y_j)\). Hence

$$\Var(X + Y) = \Var X + \Var Y + 2\Cov(X, Y), \qquad \Var\Big(\sum_i a_i X_i\Big) = \sum_{i}\sum_{j} a_i a_j \Cov(X_i, X_j).$$

Correlation and the Cauchy–Schwarz bound

Definition · Correlation

\(\rho(X, Y) = \dfrac{\Cov(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}\) for \(\sigma_X, \sigma_Y > 0\). It is unchanged by positive rescaling or shifting of either variable.

Theorem · \(|\rho| \le 1\)

\(|\rho(X, Y)| \le 1\), with \(|\rho| = 1\) exactly when \(Y = a + bX\) with probability 1 for some \(b \ne 0\) (and then \(\rho\) has the sign of \(b\)).

Proof · Cauchy–Schwarz for random variables

For any \(U, V\) with finite second moments and every real \(t\), \(0 \le \E[(V - tU)^2] = \E V^2 - 2t\,\E[UV] + t^2\,\E U^2\). A quadratic in \(t\) that is never negative has discriminant \(\le 0\): \((\E[UV])^2 \le \E U^2\,\E V^2\). Apply it to \(U = X - \mu_X\), \(V = Y - \mu_Y\): \(\Cov(X,Y)^2 \le \Var X\,\Var Y\). Equality means the discriminant is 0, so some \(t_0\) has \(\E[(V - t_0U)^2] = 0\), forcing \(V = t_0 U\) with probability 1: \(Y\) is an exact linear function of \(X\).

This is the vector Cauchy–Schwarz of L36 in disguise: \(\langle U, V\rangle = \E[UV]\) is an inner product on centred random variables, \(\sigma\) is its norm, and \(\rho\) is the cosine of the angle between \(X - \mu_X\) and \(Y - \mu_Y\).

What correlation does and does not measure

Independent implies uncorrelated: if \(X, Y\) are independent then \(\E[XY] = \E X\,\E Y\) (L102), so \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\). The converse fails, because correlation only sees the best straight-line relationship.

Worked example · \(X\) and \(X^2\) are uncorrelated

Let \(X\) be symmetric about 0 with \(\E|X|^3 < \infty\). Symmetry gives \(\E X = 0\) and \(\E X^3 = 0\), so \(\Cov(X, X^2) = \E X^3 - \E X\,\E X^2 = 0\). Yet \(X^2\) is a function of \(X\). For \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\): \(P(X^2 = 0) = \tfrac13\) but \(P(X^2 = 0 \mid X = 0) = 1\). Perfect dependence, zero correlation.

The covariance matrix

Definition · Covariance matrix

For a random vector \(x \in \R^k\) with mean \(\mu\), \(\Sigma = \E[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^T]\), so \(\Sigma_{ij} = \Cov(x_i, x_j)\) and \(\Sigma\) is symmetric with the variances on its diagonal. The correlation matrix is \(R = D^{-1/2}\Sigma D^{-1/2}\) with \(D = \operatorname{diag}(\Sigma)\).

Theorem · PSD, and the affine rule

(i) \(\Sigma\) is positive semidefinite (L56): \(v^T\Sigma v \ge 0\) for all \(v\). It is singular exactly when some combination \(v^Tx\) is constant with probability 1. (ii) If \(y = Ax + b\), then \(\E y = A\mu + b\) and \(\Cov(y) = A\Sigma A^T\).

Proof. (i) \(v^T\Sigma v = \E[v^T(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^Tv] = \E[(v^T(x - \mu))^2] = \Var(v^Tx) \ge 0\). (ii) \(y - \E y = A(x - \mu)\), so \(\E[A(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^TA^T] = A\Sigma A^T\) by linearity.

The quadratic form \(a^T\Sigma a\) is the variance formula above, in matrix dress.

Worked example · Checking \(\Cov(Ax) = A\Sigma A^T\)

\(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\), \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(y_1 = x_1 + x_2\), \(y_2 = x_1 - x_2\). By hand: \(\Var y_1 = 4 + 3 + 2(2) = 11\), \(\Var y_2 = 4 + 3 - 4 = 3\), \(\Cov(y_1, y_2) = \Var x_1 - \Var x_2 = 1\). By matrices: \(A\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 5 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(A\Sigma A^T = \begin{pmatrix} 11 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). They agree.

Sample covariance and whitening

With \(n\) observations as the rows of a data matrix and \(X_c\) its column-centred version, the sample covariance is \(S = \frac{1}{n-1}X_c^TX_c\) (the \(n - 1\) is explained in L109). Its eigenvectors are the principal directions of L58, and its eigenvalues are the variances along them.

Worked example · A 3 × 3 sample covariance by hand

Rows \((1,2,0), (2,3,1), (3,5,1), (4,6,2)\); means \((2.5, 4, 1)\). Centred rows: \((-1.5,-2,-1), (-0.5,-1,0), (0.5,1,0), (1.5,2,1)\). Summing products column by column,

$$X_c^TX_c = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 7 & 3 \\ 7 & 10 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \frac13 X_c^TX_c, \qquad r_{12} = \frac{7}{\sqrt{50}} \approx 0.99,\ r_{13} = \frac{3}{\sqrt{10}} \approx 0.95,\ r_{23} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{20}} \approx 0.89.$$

Its determinant is \(5(4) - 7(2) + 3(-2) = 0\). Semidefinite, not definite: \(v = (-2, 1, 1)\) gives \(v^TSv = 0\), and indeed every row satisfies \(x_3 = 2x_1 - x_2\). The data lie in a plane.

Whitening. If \(\Sigma\) is positive definite, the spectral theorem gives \(\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^T\) and \(\Sigma^{-1/2} = Q\Lambda^{-1/2}Q^T\). Then \(z = \Sigma^{-1/2}(x - \mu)\) has \(\Cov(z) = \Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma\Sigma^{-1/2} = I\): uncorrelated coordinates with unit variance. Any \(W\) with \(W\Sigma W^T = I\) whitens, for example \(\Lambda^{-1/2}Q^T\) (PCA whitening) or \(L^{-1}\) from the Cholesky factor (L105).

Common errors
  • “Uncorrelated, so independent.” Only for jointly Gaussian vectors (L105).
  • Reading \(\rho\) as a slope. The regression slope is \(\rho\,\sigma_Y/\sigma_X\); \(\rho = 0.9\) with a tiny \(\sigma_Y\) means a flat line.
  • Sign slips: \(\Var(X - Y) = \Var X + \Var Y - 2\Cov(X, Y)\).
  • Wrong order: \(\Cov(Ax) = A\Sigma A^T\), not \(A^T\Sigma A\); check the dimensions.

The Multivariate Gaussian

38m video50m practiceheavy lesson

The multivariate normal is a quadratic form inside an exponential. The covariance matrix fixes its shape, ellipsoids along the eigenvectors, and the family is closed under everything we do to it: affine maps, marginals and conditionals. Conditioning a Gaussian is linear regression.

The density, term by term

Definition · \(\mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)\) on \(\R^k\)

For \(\Sigma\) symmetric positive definite,

$$f(x) = (2\pi)^{-k/2}\,|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp\Big(-\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)\Big).$$
  • The exponent is \(-\tfrac12\) times a positive definite quadratic form (L15, L56): it is 0 at \(\mu\) and falls off quadratically in every direction.
  • \(|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\) is a volume correction. Substitute \(z = L^{-1}(x - \mu)\) with \(\Sigma = LL^T\): \(dx = |\det L|\,dz = |\Sigma|^{1/2}dz\) and the exponent becomes \(-\tfrac12\|z\|^2\).
  • \((2\pi)^{-k/2}\) then normalises \(\prod_{i=1}^k e^{-z_i^2/2}\), a product of \(k\) one-dimensional Gaussian integrals. So \(f\) integrates to 1.

Three cases: isotropic \(\Sigma = \sigma^2 I\) (circular contours), diagonal (axis-aligned ellipses, independent coordinates), full (rotated ellipses).

Mahalanobis distance and elliptical contours

Definition · Mahalanobis distance

\(d_M(x) = \sqrt{(x - \mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)}\): distance measured in units of the spread in that direction. The density depends on \(x\) only through \(d_M\).

Write \(\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^T\) (spectral theorem) and \(z = Q^T(x - \mu)\), the coordinates along the eigenvectors. Then \(d_M^2 = \sum_i z_i^2/\lambda_i\), so the contour \(d_M = c\) is an ellipsoid with semi-axis \(c\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) along \(q_i\). Directions of large variance are “cheap”: moving along them costs little Mahalanobis distance.

Worked example · Sketching the contours of \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\)

Characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = 0\), so \(\lambda_1 = 6\), \(\lambda_2 = 1\). For \(\lambda_1\): \((5 - 6)a + 2b = 0\) gives \(q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5\); orthogonal to it, \(q_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5\). The \(d_M = 1\) contour has semi-axes \(\sqrt6 \approx 2.45\) along \((2,1)\) and \(1\) along \((1,-2)\), tilted \(\arctan\tfrac12 \approx 26.6°\) above the \(x_1\)-axis. Check one point: \(\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac16\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 5\end{pmatrix}\) and \(x = (2,1) = \sqrt5\,q_1\) give \(x^T\Sigma^{-1}x = \tfrac16(8 - 8 + 5) = \tfrac56 = 5/\lambda_1\).

Affine maps, marginals and independence

Theorem · Closure under affine maps

If \(x \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)\) and \(A\) is \(m \times k\), then \(Ax + b \sim \mathcal N(A\mu + b, A\Sigma A^T)\) (degenerate if \(A\Sigma A^T\) is singular). The proof is today's Prove it; it uses the MGF \(M_x(s) = \E[e^{s^Tx}] = \exp(s^T\mu + \tfrac12 s^T\Sigma s)\), obtained from the one-dimensional result of L102 via \(x = \mu + Lz\).

Marginals are normal. Take \(A = [\,I\ \ 0\,]\): \(x_1 \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1, \Sigma_{11})\). You read off a marginal by deleting the other rows and columns of \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\).

Independence ⇔ zero covariance, for jointly Gaussian vectors only. If \(\Sigma_{12} = 0\), then \(|\Sigma| = |\Sigma_{11}||\Sigma_{22}|\) and the quadratic form splits, so the density factorises. The hypothesis “jointly” is essential. Let \(X \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\) and let \(W = \pm1\) be a fair sign independent of \(X\); set \(Y = WX\). By symmetry \(Y \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\), and \(\Cov(X, Y) = \E[W]\E[X^2] = 0\). Yet \(|Y| = |X|\), so they are dependent, and \(X + Y = 0\) with probability \(\tfrac12\), an atom no non-degenerate normal can have: \((X, Y)\) is not jointly Gaussian.

Conditionals and the Schur complement

Theorem · Gaussian conditioning

Partition \(x = (x_1, x_2)\) with \(\mu = (\mu_1, \mu_2)\) and blocks \(\Sigma_{ij}\). Then

$$x_1 \mid x_2 \sim \mathcal N\Big(\mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2),\ \ \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}\Big).$$
Derivation · By decorrelation

Let \(B = \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\) and \(e = x_1 - \mu_1 - B(x_2 - \mu_2)\). The pair \((e, x_2)\) is an affine function of \(x\), hence jointly Gaussian, and \(\Cov(e, x_2) = \Sigma_{12} - B\Sigma_{22} = 0\). So \(e\) is independent of \(x_2\), and conditioning on \(x_2\) leaves its distribution \(\mathcal N(0, \Cov e)\) unchanged. Therefore \(x_1 = \mu_1 + B(x_2 - \mu_2) + e\) given \(x_2\) has the stated mean, and

$$\Cov(e) = \Sigma_{11} - B\Sigma_{21} - \Sigma_{12}B^T + B\Sigma_{22}B^T = \Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21},$$

the Schur complement of \(\Sigma_{22}\). The conditional mean is linear in \(x_2\); the conditional covariance does not depend on the observed value and is never larger than \(\Sigma_{11}\). Gaussian-process regression and the Kalman filter are this formula applied repeatedly.

Worked example · Bivariate conditional and the regression line

With scalars, \(Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal N\big(\mu_Y + \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x - \mu_X),\ \sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)\big)\). The conditional mean is the population regression line, slope \(\Cov(X,Y)/\Var X\) (L116), and \(1 - \rho^2\) is the fraction of variance left unexplained. For the \(\Sigma\) above with \(\mu = 0\), \(X = x_1\), \(Y = x_2\): \(Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal N(0.4x,\ 2 - \tfrac45 = 1.2)\). The reverse regression is \(X \mid Y = y \sim \mathcal N(y,\ 5 - 2 = 3)\), slope 1, not \(1/0.4\): regressing \(X\) on \(Y\) does not invert regressing \(Y\) on \(X\).

Sampling and whitening with Cholesky

Factor \(\Sigma = LL^T\) with \(L\) lower triangular (L43). If \(z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)\) then \(x = \mu + Lz \sim \mathcal N(\mu, LL^T) = \mathcal N(\mu, \Sigma)\) by the affine rule: this is how correlated Gaussians are sampled. Run backwards, \(z = L^{-1}(x - \mu)\) whitens. For the running example, \(L = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt5 & 0 \\ 2/\sqrt5 & \sqrt{6/5}\end{pmatrix}\), and indeed \(LL^T = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & \tfrac45 + \tfrac65\end{pmatrix} = \Sigma\).

Common errors
  • Marginally normal taken for jointly normal. The \(Y = WX\) example has normal marginals and no joint density at all.
  • \(\Sigma\) in the exponent instead of \(\Sigma^{-1}\), or a missing \(|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\).
  • Semi-axes proportional to \(\lambda_i\). They scale with \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\), the standard deviations along the eigenvectors.
  • Expecting the conditional variance to depend on \(x_2\). For Gaussians it never does; only the mean moves.

Transformations of Random Variables

32m video45m practiceheavy lesson

To find the distribution of \(g(X)\), compute its CDF directly: that always works. When \(g\) is smooth and invertible the shortcut is to evaluate the old density at the preimage and multiply by the local stretch factor \(|d x/d y|\). Sums need convolution, extremes need powers of the CDF, and the delta method approximates the rest.

The CDF method

Write \(F_Y(y) = P(g(X) \le y) = P\big(X \in \{x : g(x) \le y\}\big)\), evaluate it with \(F_X\), then differentiate. Example: \(X \sim\) Uniform\((0,1)\) and \(Y = -\ln X\). For \(y > 0\), \(P(Y \le y) = P(X \ge e^{-y}) = 1 - e^{-y}\), so \(Y \sim\) Exp\((1)\). No formula to remember; only the event \(\{g(X) \le y\}\) to describe correctly.

Monotone maps: the change-of-variables formula

Theorem · Change of variables (one dimension)

Let \(g\) be strictly monotone and differentiable on the support of \(X\), with differentiable inverse \(h = g^{-1}\). Then on the range of \(g\),

$$f_Y(y) = f_X\big(h(y)\big)\,\big|h'(y)\big| = \frac{f_X(x)}{|g'(x)|}\Big|_{x = h(y)}.$$

The increasing case is today's Prove it. For decreasing \(g\), \(F_Y(y) = P(X \ge h(y)) = 1 - F_X(h(y))\), so \(f_Y = -f_X(h(y))\,h'(y)\), which is positive because \(h' < 0\): the absolute value covers both cases. Read it as conservation of probability: an interval of width \(dy\) around \(y\) comes from an interval of width \(|h'(y)|\,dy\) around \(x\), so \(f_Y\,dy = f_X\,dx\). Two examples: \(Y = aX + b\) has \(f_Y(y) = f_X\big((y - b)/a\big)/|a|\); \(Y = e^X\) with \(X \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)\) has \(h = \ln\), \(h' = 1/y\), giving the log-normal density \(\frac{1}{y\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(\ln y - \mu)^2/2\sigma^2}\) on \(y > 0\).

Non-monotone maps: add over the branches

If \(g\) is piecewise monotone, each solution \(x_i\) of \(g(x) = y\) contributes: \(f_Y(y) = \sum_i f_X(x_i)/|g'(x_i)|\).

Worked example · \(Y = X^2\) for \(X \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\)

For \(y > 0\) the roots are \(\pm\sqrt y\) and \(|g'(\pm\sqrt y)| = 2\sqrt y\):

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{\varphi(\sqrt y) + \varphi(-\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y} = \frac{\varphi(\sqrt y)}{\sqrt y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}\,e^{-y/2},$$

the \(\chi^2_1\) density, matching the CDF computation of L101.

Several variables: the Jacobian

Theorem · Multivariate change of variables

If \(y = g(x)\) is a differentiable bijection between open sets of \(\R^k\) with differentiable inverse \(h\), then \(f_Y(y) = f_X\big(h(y)\big)\,\big|\det J_h(y)\big|\), where \(J_h = \partial h/\partial y\) is the Jacobian matrix of L85.

Example: \(X, Y\) independent \(\mathcal N(0,1)\), in polar coordinates \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), \(|\det J| = r\). Then \(f(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\,r\) factorises: \(\Theta\) is uniform on \([0, 2\pi)\), \(R\) has density \(re^{-r^2/2}\), and they are independent. Running this backwards from two uniforms is the Box–Muller method for generating normals.

Sums, products and quotients

Theorem · Convolution

For independent continuous \(X, Y\): \(\displaystyle f_{X+Y}(s) = \int_\R f_X(x)\,f_Y(s - x)\,dx\).

Derivation. Condition on \(X\): \(P(X + Y \le s) = \int f_X(x)\,P(Y \le s - x)\,dx = \int f_X(x)\,F_Y(s - x)\,dx\). Differentiate in \(s\) under the integral.

Worked example · Two uniforms make a triangle

For \(X, Y \sim\) Uniform\((0,1)\) independent, the integrand \(\mathbf 1\{0 < x < 1\}\,\mathbf 1\{0 < s - x < 1\}\) is 1 exactly on \([\max(0, s-1), \min(1, s)]\), so \(f(s)\) is the length of that interval: \(f(s) = s\) on \([0,1]\) and \(2 - s\) on \([1,2]\).

When MGFs exist, \(M_{X+Y} = M_XM_Y\) (L102) is often quicker. For products and quotients the same conditioning argument gives \(f_{XY}(z) = \int f_X(x)f_Y(z/x)\,\frac{dx}{|x|}\) and \(f_{X/Y}(z) = \int f_X(zy)f_Y(y)\,|y|\,dy\). For two independent standard normals the second is \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{-(1+z^2)y^2/2}|y|\,dy = \frac{1}{\pi(1+z^2)}\): the ratio is Cauchy.

Order statistics

Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be i.i.d. with CDF \(F\) and density \(f\), sorted as \(X_{(1)} \le \cdots \le X_{(n)}\). The maximum is \(\le x\) exactly when all of them are, and the minimum exceeds \(x\) exactly when all of them do:

$$F_{\max}(x) = F(x)^n,\quad f_{\max} = nF^{n-1}f; \qquad P(X_{(1)} > x) = (1 - F(x))^n,\quad f_{\min} = n(1-F)^{n-1}f.$$

For the \(k\)-th order statistic, one observation sits at \(x\) (\(n\) choices, probability \(f(x)\,dx\)), \(k - 1\) below and \(n - k\) above: \(f_{(k)}(x) = \frac{n!}{(k-1)!\,(n-k)!}F(x)^{k-1}(1 - F(x))^{n-k}f(x)\).

Worked example · Maximum of \(n\) uniforms

\(F(x) = x\) on \([0,1]\), so \(f_{\max}(x) = nx^{n-1}\), the Beta\((n, 1)\) density, with \(\E X_{(n)} = \int_0^1 nx^n\,dx = \frac{n}{n+1}\). More generally the uniform \(X_{(k)} \sim\) Beta\((k, n - k + 1)\). L112 uses the maximum as the MLE of a uniform's endpoint.

The delta method

If \(X\) is concentrated near \(\mu\) and \(g\) is smooth, the first-order Taylor expansion of L69, \(g(X) \approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(X - \mu)\), gives \(\E g(X) \approx g(\mu)\) and \(\Var g(X) \approx g'(\mu)^2\Var X\). The asymptotic version: if \(\sqrt n(\bar X - \mu) \to \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\) and \(g'(\mu) \ne 0\), then \(\sqrt n\big(g(\bar X) - g(\mu)\big) \to \mathcal N\big(0, g'(\mu)^2\sigma^2\big)\). In several variables the factor becomes \(\nabla g^T\Sigma\nabla g\). This is where most standard errors in Phase 8 come from.

Worked example · Delta method for \(\log \bar X\)

\(g = \log\), \(g'(\mu) = 1/\mu\), \(\Var\bar X = \sigma^2/n\), so \(\Var(\log\bar X) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{n\mu^2}\) and \(\operatorname{SE}(\log\bar X) \approx \frac{\sigma/\mu}{\sqrt n}\), the coefficient of variation over \(\sqrt n\). With \(n = 100\), \(\mu \approx 50\), \(\sigma \approx 10\): about \(0.2/10 = 0.02\), i.e. roughly a 2% relative error. A second-order term shows \(\E\log\bar X \approx \log\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2n\mu^2}\), slightly below \(\log\mu\), as Jensen predicts (L107).

Common errors
  • Substituting without the Jacobian. \(f_X(h(y))\) alone is not a density; it usually does not integrate to 1.
  • Dropping the absolute value for decreasing maps, which produces a negative “density”.
  • Missing a branch, or the new support. \(Y = X^2\) needs both roots, and lives on \(y > 0\).
  • Convolution over all of \(\R\) while forgetting that the integrand vanishes outside both supports.
  • Delta method where it fails: at \(g'(\mu) = 0\), or when \(X\) is not concentrated.

L107 · DAY 90

Concentration & Classical Inequalities

34m video45m practice

When the exact distribution is unknown, inequalities still bound the probabilities that matter. Markov needs only a mean, Chebyshev a variance, Hoeffding boundedness and independence, and each extra assumption buys a dramatically thinner tail.

Markov and Chebyshev

Theorem · Markov and Chebyshev

Markov: if \(X \ge 0\) and \(a > 0\), then \(P(X \ge a) \le \E X / a\). Chebyshev: if \(\Var X = \sigma^2 < \infty\) and \(k > 0\), then \(P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2\). The proofs (today's Prove it) take three lines: Markov is the pointwise inequality \(X \ge a\,\mathbf 1\{X \ge a\}\) averaged, and Chebyshev is Markov applied to \((X - \mu)^2\).

Chebyshev is distribution-free: any distribution puts at least 75% of its mass within \(2\sigma\) of the mean and at least 89% within \(3\sigma\). The normal puts 95% and 99.7% there, so for well-behaved data the bound is loose. Neither bound can be improved in general:

  • Markov is tight for \(X = a\) with probability \(\mu/a\) and \(X = 0\) otherwise: then \(P(X \ge a) = \mu/a\) exactly.
  • Chebyshev is tight (for \(k \ge 1\)) for \(X = \mu \pm k\sigma\) with probability \(\tfrac{1}{2k^2}\) each and \(X = \mu\) otherwise: its variance is \(2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2k^2}\,k^2\sigma^2 = \sigma^2\) and \(P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) = 1/k^2\).

Markov's weakness is visible in its form: it decays only like \(1/a\), because a mean alone cannot rule out a small chance of a huge value.

Jensen and Cauchy–Schwarz

Theorem · Jensen's inequality

If \(g\) is convex and \(\E|X|\), \(\E|g(X)|\) are finite, then \(\E[g(X)] \ge g(\E X)\). For concave \(g\) the inequality reverses.

Proof. A convex function has a supporting line at \(\mu = \E X\) (L90): some \(c\) gives \(g(x) \ge g(\mu) + c(x - \mu)\) for all \(x\). Substitute \(x = X\) and take expectations: \(\E g(X) \ge g(\mu) + c\cdot 0\).

Corollaries: \(\E X^2 \ge (\E X)^2\) (\(g = x^2\)); \(\E[1/X] \ge 1/\E X\) for \(X > 0\); AM–GM, since \(\log\) is concave, \(\E\log X \le \log\E X\), and taking \(X\) uniform on \(\{a_1, \dots, a_n\}\) gives \(\big(\prod a_i\big)^{1/n} \le \frac1n\sum a_i\); \(\E\max_i \hat Q_i \ge \max_i \E\hat Q_i\) (\(\max\) is convex), the source of maximisation bias; and the non-negativity of KL divergence in L118. Cauchy–Schwarz, \((\E XY)^2 \le \E X^2\,\E Y^2\), was proved in L104.

The union bound

Theorem · Union bound (Boole)

\(P\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big) \le \sum_i P(A_i)\) for any countable collection, with no independence needed.

Proof. \(B_i = A_i \setminus \bigcup_{j < i}A_j\) are disjoint, \(B_i \subseteq A_i\), and \(\bigcup B_i = \bigcup A_i\), so \(P(\bigcup A_i) = \sum P(B_i) \le \sum P(A_i)\).

Run \(m\) tests at level \(\alpha\) each: the chance of at least one false alarm is at most \(m\alpha\), so testing each at \(\alpha/m\) controls it at \(\alpha\) (Bonferroni, L115). The same move turns a bound for one classifier into a bound for a finite class \(\mathcal H\), at the price of a \(\log|\mathcal H|\) term.

Chernoff bounds and Hoeffding's inequality

Algorithm · The Chernoff method
  1. For any \(\lambda > 0\), \(\{X \ge a\} = \{e^{\lambda X} \ge e^{\lambda a}\}\), so Markov gives \(P(X \ge a) \le e^{-\lambda a}M_X(\lambda)\).
  2. Minimise the right side over \(\lambda\). For \(Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\): \(e^{-\lambda a + \lambda^2/2}\) is smallest at \(\lambda = a\), giving \(P(Z \ge a) \le e^{-a^2/2}\).
  3. For a sum of independent terms, \(M_{\sum X_i} = \prod M_{X_i}\) (L102), so the bound decays exponentially in \(n\).
Theorem · Hoeffding's inequality

If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are independent with \(a_i \le X_i \le b_i\), then for \(t > 0\)

$$P\big(\bar X - \E\bar X \ge t\big) \le \exp\Big(-\frac{2n^2t^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}\Big), \qquad \text{so for } [0,1]\text{-valued terms } P\big(|\bar X - \mu| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-2nt^2}.$$

The proof is the Chernoff method plus Hoeffding's lemma, \(\E e^{\lambda(X - \E X)} \le e^{\lambda^2(b-a)^2/8}\) for \(X \in [a, b]\) (stated here without proof).

A variable is sub-Gaussian with parameter \(\sigma\) if \(\E e^{\lambda(X - \mu)} \le e^{\lambda^2\sigma^2/2}\) for all \(\lambda\); its tails are then at most \(2e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\), like a normal's. Hoeffding's lemma says bounded variables are sub-Gaussian with \(\sigma = (b - a)/2\). This is the engine of generalisation bounds: a fixed classifier's empirical error is within about \(\sqrt{\log(2/\delta)/2n}\) of its true error with probability \(1 - \delta\).

Worked example · \(P(X \ge 3\mu)\) three ways for \(X \sim\) Poisson\((10)\)
  • Markov: \(P(X \ge 30) \le 10/30 \approx 0.33\).
  • Chebyshev: \(P(X \ge 30) \le P(|X - 10| \ge 20) \le 10/400 = 0.025\).
  • Chernoff: \(M(\lambda) = e^{10(e^\lambda - 1)}\); minimising \(-30\lambda + 10(e^\lambda - 1)\) gives \(e^\lambda = 3\), and the bound \(e^{20}/3^{30} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-6}\).
  • Exact: \(P(X \ge 30) \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-7}\).

Each extra assumption gains orders of magnitude, and Chernoff has the right exponential rate while being off by a constant factor of about 10. Chernoff is not automatically best: for \(X \sim\) Exp\((1)\), \(P(X \ge 3) \le 3e^{-2} \approx 0.41\) by Chernoff, worse than Markov's \(\tfrac13\). Its strength is deep tails and sums.

Worked example · Sample size from Hoeffding

How many independent yes/no responses guarantee that \(\hat p\) is within \(0.05\) of \(p\) with probability at least 0.95? Require \(2e^{-2n(0.05)^2} \le 0.05\): \(n \ge \ln(40)/0.005 \approx 737.8\), so \(n = 738\). Chebyshev with \(\Var\hat p \le \tfrac1{4n}\) needs \(\tfrac{1}{4n(0.0025)} \le 0.05\), i.e. \(n \ge 2000\). The CLT suggests about 385 (L114), but that is an approximation, not a guarantee.

L108 · DAY 91

Limit Theorems, Markov Chains & Phase 7 Workshop

42m video60m practice

Averages of many independent draws stop being random (the law of large numbers), and their remaining fluctuations are Gaussian on the scale \(1/\sqrt n\) (the central limit theorem). Markov chains replace independence with one-step memory, and their long-run behaviour is an eigenvector problem.

Modes of convergence

Definition · Four ways for \(X_n \to X\)
  • In distribution: \(F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x)\) at every point where \(F_X\) is continuous.
  • In probability: \(P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).
  • Almost surely: \(P(X_n \to X) = 1\).
  • In \(L^2\): \(\E|X_n - X|^2 \to 0\).

Almost sure ⇒ in probability ⇒ in distribution, and \(L^2\) ⇒ in probability (Markov on \(|X_n - X|^2\)). Nothing else holds in general:

  • Distribution, not probability: \(X \sim \mathcal N(0,1)\), \(X_n = -X\): same law, yet \(|X_n - X| = 2|X|\).
  • \(L^2\), not almost surely: independent \(X_n \sim\) Bernoulli\((1/n)\) have \(\E X_n^2 = 1/n \to 0\), but \(\sum 1/n = \infty\), so (second Borel–Cantelli lemma) \(X_n = 1\) infinitely often.
  • Almost surely, not \(L^2\): \(X_n = n\,\mathbf 1\{U < 1/n\}\), \(U\) uniform: eventually 0 for each \(U > 0\), but \(\E X_n^2 = n\).

Laws of large numbers

For i.i.d. \(X_i\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), \(\bar X_n = \frac1n\sum_{i \le n}X_i\) has \(\E\bar X_n = \mu\) and \(\Var\bar X_n = \sigma^2/n\) (L110).

Theorem · Weak and strong laws

WLLN: \(\bar X_n \to \mu\) in probability (today's Prove it). SLLN: \(\bar X_n \to \mu\) almost surely, assuming only \(\E|X_1| < \infty\) (stated).

The weak law says a deviation is unlikely at each large \(n\); the strong law says that, with probability 1, the running average eventually enters the band \(\mu \pm \varepsilon\) and never leaves. The \(\sigma/\sqrt n\) spread is why more data helps with diminishing returns: 100 times the data buys 10 times the precision.

The central limit theorem

Theorem · CLT

If \(X_i\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(0 < \sigma^2 < \infty\), then \(Z_n = \sqrt n(\bar X_n - \mu)/\sigma\) converges in distribution to \(\mathcal N(0,1)\): \(P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z)\) for every \(z\).

Proof sketch · via MGFs

Assume an MGF exists near 0. The standardised \(Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma\) have mean 0 and variance 1, so \(M_Y(t) = 1 + \tfrac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2)\). Since \(Z_n = \sum_i Y_i/\sqrt n\),

$$M_{Z_n}(t) = M_Y\big(t/\sqrt n\big)^n = \Big(1 + \frac{t^2}{2n} + o\big(\tfrac1n\big)\Big)^n \longrightarrow e^{t^2/2},$$

the MGF of \(\mathcal N(0,1)\). A continuity theorem (convergence of MGFs near 0, or of characteristic functions, implies convergence in distribution) finishes the proof. With characteristic functions the same computation needs only a finite variance.

What it does not say: nothing about the data becoming normal (only the standardised sum is), nothing at a fixed small \(n\), and nothing without a finite variance (Cauchy averages stay Cauchy). The Berry–Esseen bound gives the rate: \(\sup_z|P(Z_n \le z) - \Phi(z)| \le C\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt n)\), \(\rho = \E|X - \mu|^3\), with an absolute constant \(C\) known to be below 0.5. Skewed populations converge slowly.

Worked example · CLT for a sample mean, and for a binomial

A population has mean 50 and standard deviation 12; take \(n = 36\). Then \(\operatorname{SE} = 12/6 = 2\) and \(P(\bar X > 53) \approx P(Z > 1.5) = 0.0668\). For \(X \sim\) Binomial\((100, 0.5)\), mean 50 and sd 5, the continuity correction treats the integer 55 as the interval up to 55.5: \(P(X \le 55) \approx \Phi(0.5 \cdot 11/5) = \Phi(1.1) \approx 0.8643\), against the exact 0.8644. Without the correction, \(\Phi(1.0) \approx 0.841\).

Markov chains

Definition · Markov chain

A sequence \(X_0, X_1, \dots\) on a finite set \(S\) with \(P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i, X_{n-1}, \dots, X_0) = P_{ij}\): the future depends on the past only through the present. \(P\) is a stochastic matrix: non-negative entries, rows summing to 1.

Chapman–Kolmogorov: conditioning on the state at time \(m\), \(P^{(m+n)}_{ij} = \sum_k P^{(m)}_{ik}P^{(n)}_{kj}\), so \(n\)-step probabilities are entries of \(P^n\) (L55) and distributions evolve as \(\pi_n = \pi_0P^n\). Classification: the chain is irreducible if every state is accessible (\(P^n_{ij} > 0\) for some \(n\)) from every other; a state is recurrent if return is certain, transient otherwise; its period is \(\gcd\{n : P^n_{ii} > 0\}\), and a self-loop makes it aperiodic.

Theorem · Stationary distribution

\(\pi\) is stationary if \(\pi P = \pi\). A finite, irreducible, aperiodic chain has a unique, positive \(\pi\), and every row of \(P^n\) converges to it. Detailed balance \(\pi_iP_{ij} = \pi_jP_{ji}\) implies stationarity, since \(\sum_i \pi_iP_{ij} = \pi_j\sum_i P_{ji} = \pi_j\); MCMC samplers are built to satisfy it. For diagonalisable \(P\) the distance to \(\pi\) shrinks like \(|\lambda_2|^n\), \(\lambda_2\) the second-largest eigenvalue in modulus.

Worked example · Stationary distribution and \(P^5\)

\(P = \begin{pmatrix} .5 & .5 & 0 \\ .25 & .5 & .25 \\ 0 & .5 & .5\end{pmatrix}\). The equations \(\pi P = \pi\) read \(\pi_1 = .5\pi_1 + .25\pi_2\) and \(\pi_3 = .25\pi_2 + .5\pi_3\), so \(\pi_2 = 2\pi_1\), \(\pi_3 = \pi_1\), and normalising, \(\pi = (\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14)\).

Eigenvalues \(1, \tfrac12, 0\) (trace 1.5, determinant 0); right eigenvectors \((1,1,1), (1,0,-1), (1,-1,1)\); left eigenvectors \(\pi, (1,0,-1), (1,-2,1)\). From \(P^n = \sum_k \lambda_k^n\,v_kw_k/(w_kv_k)\), for \(n \ge 1\)

$$P^n = \mathbf 1\pi + \Big(\frac12\Big)^n\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad P^5 = \begin{pmatrix} 17/64 & 1/2 & 15/64 \\ 1/4 & 1/2 & 1/4 \\ 15/64 & 1/2 & 17/64\end{pmatrix}.$$

At \(n = 1\) this reproduces \(P\), a useful check, and every row tends to \(\pi\).
