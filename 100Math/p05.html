Multivariable & Vector Calculus

Phase 5 extends calculus from one variable to many: gradients, Jacobians and Hessians, the chain rule as matrix multiplication, optimization with and without constraints, and integration over regions, curves and surfaces. It combines the linear algebra of Phase 3 with the calculus of Phase 4 into the toolkit machine learning runs on, and it supplies the gradients and KKT conditions that Phase 6 builds optimization theory on and the multiple integrals and change-of-variables formula that Phase 7 uses for joint densities.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Read a contour map, identify any quadric surface from its traces, and convert between Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical coordinates.
  • Show that a multivariable limit fails with two paths, or prove that it exists with a direction-free polar bound.
  • Compute gradients, directional derivatives, tangent planes, Jacobians and Hessians, and hand-propagate derivatives through a composition in reverse mode.
  • Classify critical points by the signs of the Hessian's eigenvalues and find absolute extrema on closed bounded regions.
  • Solve constrained problems with Lagrange multipliers and the KKT conditions, and interpret each multiplier as a shadow price.
  • Set up double and triple integrals in either order and in polar, cylindrical or spherical coordinates, and transform a probability density with the Jacobian determinant.
  • Test a vector field for conservativeness, evaluate line integrals, and apply Green's, Stokes' and the divergence theorems.

L75 · DAY 64

Functions of Several Variables

32m video45m practice

A function of several variables cannot be drawn once \(n > 2\), so you learn to see it through its slices: level sets, traces and cross-sections. Reading contour maps fluently is what makes gradients, saddles and loss landscapes intuitive later in this phase.

Scalar fields and vector fields

Definition

A scalar field is a function \(f: D \subseteq \R^n \to \R\). A vector field (vector-valued map) is \(F: D \subseteq \R^n \to \R^m\), \(F = (F_1, \dots, F_m)\), each \(F_i\) a scalar field. The domain \(D\) is where the formula makes sense; the range is \(\{f(x) : x \in D\}\).

A training loss \(L(w)\) with \(d\) parameters is a scalar field on \(\R^d\). A network layer \(F(x) = \sigma(Wx + b)\), \(W \in \R^{m \times n}\), is a map \(\R^n \to \R^m\), and a network is a composition of them, which is why the Jacobian chain rule (L79) is backpropagation. To sketch a domain in \(\R^2\), write each restriction as an inequality, draw its boundary curve, and dash the boundary when the inequality is strict.

Graphs, level sets and contour maps

The graph of \(f: \R^n \to \R\) is \(\{(x, f(x))\} \subset \R^{n+1}\): a surface in \(\R^3\) when \(n = 2\), undrawable once \(n \ge 3\). So we stay in the domain.

Definition · level sets

The level set at height \(c\) is \(\{x \in D : f(x) = c\}\). In \(\R^2\) these are level curves, and several of them labelled by \(c\) form a contour map. In \(\R^3\) they are level surfaces: \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = c\) gives spheres.

Draw contours at equally spaced heights. If neighbours are \(\Delta s\) apart for a height step \(\Delta c\), the slope is roughly \(\Delta c / \Delta s\): crowded contours mean steep, sparse contours mean flat. L78 sharpens this: the gradient is perpendicular to the contours with length equal to the slope. A trace is the graph cut by a plane: horizontal traces \(z = k\) are lifted level curves; vertical traces \(x = k\) or \(y = k\) are ordinary 2D curves. Traces identify a surface by hand.

Worked example

Domain and contours of \(f(x,y) = \ln(4 - x^2 - y^2)\). Domain: \(4 - x^2 - y^2 > 0\), the open disc \(x^2 + y^2 < 4\), rim excluded. Range: \(4 - x^2 - y^2\) fills \((0, 4]\), so \(f\) fills \((-\infty, \ln 4]\), maximum at the origin.

Contours: \(\ln(4 - r^2) = c \iff r^2 = 4 - e^c\), circles of radius \(\sqrt{4 - e^c}\) for every \(c < \ln 4\). For \(c = 1, 0, -1, -2\) the radii are \(1.13, 1.73, 1.91, 1.97\): equal steps in \(c\), shrinking gaps in \(r\), so the surface plunges toward the rim.

The quadric surface catalogue

A quadric surface is the zero set of a second-degree polynomial in \(x, y, z\). After a rotation and translation each non-degenerate one is one of these (\(a, b, c > 0\); horizontal traces are the slices \(z = k\)):

Algorithm · identify a quadric from its traces
  1. A variable missing: a cylinder over the curve in the other two.
  2. A variable appearing only linearly: a paraboloid along that axis; equal signs on the squares give elliptic, opposite signs hyperbolic.
  3. Otherwise squares on the left. Right side 1: no minus signs is an ellipsoid, one is a hyperboloid of one sheet, two give two sheets (axis = the odd variable). Right side 0 with mixed signs: a cone. Confirm with two traces.
Worked example

Identify \(z = x^2 - y^2\). Traces \(z = k\): \(x^2 - y^2 = k\), hyperbolas opening along \(x\) for \(k > 0\), along \(y\) for \(k < 0\), and the lines \(y = \pm x\) at \(k = 0\). Trace \(y = k\): \(z = x^2 - k^2\), parabolas opening up. Trace \(x = k\): \(z = k^2 - y^2\), opening down. Up one way, down the other: a hyperbolic paraboloid, the saddle.

The saddle is the most important shape in optimization: flat at the origin, a minimum along \(x\), a maximum along \(y\), and not an extremum. In high-dimensional losses most flat points are saddles (L81).

Cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Definition

Cylindrical \((r, \theta, z)\): \(x = r\cos\theta,\ y = r\sin\theta,\ z = z\), \(r \ge 0\), \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\). Spherical \((\rho, \varphi, \theta)\): \(x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\ y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\ z = \rho\cos\varphi\), with \(\varphi \in [0, \pi]\) measured down from the positive \(z\)-axis and \(\theta\) the cylindrical azimuth. Then \(\rho^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) and \(r = \rho\sin\varphi\). Physics texts often swap the names \(\theta\), \(\varphi\): always say which angle is measured from the \(z\)-axis.

Worked example

Convert \((1, 1, \sqrt 2)\). Cylindrical: \(r = \sqrt 2\); \((1,1)\) is in the first quadrant so \(\theta = \pi/4\); \(z = \sqrt 2\). Spherical: \(\rho = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 2} = 2\), \(\cos\varphi = \sqrt2/2\) so \(\varphi = \pi/4\), \(\theta = \pi/4\). Check: \(\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta = 2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} = 1\) ✓ and \(\rho\cos\varphi = \sqrt 2\) ✓.

Common errors
  • Mixing the two pictures. A contour map lives in the domain (the \(xy\)-plane); the graph is a surface in \(\R^3\).
  • Closing an open domain. \(\ln(4 - x^2 - y^2)\) is undefined on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\).
  • Calling \(z = x^2\) a parabola. In \(\R^3\) it is a parabolic cylinder: \(y\) is free.
  • \(\theta = \arctan(y/x)\) without a quadrant check. \((-1, -1)\) has \(\theta = 5\pi/4\), not \(\pi/4\).
  • Miscounting minus signs. With right side 1, one minus sign is one sheet; two are two sheets.

Practice · 45 min

  1. Prove it. The level curves of the plane \(ax + by + cz = d\) (\(c \ne 0\), \((a, b) \ne (0,0)\)) are parallel straight lines, equally spaced for equally spaced heights. Hint: solve for \(z\), set \(z = k\), compare normals.
    Proof sketch

    The plane is the graph of \(z = (d - ax - by)/c\). Its level curve at height \(k\) is \(ax + by = d - ck\): a line with normal \((a, b)\), the same for every \(k\), hence parallel lines, distinct for distinct \(k\). The distance between levels \(k\) and \(k'\) is \(|c|\,|k - k'|/\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\), which depends only on \(k - k'\): equal height steps, equal spacing, constant steepness.

  2. Sketch the domain of \(f(x, y) = \sqrt{y - x^2} + \ln x\).
    Answer

    \(y \ge x^2\) and \(x > 0\): on or above the parabola, strictly right of the \(y\)-axis.

  3. Identify \(4x^2 - y^2 + z^2 = 4\) and \(x^2 + 4y^2 - z^2 + 4 = 0\).
    Answer

    \(x^2 - y^2/4 + z^2/4 = 1\): hyperboloid of one sheet, axis \(y\). \(z^2/4 - x^2/4 - y^2 = 1\): two sheets, axis \(z\), nothing with \(|z| < 2\).

  4. Convert spherical \((4, \pi/3, \pi/2)\) to Cartesian and cylindrical.
    Answer

    \((0, 2\sqrt3, 2)\); cylindrical \((2\sqrt3, \pi/2, 2)\).

  5. Draw contours of \(x^2 + 4y^2\) at \(c = 1, 4, 9\). Is \(f\) steeper along \(x\) or \(y\)?
    Answer

    Ellipses with semi-axes \(\sqrt c\) and \(\sqrt c/2\): contours are twice as close along the \(y\)-axis, so steeper in \(y\).

Limits & Continuity in ℝⁿ

28m video40m practiceheavy lesson

In one variable you can approach a point from two sides; in the plane you can approach it along infinitely many paths, and a limit must agree along all of them. Paths can only ever disprove a limit; to prove one you need a bound that does not depend on the direction of approach.

Neighbourhoods and the shape of sets

Definition

The open ball of radius \(r\) about \(a\) is \(B(a, r) = \{x \in \R^n : \|x - a\| < r\}\). For a set \(S \subseteq \R^n\), a point \(a\) is an interior point if some ball \(B(a, r) \subseteq S\), and a boundary point if every ball about \(a\) meets both \(S\) and its complement. \(S\) is open if every point of \(S\) is interior, closed if it contains all its boundary points (equivalently, its complement is open), and bounded if \(S \subseteq B(0, R)\) for some \(R\).

The open disc \(x^2 + y^2 < 1\) is open and bounded, with boundary the unit circle; the closed disc is closed and bounded; the half-plane \(x > 0\) is open and unbounded; the annulus \(1 \le x^2 + y^2 < 4\) is neither open nor closed. \(\R^n\) and \(\varnothing\) are both open and closed, so "closed" does not mean "not open".

The definition, and why it is so demanding

Definition · limit

Let \(a\) be a limit point of the domain of \(f: D \subseteq \R^n \to \R\). Then \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) means: for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(\delta > 0\) such that \(x \in D\) and \(0 < \|x - a\| < \delta\) imply \(|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon\).

The condition \(0 < \|x - a\| < \delta\) is a whole punctured ball, so \(x\) may approach \(a\) along lines, parabolas, spirals, or by jumping around. If the limit exists, then for every path \(\gamma(t) \to a\) inside the domain, \(f(\gamma(t)) \to L\). The contrapositive is the workhorse for disproof.

Theorem · path test

If \(f \to L_1\) along one path into \(a\) and \(f \to L_2 \ne L_1\) along another (or fails to converge along some path), then \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\) does not exist. Agreement along any number of paths proves nothing.

Worked example

\(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0} \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}\) does not exist. Along the \(x\)-axis (\(y = 0\)): \(f = 0/x^2 = 0\). Along \(y = x\): \(f = x^2/(2x^2) = \tfrac12\). Two paths, two values, no limit. More generally along \(y = mx\) the value is \(m/(1 + m^2)\), and in polar form \(f = \cos\theta\sin\theta = \tfrac12\sin 2\theta\): the function is constant on each ray and different on different rays.

Lines are not enough. For \(g(x, y) = x^2y/(x^4 + y^2)\), along \(y = mx\): \(g = mx^3/(x^4 + m^2x^2) = mx/(x^2 + m^2) \to 0\) for every \(m\), and \(g = 0\) on the \(y\)-axis. But along the parabola \(y = kx^2\): \(g = kx^4/(x^4 + k^2x^4) = k/(1 + k^2)\), which is \(\tfrac12\) for \(k = 1\). Every line gives 0; the limit does not exist.

Proving a limit exists: polar coordinates and squeezing

Theorem · squeeze in ℝⁿ

If \(|f(x) - L| \le g(x)\) for all \(x\) in a punctured ball about \(a\), and \(g(x) \to 0\), then \(f(x) \to L\). The polar technique is the special case at the origin of \(\R^2\): write \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\) and find a bound \(|f - L| \le h(r)\) with \(h(r) \to 0\) that does not depend on \(\theta\).

Worked example

\(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0} \frac{x^2y}{x^2 + y^2} = 0\). In polar coordinates, for \(r > 0\): \(\dfrac{r^2\cos^2\theta \cdot r\sin\theta}{r^2} = r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\), so \(|f| \le r\) for every \(\theta\). As \(r = \|(x,y)\| \to 0\), \(|f| \to 0\). In \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) form: given \(\varepsilon\), take \(\delta = \varepsilon\); then \(0 < r < \delta\) gives \(|f| \le r < \varepsilon\).

The uniformity in \(\theta\) is essential. For \(g = x^2y/(x^4 + y^2)\), polar form gives \(r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta/(r^2\cos^4\theta + \sin^2\theta)\), which tends to 0 for each fixed \(\theta\) but not uniformly: near \(\theta = 0\) the denominator is tiny. That is exactly the parabola escaping through the gap.

Continuity and the Extreme Value Theorem

Definition · continuity

\(f\) is continuous at \(a \in D\) if \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)\), and continuous on \(D\) if continuous at every point. Sums, products, quotients (where the denominator is nonzero) and compositions of continuous functions are continuous; the coordinate maps \(x \mapsto x_i\) are continuous; hence every polynomial, and every formula built from \(\exp\), \(\ln\), \(\sin\), roots and so on, is continuous wherever it is defined.

Theorem · Extreme Value Theorem in ℝⁿ

If \(K \subset \R^n\) is closed and bounded and \(f: K \to \R\) is continuous, then \(f\) attains a maximum and a minimum on \(K\).

Both hypotheses are needed: \(x^2 + y^2\) on the open unit disc has no maximum (the supremum 1 lives on the missing boundary), and \(f(x, y) = x\) on the closed but unbounded plane has neither. This theorem is the existence guarantee behind every "check the interior and the boundary" problem in L81 and every Lagrange problem on a compact constraint set in L82. Its proof needs the completeness of \(\R\) (Bolzano–Weierstrass), revisited in L121.

Common errors
  • Testing only straight lines. \(x^2y/(x^4 + y^2)\) passes every line test and still has no limit.
  • Concluding existence from agreeing paths. Paths disprove; only a direction-free bound proves.
  • A polar bound that depends on \(\theta\). \(|f| \le r/\sin^2\theta\) is not a proof, because the factor blows up near \(\theta = 0\).
  • Using the value at the point. The limit ignores \(f(a)\); continuity is the extra demand that the two agree.
  • Applying the EVT to an open or unbounded set. Without both closed and bounded, a maximum may not exist.

Practice · 40 min

  1. Prove it. If a multivariable limit exists, it is unique. Hint: assume two limits \(L \ne M\) and use \(\varepsilon = |L - M|/2\).
    Proof sketch

    Suppose \(f \to L\) and \(f \to M\) with \(L \ne M\), and let \(\varepsilon = |L - M|/2 > 0\). Choose \(\delta_1\), \(\delta_2\) for \(L\) and \(M\) and let \(\delta = \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)\). Because \(a\) is a limit point of the domain, some \(x \in D\) has \(0 < \|x - a\| < \delta\). Then \(|L - M| \le |L - f(x)| + |f(x) - M| < 2\varepsilon = |L - M|\), a contradiction.

  2. Does \(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0} \frac{x^2 - y^2}{x^2 + y^2}\) exist?
    Answer

    No: 1 along the \(x\)-axis, \(-1\) along the \(y\)-axis.

  3. Evaluate \(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0} \frac{x^3 + y^3}{x^2 + y^2}\).
    Answer

    Polar: \(r(\cos^3\theta + \sin^3\theta)\), bounded by \(2r\) in absolute value, so the limit is 0.

  4. Does \(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0} \frac{xy^2}{x^2 + y^4}\) exist?
    Answer

    No. Along \(y = mx\) it is \(m^2x/(1 + m^4x^2) \to 0\), but along \(x = y^2\) it is \(y^4/(2y^4) = \tfrac12\).

  5. Let \(f(x,y) = \sin(x^2 + y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\) with \(f(0,0) = 1\). Is \(f\) continuous at the origin? Classify \(\{(x, y): 1 \le x^2 + y^2 < 4\}\).
    Answer

    Yes: with \(u = r^2 \to 0\), \(\sin u/u \to 1 = f(0,0)\). The annulus is bounded, neither open nor closed; its boundary is both circles.

L77 · DAY 65

Partial Derivatives

32m video40m practiceheavy lesson

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative taken along one coordinate axis with every other variable frozen. It is easy to compute and it measures the sensitivity to one input, but on its own it sees only the axes, which is why existence of all partials does not even guarantee continuity.

Definition and geometry

Definition

For \(f(x, y)\), the partial derivatives at \((a, b)\) are

$$f_x(a, b) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h, b) - f(a, b)}{h}, \qquad f_y(a, b) = \lim_{k \to 0} \frac{f(a, b + k) - f(a, b)}{k},$$

when the limits exist. Notations: \(f_x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \partial_x f = D_1 f\). For \(f(x_1, \dots, x_n)\), \(\partial f/\partial x_i\) varies \(x_i\) alone.

Geometrically, freeze \(y = b\): the plane \(y = b\) cuts the surface \(z = f(x, y)\) in the trace curve \(z = f(x, b)\), and \(f_x(a, b)\) is the slope of that curve at \(x = a\). To compute, treat every other variable as a constant and differentiate by the one-variable rules: for \(f = x^2y + \sin(xz)\), \(f_x = 2xy + z\cos(xz)\), \(f_y = x^2\), \(f_z = x\cos(xz)\).

As a sensitivity, \(\partial L/\partial w_i\) answers "how much does the loss change per unit nudge of this one weight, all others held fixed?" Gradient descent (L91) nudges every weight by its own sensitivity at once.

Higher-order partials and Clairaut's theorem

Differentiate again: \(f_{xx} = (f_x)_x\), \(f_{xy} = (f_x)_y\), \(f_{yx} = (f_y)_x\), \(f_{yy}\). In Leibniz notation \(f_{xy} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\,\partial x}\): subscripts read left to right, the fraction reads right to left.

Theorem · Clairaut–Schwarz

If \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) exist on an open set containing \((a, b)\) and are continuous at \((a, b)\), then \(f_{xy}(a, b) = f_{yx}(a, b)\). In particular every \(C^2\) function has equal mixed partials, in any number of variables.

Proof

Consider the second difference \(\Delta(h, k) = f(a+h, b+k) - f(a+h, b) - f(a, b+k) + f(a, b)\). With \(g(x) = f(x, b+k) - f(x, b)\), \(\Delta = g(a+h) - g(a)\); the mean value theorem twice gives \(\Delta = h\,[f_x(a + \theta_1 h, b+k) - f_x(a + \theta_1 h, b)] = hk\, f_{xy}(a + \theta_1 h, b + \theta_2 k)\) for some \(\theta_1, \theta_2 \in (0,1)\). Grouping the four terms the other way, with \(q(y) = f(a+h, y) - f(a, y)\), gives \(\Delta = hk\, f_{yx}(a + \theta_4 h, b + \theta_3 k)\). Divide by \(hk\) and let \((h, k) \to 0\): continuity of both mixed partials at \((a,b)\) forces them to the same limit.

The hypothesis matters. For \(f = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\), \(f(0,0) = 0\): \(f_x(0, y) = \lim_h f(h, y)/h = -y\) and \(f_y(x, 0) = x\), so \(f_{xy}(0,0) = -1\) while \(f_{yx}(0,0) = +1\). The mixed partials exist everywhere but are not continuous at the origin.

Worked example

All second partials of \(f = x^3y^2 - e^{xy}\). \(f_x = 3x^2y^2 - y e^{xy}\), \(f_y = 2x^3y - x e^{xy}\) (chain rule on \(e^{xy}\): the inner derivative is \(y\), respectively \(x\)).

\(f_{xx} = 6xy^2 - y^2e^{xy}\), \(f_{yy} = 2x^3 - x^2e^{xy}\).

\(f_{xy} = \partial_y(3x^2y^2 - ye^{xy}) = 6x^2y - (1 + xy)e^{xy}\) (product rule on \(y \cdot e^{xy}\)); \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(2x^3y - xe^{xy}) = 6x^2y - (1 + xy)e^{xy}\). Equal, as Clairaut promises for this \(C^\infty\) function.

Partials do not imply continuity; differentiability does

Worked example

Both partials exist, \(f\) is discontinuous. Let \(f = xy/(x^2 + y^2)\) with \(f(0,0) = 0\). Then \(f(h, 0) = 0\) for all \(h\), so \(f_x(0,0) = \lim_h 0/h = 0\); likewise \(f_y(0,0) = 0\). But L76 showed \(f = \tfrac12\) along \(y = x\), so \(f\) has no limit at the origin and is not continuous there.

Definition · differentiability

\(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if there is a linear approximation with vanishing relative error: \(f(a + h) = f(a) + \nabla f(a) \cdot h + o(\|h\|)\), i.e. \(\frac{f(a+h) - f(a) - \nabla f(a)\cdot h}{\|h\|} \to 0\). Differentiable implies continuous (the error term tends to 0). A convenient sufficient condition: all partials exist near \(a\) and are continuous at \(a\). L78 develops this.

The total differential and linear approximation

For a differentiable \(z = f(x, y)\), the total differential is \(dz = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy\), and \(f(a + \Delta x, b + \Delta y) \approx f(a,b) + f_x \Delta x + f_y \Delta y\). Example: \(f = x^2y\) at \((3, 2)\) has \(f = 18\), \(f_x = 2xy = 12\), \(f_y = x^2 = 9\), so \(f(3.02, 1.97) \approx 18 + 12(0.02) + 9(-0.03) = 17.97\); the exact value is \(17.967\ldots\)

Common errors
  • Dropping the inner derivative. \(\partial_x e^{xy} = ye^{xy}\), not \(e^{xy}\).
  • Notation order. \(f_{xy}\) means \(x\) first, then \(y\); \(\partial^2 f/\partial y\,\partial x\) means the same. Only Clairaut makes the order irrelevant.
  • Assuming Clairaut for piecewise formulas. At the seam point, check continuity of the mixed partials or compute both from the limit definition.
  • "Partials exist, so \(f\) is differentiable." Partials see two lines; differentiability needs every direction at once.
  • Power rule in the exponent. \(\partial_y x^y \ne yx^{y-1}\) (see Prove it).

Practice · 40 min

  1. Prove it. For \(f(x, y) = x^y\) (\(x > 0\)) compute \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) and explain why they look so different. Hint: freeze one variable and ask what kind of function remains; then rewrite \(x^y = e^{y\ln x}\).
    Proof sketch

    With \(y\) frozen, \(x^y\) is a power function, so \(f_x = yx^{y-1}\). With \(x\) frozen, it is an exponential with base \(x\), so \(f_y = x^y\ln x\). The single formula \(e^{y\ln x}\) gives both: \(f_x = e^{y\ln x}\cdot y/x = yx^{y-1}\), \(f_y = e^{y\ln x}\ln x\). Check Clairaut: \(f_{xy} = x^{y-1} + yx^{y-1}\ln x\) and \(f_{yx} = yx^{y-1}\ln x + x^{y-1}\), equal.

  2. For \(f = xe^{yz} + \ln(xz)\) find \(f_x, f_y, f_z\) and \(f_{xz}\).
    Answer

The Gradient & Directional Derivatives

36m video50m practice

The gradient packs all first-order information about \(f\) into one vector: the rate of change in any direction is a dot product with it, so it points uphill as steeply as possible, its length is that steepest slope, and it is perpendicular to the level sets. Gradient descent is nothing more than walking along \(-\nabla f\).

Directional derivatives and the gradient

Definition

For a unit vector \(u \in \R^n\), the directional derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) is \(D_u f(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(a + tu) - f(a)}{t}\). The gradient is the vector of partials, \(\nabla f(a) = \big(\partial_1 f(a), \dots, \partial_n f(a)\big)\). With \(u = e_i\), \(D_{e_i} f = \partial_i f\).

Definition · differentiability in ℝⁿ

\(f\) is differentiable at \(a\) if \(f(a + h) = f(a) + \nabla f(a)^\top h + R(h)\) with \(R(h)/\|h\| \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\). The affine map \(h \mapsto f(a) + \nabla f(a)^\top h\) is the linear approximation and \(df = \nabla f^\top dx = \sum_i \partial_i f\, dx_i\) is the differential. Continuous partials near \(a\) suffice.

Theorem · directional derivative formula

If \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), then for every unit \(u\), \(D_u f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u\).

Proof

Put \(h = tu\) in the definition: \(f(a + tu) - f(a) = t\,\nabla f(a)\cdot u + R(tu)\). Divide by \(t\): the last term is \(\pm\|u\|\,R(tu)/\|tu\| \to 0\) because \(\|tu\| = |t| \to 0\). So the difference quotient tends to \(\nabla f(a)\cdot u\).

Differentiability is needed. For \(f = x^2y/(x^2 + y^2)\), \(f(0,0) = 0\), both partials at the origin are 0, so the formula would predict \(D_u f = 0\) in every direction. Yet along \(u = (1,1)/\sqrt2\), \(f(tu) = t/(2\sqrt2)\), so \(D_u f(0) = 1/(2\sqrt2)\). This continuous function has every directional derivative and is still not differentiable.

The three defining properties

Write \(\theta\) for the angle between \(\nabla f(a) \ne 0\) and \(u\). Then \(D_u f = \|\nabla f\|\,\|u\|\cos\theta = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta\), and everything follows from this one line.

Theorem · what the gradient is
  1. Steepest ascent. \(D_u f\) is largest at \(\theta = 0\), i.e. \(u = \nabla f/\|\nabla f\|\).
  2. Maximum rate. That largest value is \(\|\nabla f\|\); the smallest, at \(\theta = \pi\), is \(-\|\nabla f\|\), in direction \(-\nabla f\).
  3. Orthogonal to level sets. If \(r(t)\) is a curve in the level set \(f = c\) with \(r(0) = a\), then differentiating \(f(r(t)) = c\) gives \(\nabla f(a)\cdot r'(0) = 0\): the gradient is perpendicular to every tangent direction of the level set. Equivalently, tangent directions are those with \(\cos\theta = 0\), where \(f\) does not change to first order.

Gradient descent takes \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k)\): the locally steepest downhill direction, scaled by a step size. "Locally" matters: on stretched contours \(-\nabla f\) does not point at the minimum (figure, and L91). The map \(x \mapsto \nabla f(x)\) is itself a vector field, a gradient field; L86 asks which vector fields are gradients.

Tangent planes and normal lines

Result · tangent planes

Graph \(z = f(x, y)\) at \((a, b, f(a,b))\): \(z = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)(x - a) + f_y(a,b)(y - b)\), the linear approximation; a normal vector is \((f_x, f_y, -1)\).
Level surface \(g(x, y, z) = c\) at \(P\) with \(\nabla g(P) \ne 0\): \(\nabla g(P)\cdot(X - P) = 0\), and the normal line is \(X = P + t\,\nabla g(P)\). The graph case is the level surface \(g = f(x,y) - z = 0\).

Worked example · temperature in a given direction

\(T(x, y) = 100 - x^2 - 2y^2\). At \((1, 2)\), in the direction of \(v = (3, 4)\): \(\nabla T = (-2x, -4y) = (-2, -8)\), \(u = v/\|v\| = (3/5, 4/5)\), so \(D_u T = -6/5 - 32/5 = -7.6\) degrees per unit distance. The fastest increase is \(\|\nabla T\| = \sqrt{68} = 2\sqrt{17} \approx 8.25\), in direction \((-1, -4)/\sqrt{17}\).

Worked example · tangent plane to an ellipsoid

\(g = x^2/4 + y^2 + z^2/9 = 3\) at \(P = (-2, 1, -3)\) (check: \(1 + 1 + 1 = 3\)). \(\nabla g = (x/2, 2y, 2z/9) = (-1, 2, -2/3)\). Plane: \(-(x + 2) + 2(y - 1) - \tfrac23(z + 3) = 0\), i.e. \(3x - 6y + 2z + 18 = 0\) (it passes through \(P\): \(-6 - 6 - 6 + 18 = 0\)). Normal line: \((-2, 1, -3) + t(3, -6, 2)\).

Worked example

\(\nabla f \perp\) level curve for \(f = x^2 + y^2\). The level curve \(f = c\) is \(r(t) = (\sqrt c\cos t, \sqrt c\sin t)\) with \(r'(t) = \sqrt c(-\sin t, \cos t)\). On it \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y) = 2\sqrt c(\cos t, \sin t)\), and \(\nabla f\cdot r' = 2c(-\cos t\sin t + \sin t\cos t) = 0\). The gradient is radial, the contours are circles.

Common errors
  • Not normalizing the direction. \(\nabla f\cdot v\) with \(\|v\| = 5\) is five times the directional derivative.
  • Putting the gradient on the surface. For \(f(x, y)\), \(\nabla f \in \R^2\) lives in the domain; the 3D normal to the graph is \((f_x, f_y, -1)\).
  • Mixing the two tangent-plane formulas. Use the graph formula for \(z = f(x,y)\) and \(\nabla g\cdot(X - P) = 0\) for implicit surfaces.
  • Using \(\nabla f\cdot u\) without differentiability. The \(x^2y/(x^2+y^2)\) example breaks it.
  • Expecting \(-\nabla f\) to point at the minimum. It does only for round contours.

    \(D_u f = \nabla f\cdot u \le |\nabla f\cdot u| \le \|\nabla f\|\,\|u\| = \|\nabla f\|\), with equality in Cauchy–Schwarz iff \(u\) is a non-negative multiple of \(\nabla f\). Multivariable Chain Rule, the Total Derivative & the Jacobian

38m video55m practice

The derivative of a map \(\R^n \to \R^m\) is a matrix, the Jacobian, and the chain rule says the derivative of a composition is the product of the Jacobians. Backpropagation is that product evaluated from the output end, which is cheap when the output is a single number.

The chain rule with tree diagrams

Theorem · chain rule (two forms)

If \(f\) is differentiable and \(x(t), y(t)\) are differentiable, then \(z = f(x(t), y(t))\) has \(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = f_x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + f_y\dfrac{dy}{dt}\). If \(x = x(s, t)\), \(y = y(s, t)\), then \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial s} = f_x x_s + f_y y_s\) and \(\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial t} = f_x x_t + f_y y_t\).

A tree diagram makes this mechanical: put \(z\) at the top, its arguments \(x, y\) below, and their arguments \(s, t\) below those; label each edge with the partial derivative of the upper variable with respect to the lower. Then \(\partial z/\partial s\) is the sum, over every path from \(z\) down to \(s\), of the product of edge labels along the path. Missing a path is the most common error.

Worked example · a nested map

\(z = e^x\sin y\) with \(x = st^2\), \(y = s^2t\). Edges: \(z_x = e^x\sin y\), \(z_y = e^x\cos y\), \(x_s = t^2\), \(x_t = 2st\), \(y_s = 2st\), \(y_t = s^2\). Two paths to each leaf:

$$\frac{\partial z}{\partial s} = t^2e^{st^2}\sin(s^2t) + 2st\,e^{st^2}\cos(s^2t), \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} = 2st\,e^{st^2}\sin(s^2t) + s^2e^{st^2}\cos(s^2t).$$

The Jacobian is the total derivative

Definition · Jacobian

For \(F = (F_1, \dots, F_m): \R^n \to \R^m\), the Jacobian at \(a\) is the \(m \times n\) matrix \(J_F(a)_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j\): one row per output, one column per input. \(F\) is differentiable at \(a\) when \(F(a + h) = F(a) + J_F(a)h + o(\|h\|)\); the linear map \(h \mapsto J_F(a)h\) is the total derivative, the best linear approximation to \(F\) near \(a\).

For scalar \(f\) (\(m = 1\)) the Jacobian is the \(1 \times n\) row \(Df = (\partial_1 f, \dots, \partial_n f)\), whereas we have been writing the gradient as a column. Both conventions are in use; L88 fixes one (\(\nabla f = Df^\top\)) and keeps it.

Theorem · general chain rule

If \(G: \R^n \to \R^k\) is differentiable at \(a\) and \(F: \R^k \to \R^m\) is differentiable at \(G(a)\), then \(J_{F\circ G}(a) = J_F(G(a))\,J_G(a)\), an \((m \times k)(k \times n)\) product. Entry by entry this is exactly the tree-diagram sum \(\partial(F\circ G)_i/\partial x_j = \sum_l (\partial F_i/\partial u_l)(\partial u_l/\partial x_j)\).

Worked example · the polar map

\(F(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)\). Then \(J_F = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\det J_F = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r\). The determinant of a Jacobian is the factor by which the map scales small areas (volumes in higher dimensions); this is why \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) in L85.

Composing Jacobians is backpropagation

Take a scalar loss built in three stages, \(L = f(g(h(x)))\), with \(h: \R^n \to \R^k\), \(g: \R^k \to \R^m\), \(f: \R^m \to \R\). The chain rule gives \(DL = J_f\,J_g\,J_h\), shapes \((1 \times m)(m \times k)(k \times n)\). Matrix multiplication is associative, but the cost is not. Right to left (forward mode) first forms the \(m \times n\) matrix \(J_gJ_h\) at cost \(mkn\), then \(mn\) more. Left to right (reverse mode) only ever multiplies a row vector by a matrix: \(mk\), then \(kn\). With \(n = k = m = 1000\) that is about \(10^9\) against \(2 \times 10^6\) operations. Reverse mode wins whenever there are many inputs and one output, which is every training loss.

Worked example

Hand-propagate through \(f(g(h(x)))\). \(h(x) = (x_1 + x_2,\ x_1 - x_2)\), \(g(u) = (u_1u_2,\ u_1 + u_2^2)\), \(f(v) = v_1^2 + v_2^2\), at \(x = (1, 1)\).

Forward pass: \(u = (2, 0)\), \(v = (0, 2)\), \(L = 4\). Local Jacobians: \(J_h = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}\), \(J_g = \begin{pmatrix} u_2 & u_1 \\ 1 & 2u_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), \(J_f = (2v_1,\ 2v_2) = (0,\ 4)\).

Reverse pass (row times matrix): \(\bar v = (0, 4)\); \(\bar u = \bar v J_g = (4, 0)\); \(\bar x = \bar u J_h = (4, 4)\). So \(\nabla_x L = (4, 4)\).

Check by forward mode: \(J_gJ_h = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), and \((0, 4)\,J_gJ_h = (4, 4)\). Check directly: with \(a = x_1 + x_2\), \(b = x_1 - x_2\), \(L = a^2b^2 + (a + b^2)^2\); \(L_a = 2ab^2 + 2(a + b^2) = 4\) and \(L_b = 2a^2b + 4b(a + b^2) = 0\) at \((2, 0)\), so \(L_{x_1} = L_a + L_b = 4\), \(L_{x_2} = L_a - L_b = 4\). ✓

Implicit functions and inverse functions

If \(F(x, y, z) = 0\) defines \(z\) as a function of \((x, y)\), differentiate \(F(x, y, z(x,y)) = 0\) with respect to \(x\): \(F_x + F_z z_x = 0\), so \(z_x = -F_x/F_z\) and likewise \(z_y = -F_y/F_z\), wherever \(F_z \ne 0\). The two big theorems say when this is legitimate.

Theorem · Implicit and Inverse Function Theorems

Implicit. Let \(F: \R^{n} \times \R^{m} \to \R^m\) be \(C^1\) with \(F(a, b) = 0\), and suppose the \(m \times m\) block \(\partial F/\partial y\,(a, b)\) is invertible. Then near \((a, b)\) the solutions of \(F(x, y) = 0\) are exactly the graph of a unique \(C^1\) function \(y = g(x)\), with \(Dg = -(\partial F/\partial y)^{-1}\,\partial F/\partial x\).

Inverse. If \(F: \R^n \to \R^n\) is \(C^1\) and \(\det J_F(a) \ne 0\), then \(F\) has a \(C^1\) inverse on a neighbourhood of \(F(a)\), with \(J_{F^{-1}}(F(a)) = J_F(a)^{-1}\).

Both are local. The polar map has \(\det J = r \ne 0\) for \(r > 0\), so it is invertible near every such point, but not globally, since \(\theta\) and \(\theta + 2\pi\) give the same point.

Common errors
  • Missing a branch of the tree, e.g. when \(z\) depends on \(t\) both directly and through \(x(t)\).
  • Transposed Jacobians. Rows are outputs, columns are inputs; check that \(J_FJ_G\) has matching inner dimensions.
  • Evaluating at the wrong point. \(J_F\) is evaluated at \(G(a)\), not at \(a\).
  • Dropping the minus sign in \(z_x = -F_x/F_z\).
  • Reading the Inverse Function Theorem globally. \(\det J \ne 0\) everywhere does not make a map injective.

Second-Order Structure: The Hessian & Taylor in ℝⁿ

34m video50m practice

Near any point a smooth function looks like a quadratic: a constant, a linear term from the gradient, and a quadratic form from the Hessian. Because the Hessian is symmetric, its eigenvalues tell you the shape of that quadratic exactly (bowl, dome or saddle) and its condition number tells you how stretched the bowl is.

The Hessian matrix

Definition

For \(f: \R^n \to \R\) with second partials, the Hessian is the \(n \times n\) matrix \(H(x) = \nabla^2 f(x)\) with \(H_{ij} = \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\). It is the Jacobian of the gradient field \(\nabla f\).

If \(f \in C^2\), Clairaut (L77) gives \(H_{ij} = H_{ji}\): the Hessian is symmetric. Everything proved for real symmetric matrices in L56 now applies: real eigenvalues, an orthonormal eigenbasis, \(H = Q\Lambda Q^\top\), and the definiteness tests.

Taylor's theorem in ℝⁿ

Theorem · second-order Taylor expansion

If \(f\) is \(C^2\) near \(x\), then for small \(h\)

$$f(x + h) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top h + \tfrac12\,h^\top H(x)\,h + o(\|h\|^2),$$

and exactly, \(f(x + h) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top h + \tfrac12\,h^\top H(x + \tau h)\,h\) for some \(\tau \in (0, 1)\).

Derivation · reduce to one variable along a line

Let \(g(t) = f(x + th)\). By the chain rule, \(g'(t) = \sum_i \partial_i f(x + th)\,h_i = \nabla f(x+th)^\top h\). Differentiating again, each \(\partial_i f(x + th)\) contributes \(\sum_j \partial_j\partial_i f(x+th)\,h_j\), so \(g''(t) = \sum_{i,j} h_i H_{ij}(x + th) h_j = h^\top H(x + th)\,h\). One-variable Taylor with Lagrange remainder, \(g(1) = g(0) + g'(0) + \tfrac12 g''(\tau)\), is the exact form. Continuity of \(H\) turns \(H(x + \tau h)\) into \(H(x) + o(1)\), which gives the \(o(\|h\|^2)\) form.

The truncation \(m(h) = f(x) + g^\top h + \tfrac12 h^\top H h\) (with \(g = \nabla f(x)\)) is the local quadratic model. Every second-order optimizer works with it: Newton's method (L92) jumps to its minimizer, trust-region methods minimize it over a ball.

Worked example

Second-order Taylor of \(e^{x+y}\) at the origin. Every partial derivative of \(e^{x+y}\) is \(e^{x+y}\), equal to 1 at the origin: \(f = 1\), \(\nabla f = (1, 1)\), \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). So \(e^{x+y} \approx 1 + (x + y) + \tfrac12(x^2 + 2xy + y^2) = 1 + u + \tfrac12u^2\) with \(u = x + y\), the one-variable series of \(e^u\), as it must be. \(H\) has eigenvalues 2 (along \((1,1)\)) and 0 (along \((1,-1)\)): the function is constant along the lines \(x + y = \text{const}\), so there is no curvature in that direction.

Reading curvature from eigenvalues

Diagonalize \(H = Q\Lambda Q^\top\) and change coordinates to \(y = Q^\top h\) (a rotation). Then

$$h^\top H h = y^\top \Lambda y = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2 .$$

The quadratic term is a sum of independent one-dimensional parabolas along the eigenvectors, with curvatures \(\lambda_i\). All \(\lambda_i > 0\) (positive definite): a bowl. All \(\lambda_i < 0\): a dome. Mixed signs (indefinite): a saddle, curving up along some eigenvectors and down along others. Some \(\lambda_i = 0\) (semidefinite): flat directions where second order says nothing. For \(2 \times 2\) matrices, Sylvester's test from L56 is quickest: \(H\) is positive definite iff \(H_{11} > 0\) and \(\det H > 0\); \(\det H < 0\) means indefinite.

Result · Hessian of a quadratic form

For \(q(x) = x^\top A x\): \(\nabla q = (A + A^\top)x\) and \(\nabla^2 q = A + A^\top\), which is \(2A\) when \(A\) is symmetric. Only the symmetric part of \(A\) matters, since \(x^\top A x = x^\top \tfrac{A + A^\top}{2} x\).

Conditioning: how stretched is the bowl?

For positive definite \(H\), the level set \(\tfrac12 h^\top H h = c\) is \(\sum_i \lambda_i y_i^2 = 2c\), an ellipsoid with semi-axes \(\sqrt{2c/\lambda_i}\) along the eigenvectors. The ratio of longest to shortest axis is \(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}} = \sqrt{\kappa}\), where \(\kappa(H) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) is the condition number of L58; the eccentricity of a 2D level ellipse is \(\sqrt{1 - 1/\kappa}\). Large \(\kappa\) means long thin valleys: the gradient points mostly across the valley, and gradient descent zig-zags (L91).

Worked example

Hessian of \(f = x^3 + 2xy^2 - y^3\). \(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2\), \(f_y = 4xy - 3y^2\), and \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 6x & 4y \\ 4y & 4x - 6y\end{pmatrix}\).

Critical points. \(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2 = 0\) forces \(x = y = 0\), so the origin is the only one, and there \(H = 0\): all eigenvalues are 0 and the test is silent. By inspection, \(f(x, 0) = x^3\) changes sign at 0, so the origin is neither a maximum nor a minimum. The Hessian still shows the shape nearby: on the \(x\)-axis \(H = \operatorname{diag}(6x, 4x)\) is positive definite for \(x > 0\) and negative definite for \(x < 0\), so the origin sits where a bowl region meets a dome region. At \((1,1)\), \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 4 \\ 4 & -2\end{pmatrix}\) has \(\det = -28\) and eigenvalues \(2 \pm 4\sqrt2\): saddle-shaped curvature there.

A companion with clean critical points, \(f = x^3 + y^3 - 3xy\): \(\nabla f = (3x^2 - 3y, 3y^2 - 3x) = 0\) gives \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). \(H = \begin{pmatrix} 6x & -3 \\ -3 & 6y\end{pmatrix}\). At the origin the eigenvalues are \(\pm 3\), a saddle; at \((1,1)\) they are \(6 \pm 3 = 3, 9\), a local minimum with \(\kappa = 3\).

Common errors
  • Losing the ½. The quadratic term is \(\tfrac12 h^\top H h\); with \(H\) for \(x^2\) equal to 2, the ½ is what returns \(x^2\).
  • \(\nabla^2(x^\top A x) = 2A\) for non-symmetric \(A\). It is \(A + A^\top\).
  • Positive diagonal ≠ positive definite. \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues 3 and \(-1\).
  • Reading \(H = 0\) as "flat, so a minimum". A zero or semidefinite Hessian decides nothing; higher-order terms do.
  • Axis ratio \(= \kappa\). It is \(\sqrt\kappa\); the ratio of curvatures is \(\kappa\).

Unconstrained Optimization in ℝⁿ

34m video50m practice

To optimize without constraints: solve \(\nabla f = 0\) for the candidates, then let the Hessian's eigenvalue signs sort them into minima, maxima and saddles. On a closed bounded region add the boundary; on an unbounded one you need coercivity before a global minimum is even guaranteed to exist.

Extrema and the first-order condition

Definition

\(x^*\) is a local minimum of \(f\) if \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) for all \(x\) in some ball around \(x^*\), and strict if the inequality is strict for \(x \ne x^*\); a global minimum if \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) on the whole domain. Maxima likewise. A point with \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\) is a critical (stationary) point.

Theorem · first-order necessary condition

If \(f\) is differentiable at an interior local extremum \(x^*\), then \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\).

Proof

For any direction \(v\), \(g(t) = f(x^* + tv)\) has a local extremum at \(t = 0\), so by the one-variable result \(g'(0) = \nabla f(x^*)^\top v = 0\). Taking \(v = \nabla f(x^*)\) gives \(\|\nabla f(x^*)\|^2 = 0\).

The condition is necessary, not sufficient: saddles satisfy it too. In practice it is a system of \(n\) (usually nonlinear) equations. Solve it by factoring and case-splitting rather than dividing, so no root is lost.

The second-derivative test

Theorem · Hessian test at a critical point (f ∈ C²)
  • \(H(x^*)\) positive definite ⇒ strict local minimum.
  • \(H(x^*)\) negative definite ⇒ strict local maximum.
  • \(H(x^*)\) indefinite (eigenvalues of both signs) ⇒ saddle point: \(f\) increases along some directions and decreases along others.
  • \(H(x^*)\) semidefinite and singular ⇒ inconclusive.

In two variables, \(\det H = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2\) and \(\operatorname{tr} H = f_{xx} + f_{yy} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2\). So with \(D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2\): \(D > 0, f_{xx} > 0\) is a minimum (both eigenvalues positive); \(D > 0, f_{xx} < 0\) a maximum; \(D < 0\) a saddle (opposite signs); \(D = 0\) says nothing. The discriminant is the determinant of the Hessian.

Degenerate cases are settled by inspection. At the origin all of \(x^4 + y^4\), \(-(x^4 + y^4)\), \(x^4 - y^4\) and \(x^2 + y^3\) have \(D = 0\). They are respectively a strict minimum (\(f > 0\) elsewhere), a maximum, a saddle (sign differs on the axes) and not an extremum (\(f(0, y) = y^3\) changes sign).

Worked example

Classify the critical points of \(f = x^4 + y^4 - 4xy\). \(\nabla f = (4x^3 - 4y,\ 4y^3 - 4x) = 0\) gives \(y = x^3\) and \(x = y^3 = x^9\), so \(x(x^8 - 1) = 0\): \(x \in \{0, 1, -1\}\). Critical points \((0,0), (1,1), (-1,-1)\).

\(H = \begin{pmatrix} 12x^2 & -4 \\ -4 & 12y^2\end{pmatrix}\). At \((0,0)\): \(D = -16 < 0\) (eigenvalues \(\pm4\)), a saddle. At \((\pm1, \pm1)\): \(D = 144 - 16 = 128 > 0\), \(f_{xx} = 12 > 0\) (eigenvalues 8 and 16), local minima with \(f = -2\).

Global? Using \(4|xy| \le 2(x^2 + y^2)\) and \(x^4 + y^4 \ge \tfrac12(x^2 + y^2)^2\): \(f \ge \tfrac12 r^4 - 2r^2 \to \infty\). So \(f\) is coercive, a global minimum exists, it must be a critical point, and the smallest critical value is \(-2\): both \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\) are global minima.

Why saddles dominate in high dimensions

A critical point is a local minimum only if all \(n\) Hessian eigenvalues are positive. As a heuristic, if each eigenvalue's sign at a "typical" critical point were an independent coin flip, the chance of a minimum would be \(2^{-n}\), which is \(10^{-301}\) for \(n = 1000\). Real Hessians are not coin flips, but random-matrix models of high-dimensional landscapes (Bray & Dean 2007) and experiments on neural networks (Dauphin et al. 2014) point the same way: critical points with high loss are overwhelmingly saddles, and the fraction of negative eigenvalues tends to grow with the loss. So the obstacle in training is mostly escaping saddles and plateaus, not bad local minima. Treat this as a well-supported picture, not a theorem about any particular network.

Absolute extrema on closed bounded regions

Algorithm · closed-region method
  1. The Extreme Value Theorem (L76) guarantees a maximum and a minimum for continuous \(f\) on a closed bounded \(K\).
  2. Find the critical points in the interior of \(K\) and evaluate \(f\) there.
  3. Parametrize each smooth piece of the boundary, reduce \(f\) to a one-variable function, and find its critical points and endpoints (corners).
  4. The largest of all candidate values is the absolute maximum, the smallest the absolute minimum. No second-derivative test is needed.
Worked example

Absolute extrema of \(f = xy\) on the unit disc. Interior: \(\nabla f = (y, x) = 0\) only at the origin, \(f = 0\) (a saddle). Boundary: \(x = \cos t\), \(y = \sin t\), \(f = \cos t\sin t = \tfrac12\sin 2t\), which ranges over \([-\tfrac12, \tfrac12]\): maximum at \(t = \pi/4, 5\pi/4\), minimum at \(t = 3\pi/4, 7\pi/4\). Compare: absolute maximum \(\tfrac12\) at \(\pm(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}, \tfrac1{\sqrt2})\), absolute minimum \(-\tfrac12\) at \(\pm(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}, -\tfrac1{\sqrt2})\).

Existence on unbounded domains: coercivity

Theorem · coercive functions have global minima

If \(f: \R^n \to \R\) is continuous and coercive, \(f(x) \to \infty\) as \(\|x\| \to \infty\), then \(f\) attains a global minimum.

Proof

Fix any \(x_0\). Coercivity gives \(R\) with \(f(x) > f(x_0)\) whenever \(\|x\| > R\). On the closed ball \(\|x\| \le R\) (closed and bounded, containing \(x_0\)), the EVT gives a minimizer \(x^*\) with \(f(x^*) \le f(x_0)\). Outside the ball \(f > f(x_0) \ge f(x^*)\). So \(x^*\) is a global minimizer.

Without coercivity a minimum can fail to exist even for functions bounded below: \(e^x + y^2 > 0\) everywhere, but its infimum 0 is never attained.

Common errors
  • Dividing to solve \(\nabla f = 0\). From \(x(x^8 - 1) = 0\), dividing by \(x\) loses the saddle at the origin.
  • Forgetting \(f_{xy}^2\) in \(D\), or using \(f_{xx}\) to decide when \(D < 0\) (it is a saddle regardless).
  • Concluding from \(D = 0\). Inspect the function along lines or curves instead.
  • Skipping the boundary or the corners in an absolute-extremum problem.
  • Promoting a local minimum to global without coercivity, convexity or a complete candidate list.

Constrained Optimization: Lagrange Multipliers

36m video50m practiceheavy lesson

At a constrained optimum you cannot improve \(f\) by sliding along the constraint, so the level set of \(f\) is tangent to the constraint set and the two gradients are parallel: \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\). The multiplier \(\lambda\) is not a by-product; it is the rate at which the optimal value changes when the constraint is relaxed.

The tangency condition

Problem: optimize \(f(x)\) over \(x \in \R^n\) subject to \(g(x) = c\), with \(f, g \in C^1\). Walking along the constraint surface \(S = \{g = c\}\), the only directions available at \(x\) are tangent ones, those \(v\) with \(\nabla g(x)\cdot v = 0\).

Theorem · Lagrange multiplier rule

If \(x^*\) is a local extremum of \(f\) on \(S\) and \(\nabla g(x^*) \ne 0\), then there is a scalar \(\lambda\) with \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda\,\nabla g(x^*)\).

Proof

Because \(\nabla g(x^*) \ne 0\), the Implicit Function Theorem (L79) makes \(S\) a smooth surface near \(x^*\), and every \(v\) with \(\nabla g(x^*)\cdot v = 0\) is the velocity \(r'(0)\) of some curve \(r(t) \subset S\) with \(r(0) = x^*\). Then \(t \mapsto f(r(t))\) has a local extremum at \(t = 0\), so \(\nabla f(x^*)\cdot r'(0) = 0\). Hence \(\nabla f(x^*)\) is orthogonal to every vector orthogonal to \(\nabla g(x^*)\), which forces \(\nabla f(x^*) \in \operatorname{span}\{\nabla g(x^*)\}\).

Define the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\,(g(x) - c)\). Then \(\nabla_x\mathcal L = 0\) is \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\) and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\lambda = 0\) is the constraint: the constrained problem becomes "find the critical points of \(\mathcal L\)", \(n + 1\) equations in \(n + 1\) unknowns.

Algorithm · solving a Lagrange problem
  1. Write \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\) componentwise, plus \(g = c\).
  2. Solve by cases. Never divide by a quantity that might be zero; split "\(x = 0\) or …" instead.
  3. Add any points where \(\nabla g = 0\) on \(S\), and endpoints or edges if \(S\) has them.
  4. Evaluate \(f\) at every candidate. If \(S\) is closed and bounded, the EVT guarantees the largest is the maximum and the smallest the minimum; otherwise argue existence separately.

Three worked problems

Worked example

Maximize \(xyz\) subject to \(x + y + z = 3\). Take \(x, y, z \ge 0\); without that the problem is unbounded (\(x = y = -t\), \(z = 3 + 2t\) gives \(xyz = t^2(3 + 2t) \to \infty\)). Equations: \(yz = \lambda\), \(xz = \lambda\), \(xy = \lambda\), \(x + y + z = 3\).

Case all nonzero: \(yz = xz\) gives \(y = x\) (dividing by \(z \ne 0\) is now legitimate), and \(xz = xy\) gives \(z = y\). So \(x = y = z = 1\), \(\lambda = 1\), \(f = 1\). Case some variable zero, say \(x = 0\): then \(\lambda = xz = 0\), so \(yz = 0\) and the candidates are \((0,0,3)\) and permutations, plus the edges of the triangle, all with \(f = 0\). The feasible triangle is closed and bounded, so the maximum is \(1\) at \((1,1,1)\).

Worked example

Distance from the origin to the plane \(x + 2y + 2z = 9\). Minimize \(f = x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) (the squared distance). \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\): \((2x, 2y, 2z) = \lambda(1, 2, 2)\), so \((x, y, z) = \tfrac\lambda2(1, 2, 2)\). The constraint gives \(\tfrac\lambda2(1 + 4 + 4) = 9\), so \(\lambda = 2\), the point is \((1, 2, 2)\), and the distance is 3. This matches \(|d|/\|n\| = 9/3\), and the closest point is \(dn/\|n\|^2\), the projection from L36.

The Rayleigh quotient: eigenvalues from optimization

Worked example

Maximize \(x^\top A x\) on the unit sphere. For symmetric \(A\), \(\mathcal L = x^\top A x - \lambda(x^\top x - 1)\) and \(\nabla_x\mathcal L = 2Ax - 2\lambda x = 0\), so \(Ax = \lambda x\): every candidate is a unit eigenvector, and its value is \(x^\top A x = \lambda x^\top x = \lambda\). The sphere is compact, so the maximum exists and equals \(\lambda_{\max}\), attained at the top eigenvector; the minimum is \(\lambda_{\min}\).

For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\): eigenvalues 3 at \((1,1)/\sqrt2\) and 1 at \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\). Check by brute force: \(x = (\cos t, \sin t)\) gives \(x^\top A x = 2 + \sin 2t \in [1, 3]\).

This is PCA from the optimization side. The variance of data projected on a unit direction \(w\) is \(w^\top\Sigma w\) with \(\Sigma\) the covariance matrix, so the direction of maximal variance is the top eigenvector of \(\Sigma\), and the variance it captures is \(\lambda_{\max}\). L58 reached the same answer through the SVD.

Several constraints, and when the rule fails

With constraints \(g_1 = c_1, \dots, g_k = c_k\), the rule becomes \(\nabla f = \sum_i \lambda_i\nabla g_i\), provided the \(\nabla g_i(x^*)\) are linearly independent (the constraint qualification). Without it the rule can fail. Minimize \(f = x\) on the cusp \(y^2 = x^3\): the minimum is at the origin (since \(x^3 = y^2 \ge 0\)), but there \(\nabla g = (-3x^2, 2y) = 0\) and \(\nabla f = (1, 0)\) is no multiple of it. Such points must be added to the candidate list by hand.

The multiplier is a shadow price

Theorem · sensitivity of the optimal value

Let \(f^*(c) = f(x^*(c))\) be the optimal value as a function of the constraint level, with \(x^*(c)\) and \(\lambda(c)\) differentiable in \(c\). Then \(\dfrac{df^*}{dc} = \lambda\).

So \(\lambda\) prices the constraint: relaxing \(g = c\) by \(\Delta c\) changes the best attainable value by about \(\lambda\,\Delta c\). Check: for the plane, \(f^* = d^2/9\) and \(df^*/dd = 2d/9 = 2 = \lambda\) at \(d = 9\). For \(xyz\), \(f^*(c) = (c/3)^3\) has derivative \((c/3)^2 = 1 = \lambda\) at \(c = 3\).

Common errors
  • Dividing by a variable that might vanish, losing whole families of candidates.
  • Forgetting the constraint equation: \(\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g\) alone has too many solutions.
  • Missing points where \(\nabla g = 0\) (or the \(\nabla g_i\) are dependent): the rule does not apply there.
  • Calling a Lagrange point a maximum on an unbounded set. Candidates are not answers until you compare values and know an optimum exists.
  • Sign conventions. With \(\mathcal L = f + \lambda(g - c)\) the multiplier flips sign and \(df^*/dc = -\lambda\). Fix one convention.

Inequality Constraints & the KKT Conditions

30m video40m practiceheavy lesson

An inequality constraint either matters at the optimum (it is active, and behaves like an equality with a multiplier of the right sign) or it does not (its multiplier is zero). The KKT conditions encode exactly that, and for convex problems they are not just necessary but sufficient for a global minimum.

Active and inactive constraints

Standard form: minimize \(f(x)\) subject to \(g_i(x) \le 0\) (\(i = 1..m\)) and \(h_j(x) = 0\) (\(j = 1..p\)). At a feasible point, \(g_i\) is active if \(g_i(x) = 0\) and inactive if \(g_i(x) < 0\). An inactive constraint has slack in every direction, so locally it is invisible; an active one blocks movement only to one side. The generalized Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal L(x, \mu, \lambda) = f(x) + \sum_i \mu_i\, g_i(x) + \sum_j \lambda_j\, h_j(x).$$

The four KKT conditions

Theorem · Karush–Kuhn–Tucker

If \(x^*\) is a local minimum and a constraint qualification holds (for instance, the gradients of the active \(g_i\) and of all \(h_j\) are linearly independent), then there exist \(\mu\), \(\lambda\) with

  1. Stationarity: \(\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i\nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \lambda_j\nabla h_j(x^*) = 0\).
  2. Primal feasibility: \(g_i(x^*) \le 0\), \(h_j(x^*) = 0\).
  3. Dual feasibility: \(\mu_i \ge 0\).
  4. Complementary slackness: \(\mu_i\, g_i(x^*) = 0\) for every \(i\).

Geometry. Stationarity says \(-\nabla f = \sum_i \mu_i\nabla g_i + \sum_j\lambda_j\nabla h_j\). Each \(\nabla g_i\) points out of the feasible set (toward larger \(g_i\)). If \(-\nabla f\), the downhill direction, had any component pointing into the feasible set, you could step that way and do better. So at the optimum downhill must point "outward", into the cone spanned by the outward normals of the active constraints: that is what \(\mu_i \ge 0\) says. An equality can be pushed against from either side, so \(\lambda_j\) has no sign. Complementary slackness says an inactive constraint (\(g_i < 0\)) cannot push at all (\(\mu_i = 0\)), and a constraint with \(\mu_i > 0\) must be tight. As in L82, \(\mu_i\) is a price: how fast the optimal value would fall if constraint \(i\) were relaxed.

KKT conditions are necessary under a constraint qualification, and sufficient when \(f\) and the \(g_i\) are convex and the \(h_j\) are affine (Prove it, and L90, L93).

Algorithm · KKT by case analysis
  1. Choose a subset of inequality constraints to be active; set \(g_i = 0\) for those and \(\mu_i = 0\) for the rest.
  2. Solve stationarity together with the active constraints and \(h_j = 0\).
  3. Reject the case if the solution violates an inactive constraint or has some \(\mu_i < 0\).
  4. Among surviving cases compare objective values (for a convex problem, any survivor is optimal).
Worked example

Minimize \(x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + y \ge 2\). Write the constraint as \(g = 2 - x - y \le 0\). Case inactive (\(\mu = 0\)): \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y) = 0\) gives \((0, 0)\), but \(g(0,0) = 2 > 0\): infeasible, rejected. Case active: \(x + y = 2\) and \((2x, 2y) + \mu(-1, -1) = 0\), so \(x = y = \mu/2\), then \(\mu = 2 \ge 0\) and \(x = y = 1\). The minimum is 2 at \((1, 1)\), and since the problem is convex this KKT point is the global minimum.

Worked examples · a box and a ball

Box. Minimize \((x - 2)^2 + (y + 1)^2\) on \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(0 \le y \le 1\). The unconstrained minimizer \((2, -1)\) lies outside, so guess \(x = 1\) and \(y = 0\) active: \(g_1 = x - 1\), \(g_2 = -y\). Stationarity: \(2(x - 2) + \mu_1 = 0\) gives \(\mu_1 = 2\), and \(2(y + 1) - \mu_2 = 0\) gives \(\mu_2 = 2\). Both are non-negative, so \((1, 0)\) is optimal: the projection of \((2, -1)\) onto the box, coordinate by coordinate.

Ball. Minimize \(c^\top x\) subject to \(\|x\|^2 \le 1\), \(c \ne 0\). Inactive would need \(c = 0\), so the constraint is active: \(c + 2\mu x = 0\) with \(\|x\| = 1\) gives \(x^* = -c/\|c\|\), \(\mu = \|c\|/2 \ge 0\), value \(-\|c\|\).

The 1D lasso and soft thresholding

Worked example

Minimize \(\tfrac12(w - z)^2 + \lambda|w|\), \(\lambda > 0\). Remove the kink with an epigraph variable: minimize \(\tfrac12(w - z)^2 + \lambda t\) subject to \(w - t \le 0\) and \(-w - t \le 0\) (so \(t \ge |w|\)). Multipliers \(\mu_1, \mu_2 \ge 0\). Stationarity in \(w\): \(w - z + \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\); in \(t\): \(\lambda - \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\).

Both active (\(w = t = 0\)): \(\mu_1 - \mu_2 = z\), \(\mu_1 + \mu_2 = \lambda\), so \(\mu_{1,2} = (\lambda \pm z)/2\), non-negative iff \(|z| \le \lambda\). Only the first (\(w = t > 0\), \(\mu_2 = 0\)): \(\mu_1 = \lambda\), \(w = z - \lambda\), valid iff \(z > \lambda\). Only the second (\(-w = t > 0\)): \(w = z + \lambda\), valid iff \(z < -\lambda\). Neither: \(\mu = 0\) contradicts \(\lambda > 0\).

Together: the soft-thresholding rule \(w^* = \operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \lambda, 0)\). Every \(|z| \le \lambda\) is snapped to exactly zero; larger values are shrunk toward zero by \(\lambda\).

Ridge and lasso as norm-constrained least squares

Ridge is \(\min \|Ax - b\|^2\) subject to \(\|x\|_2^2 \le t\); lasso is the same with \(\|x\|_1 \le t\). When the constraint is active, stationarity for ridge reads \(2A^\top(Ax - b) + 2\mu x = 0\), i.e. \((A^\top A + \mu I)x = A^\top b\): the penalized ridge solution with penalty \(\mu\). Each budget \(t\) corresponds to a penalty, and the multiplier is that penalty. The lasso's \(\ell_1\) ball, the diamond of L36, has corners on the axes. The elliptical contours of the squared error usually first touch a convex body at a point where the body "sticks out" most, and for a polytope that is typically a vertex or low-dimensional face, where some coordinates are exactly zero. The \(\ell_2\) ball is round with no corners, so the touching point generically has every coordinate nonzero.

Common errors
  • Writing \(g \ge 0\) constraints with the \(\le\) sign convention. Convert to \(g \le 0\) first, or the multiplier signs come out reversed.
  • Reporting the unconstrained optimum without checking it is feasible.
  • Keeping a case with \(\mu_i < 0\). It is a stationary point of the Lagrangian, not a KKT point.
  • Treating KKT as sufficient for non-convex problems. Without convexity a KKT point can be a saddle or a local maximum.
  • Forgetting \(w = 0\) in lasso problems, where \(|w|\) is not differentiable; that case is exactly where sparsity lives.

Double & Triple Integrals

36m video50m practice

A double integral adds up \(f\,dA\) over a region, and Fubini's theorem lets you do it as two ordinary integrals, one inside the other. All the work is in describing the region correctly, which is why you always sketch it first, and why changing the order of integration is a matter of redescribing the same region.

Riemann sums and Fubini's theorem

Definition · double integral

Cut a rectangle \(R\) into subrectangles \(R_{ij}\) of area \(\Delta A\), pick sample points \((x_{ij}^*, y_{ij}^*)\), and set \(\iint_R f\,dA = \lim \sum_{i,j} f(x_{ij}^*, y_{ij}^*)\,\Delta A\) as the mesh goes to 0, when the limit exists (it does for continuous \(f\)). For \(f \ge 0\) it is the volume under the surface \(z = f(x, y)\). Over a general bounded region \(D\), integrate \(f\cdot\mathbf 1_D\) over a rectangle containing \(D\); \(\iint_D 1\,dA\) is the area of \(D\).

Theorem · Fubini

If \(f\) is continuous on \(R = [a, b] \times [c, d]\), then

$$\iint_R f\,dA = \int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x, y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x, y)\,dx\,dy .$$

More generally the order may be swapped whenever \(\iint |f|\,dA < \infty\), and always for \(f \ge 0\) (Tonelli's version), even on unbounded regions.

The integrability hypothesis is not decoration. Since \(\partial_y\big[y/(x^2 + y^2)\big] = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2\), integrating that function first in \(y\) over \([0,1]\) gives \(1/(1 + x^2)\) and then \(\pi/4\); by the antisymmetry \(x \leftrightarrow y\) the other order gives \(-\pi/4\). Here \(\iint |f| = \infty\) near the origin.

Type I and Type II regions

Definition

Type I: \(D = \{a \le x \le b,\ g_1(x) \le y \le g_2(x)\}\), so \(\iint_D f\,dA = \int_a^b\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f\,dy\,dx\). Type II: \(D = \{c \le y \le d,\ h_1(y) \le x \le h_2(y)\}\), so \(\iint_D f\,dA = \int_c^d\int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f\,dx\,dy\). The inner limits may depend on the outer variable; the outer limits must be constants.

Algorithm · setting up the limits
  1. Sketch \(D\) and label its boundary curves and corners.
  2. For Type I, draw a vertical line through \(D\) at a typical \(x\): it enters at \(y = g_1(x)\) and leaves at \(y = g_2(x)\). The outer range is the extreme \(x\)-values of \(D\).
  3. For Type II, use a horizontal line instead. If one family of lines crosses a boundary that changes formula, split \(D\) or use the other type.
Worked example

\(\iint_T xy\,dA\) over that triangle, both orders. Type I: \(\int_0^1\int_0^x xy\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 x\cdot\tfrac{x^2}{2}\,dx = \tfrac18\). Type II: \(\int_0^1\int_y^1 xy\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1 y\cdot\tfrac{1 - y^2}{2}\,dy = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\big) = \tfrac18\). Same region, same answer.

Changing the order: convenience or necessity

Worked example

\(\int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy\). The inner integral \(\int e^{x^2}dx\) has no elementary antiderivative, so this order is hopeless. The limits describe \(0 \le y \le 1\), \(y \le x \le 1\): the same triangle as in the figure. As Type I it is \(0 \le x \le 1\), \(0 \le y \le x\):

$$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 x\,e^{x^2}\,dx = \tfrac12\big[e^{x^2}\big]_0^1 = \tfrac12(e - 1).$$

The new inner integral produced the factor \(x\) that makes the substitution \(u = x^2\) work.

Triple integrals and physical applications

A triple integral \(\iiint_E f\,dV\) is built the same way, and for a box Fubini allows all \(3! = 6\) orders. For a general solid, describe \(E\) as \(\{(x,y) \in D,\ u_1(x,y) \le z \le u_2(x,y)\}\) (or analogously in another variable), integrate \(z\) first, then do the double integral over the shadow \(D\).

Worked example

Volume of the tetrahedron \(x/a + y/b + z/c \le 1\), \(x, y, z \ge 0\). \(z\) runs from 0 to \(c(1 - x/a - y/b)\); the shadow is the triangle \(0 \le x \le a\), \(0 \le y \le b(1 - x/a)\). With \(s = 1 - x/a\): the inner integral is \(c(s - y/b)\); integrating in \(y\) from 0 to \(bs\) gives \(c\big(bs^2 - bs^2/2\big) = \tfrac{bc}{2}s^2\); finally \(\int_0^a \tfrac{bc}{2}(1 - x/a)^2\,dx = \tfrac{bc}{2}\cdot\tfrac a3\). So \(V = abc/6\).

With density \(\rho\): mass \(m = \iint_D\rho\,dA\), moments \(M_y = \iint x\rho\,dA\), \(M_x = \iint y\rho\,dA\), centre of mass \((\bar x, \bar y) = (M_y/m, M_x/m)\); in 3D likewise with \(dV\).

The probabilistic reading

Definition · joint density

A joint density is \(p(x, y) \ge 0\) with \(\iint_{\R^2} p\,dA = 1\). Probabilities are volumes: \(P\big((X, Y) \in A\big) = \iint_A p\,dA\). The marginal density of \(X\) is \(p_X(x) = \int_{\R} p(x, y)\,dy\): integrate out the other variable.

Worked example · uniform density on the triangle

The triangle has area \(\tfrac12\), so \(p = 2\) on it. Marginals: \(p_X(x) = \int_0^x 2\,dy = 2x\) and \(p_Y(y) = \int_y^1 2\,dx = 2(1 - y)\) on \([0, 1]\); note that the inner limits depend on the outer variable, exactly as in the setup rules. \(P(X + Y \le 1) = 2\cdot\text{area}\{0 \le y \le x,\ x + y \le 1\} = 2\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac12\). This is the setup L103 builds on.

Common errors
  • Variable outer limits. The outer integral's limits must be numbers; only the inner ones may depend on the outer variable.
  • Reversing the order by swapping limits. Redraw the region and redescribe it.
  • Swapping orders when \(\iint |f| = \infty\). The two iterated integrals can disagree.
  • Centre of mass without dividing by the mass.
  • Marginals over the wrong range. On a non-rectangular support, \(\int p(x,y)\,dy\) runs only over the \(y\) with \((x, y)\) in the support.

Change of Variables & the Jacobian Determinant

32m video45m practice

A change of variables distorts small areas, and the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant is exactly the local stretch factor. Put it in the integral and you can use any coordinates that fit the region or the integrand; put it in a density and you have the rule for transforming random variables.

One dimension, re-read

The substitution \(x = x(u)\) gives \(\int_{x(\alpha)}^{x(\beta)} f(x)\,dx = \int_\alpha^\beta f(x(u))\,x'(u)\,du\). Read it as a statement about lengths: a small interval of length \(du\) is mapped to one of length \(|x'(u)|\,du\). Written over sets instead of oriented intervals, with \(x\) monotone, \(\int_{x(I)} f\,dx = \int_I f(x(u))\,|x'(u)|\,du\). The absolute value is the orientation-free version, and it is the one that generalizes.

The change-of-variables theorem

Theorem · change of variables in the plane

Let \(T(u, v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))\) be \(C^1\), one-to-one on the interior of a region \(S\), with Jacobian determinant \(\dfrac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} x_u & x_v \\ y_u & y_v\end{pmatrix} \ne 0\) there, and let \(R = T(S)\). Then for continuous \(f\)

$$\iint_R f(x, y)\,dx\,dy = \iint_S f\big(x(u,v), y(u,v)\big)\left|\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)}\right| du\,dv .$$

The same holds in \(\R^n\) with the \(n \times n\) Jacobian determinant.

Why. A tiny \(du \times dv\) rectangle at \((u, v)\) is mapped, to first order, by the linear map \(J_T\): onto the parallelogram spanned by \(T_u\,du\) and \(T_v\,dv\). By L45 its area is \(|\det[T_u\ T_v]|\,du\,dv = |\det J_T|\,du\,dv\). Summing these pieces is the theorem; the full proof controls the first-order error.

Polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Result · volume elements

Polar: \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\). Cylindrical: \(dV = r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta\) (polar in \(x, y\), with \(z\) untouched). Spherical (\(\varphi\) from the positive \(z\)-axis, \(\varphi \in [0, \pi]\)): \(dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta\).

Derivation · the spherical Jacobian

With \(x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta\), \(y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta\), \(z = \rho\cos\varphi\), the Jacobian with columns \(\partial_\rho, \partial_\varphi, \partial_\theta\) is

$$J = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\varphi\cos\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\cos\theta & -\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta \\ \sin\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\cos\varphi\sin\theta & \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta \\ \cos\varphi & -\rho\sin\varphi & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Expand along the bottom row. The \((3,1)\) minor is \(\rho^2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\) and the \((3,2)\) minor is \(\rho\sin^2\varphi\), so \(\det J = \cos\varphi\cdot\rho^2\sin\varphi\cos\varphi + \rho\sin\varphi\cdot\rho\sin^2\varphi = \rho^2\sin\varphi\). It is non-negative because \(\sin\varphi \ge 0\) on \([0, \pi]\), which is one reason for that convention.

Choosing a substitution. Pick new variables that turn the region into a rectangle or the integrand into something integrable. Example: over the region bounded by \(x - y = 0, 2\) and \(x + y = 0, 3\), integrate \((x + y)e^{x^2 - y^2}\). With \(u = x + y\), \(v = x - y\): the region becomes \([0,3]\times[0,2]\), \(x^2 - y^2 = uv\), and \(x = \tfrac{u+v}2\), \(y = \tfrac{u - v}2\) give \(\big|\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)\big| = \tfrac12\). The integral is \(\tfrac12\int_0^3\int_0^2 ue^{uv}\,dv\,du = \tfrac12\int_0^3(e^{2u} - 1)\,du = \tfrac14(e^6 - 7)\).

Worked example

\(\iint_{\R^2} e^{-(x^2 + y^2)}\,dA\) and the Gaussian integral. The integrand is positive, so Tonelli lets us compute the improper integral in any coordinates: \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\big[-\tfrac12e^{-r^2}\big]_0^\infty = \pi\). On the other hand it factors: \(\iint e^{-x^2}e^{-y^2}\,dx\,dy = I^2\) with \(I = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}dx\). So \(I = \sqrt\pi\), finishing what L73 previewed. The factor \(r\) is what makes \(e^{-r^2}\) integrable in closed form.

Worked example

Volume of a ball of radius \(a\). \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^a \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\big[-\cos\varphi\big]_0^\pi\cdot\tfrac{a^3}{3} = 2\pi\cdot 2\cdot\tfrac{a^3}3 = \tfrac43\pi a^3\).

Transforming probability densities

Theorem · density of a transformed variable

Let \(X\) have density \(p_X\) on \(\R^n\) and \(Y = T(X)\) with \(T\) one-to-one and \(C^1\) with \(C^1\) inverse. Then

$$p_Y(y) = p_X(x)\,\left|\det\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right| = \frac{p_X(x)}{\left|\det J_T(x)\right|}, \qquad x = T^{-1}(y).$$

Proof in one line: \(P(Y \in B) = \int_{T^{-1}(B)} p_X(x)\,dx\), and changing variables \(x = T^{-1}(y)\) gives \(\int_B p_X(T^{-1}(y))\,|\det J_{T^{-1}}(y)|\,dy\). Probability mass is conserved, so wherever \(T\) stretches volume the density thins out. Normalizing flows are built on the log form, \(\log p_Y(y) = \log p_X(x) - \log|\det J_T(x)|\), with maps chosen so the determinant is cheap. L106 develops this for random variables.

Worked example

\(X \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\), \(Y = X^2\). On \((0, 1)\), \(T(x) = x^2\) is increasing with inverse \(x = \sqrt y\) and \(dx/dy = 1/(2\sqrt y)\). So \(p_Y(y) = 1\cdot\frac1{2\sqrt y}\) on \((0, 1)\). Check: \(\int_0^1\frac{dy}{2\sqrt y} = 1\), and \(P(Y \le y) = P(X \le \sqrt y) = \sqrt y\) differentiates to the same thing. The density blows up at 0 because squaring crushes \([0, \varepsilon]\) into the much shorter \([0, \varepsilon^2]\). If instead \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(-1,1)\), \(T\) is two-to-one and you add the contributions of both branches \(x = \pm\sqrt y\).

Common errors
  • Dropping the Jacobian: \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\), never \(dr\,d\theta\).
  • Forgetting the absolute value of the determinant (the \(u, v\) example has \(\det = -\tfrac12\)).
  • Spherical conventions: swapping the roles of \(\varphi\) and \(\theta\), losing \(\sin\varphi\), or letting \(\varphi\) run to \(2\pi\).
  • Inverting the density factor: multiply by \(|\det \partial x/\partial y|\), equivalently divide by \(|\det J_T|\).
  • Non-injective maps: sum over all preimages, as for \(Y = X^2\) with \(X\) symmetric.

Vector Fields, Line Integrals & Conservative Fields

34m video45m practice

A line integral adds up a field along a curve. For most fields the answer depends on the path; for gradient fields it does not, because the integral of a derivative is a difference of endpoint values. Telling the two apart, and recovering the potential when it exists, is the content of this lesson.

Vector fields and curves

Definition

A vector field on \(D \subseteq \R^2\) assigns a vector \(F(x, y) = (P(x,y), Q(x,y))\) to each point; in \(\R^3\), \(F = (P, Q, R)\). Sketch one by drawing \(F\) as a small arrow at sample points. A gradient field is \(F = \nabla\varphi\) for some scalar \(\varphi\), the potential. A parametrized curve is \(r: [a, b] \to \R^n\); it is smooth if \(r'\) is continuous and nonzero, piecewise smooth if it is a finite chain of smooth pieces. Its arc-length element is \(ds = \|r'(t)\|\,dt\).

Two kinds of line integral

Definition

Scalar field: \(\displaystyle\int_C f\,ds = \int_a^b f(r(t))\,\|r'(t)\|\,dt\): mass of a wire with density \(f\); it does not depend on the direction of travel.

Vector field: \(\displaystyle\int_C F\cdot dr = \int_a^b F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)\,dt = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy\ (+R\,dz)\): the work done by force \(F\) along \(C\). Reversing the orientation flips its sign. With \(T\) the unit tangent, \(F\cdot dr = (F\cdot T)\,ds\): only the component along the path counts.

Both are independent of the parametrization (up to orientation for the second), by the one-variable substitution rule. Example: on the upper unit semicircle, \(\int_C (2 + x^2y)\,ds = \int_0^\pi(2 + \cos^2t\sin t)\,dt = 2\pi + \tfrac23\). Around a closed curve, \(\oint_C F\cdot T\,ds\) is the circulation; in the plane, \(\oint_C F\cdot n\,ds\) with \(n\) the outward normal is the flux across \(C\), the net outflow.

Worked example · two paths, two answers

\(F = (-y, x)\) from \((1, 0)\) to \((-1, 0)\). Upper semicircle \(r = (\cos t, \sin t)\), \(t \in [0, \pi]\): \(F\cdot r' = (-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t) = 1\), so the integral is \(\pi\). Straight segment along the \(x\)-axis: \(y = 0\) so \(F = (0, x)\), perpendicular to the path, and the integral is \(0\). (The lower semicircle gives \(-\pi\).) The work depends on the route, so \(F\) is not a gradient field. The cross-partial test agrees: \(P_y = -1 \ne Q_x = 1\).

Conservative fields and the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

Theorem · Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals

If \(\varphi\) is \(C^1\) on an open set containing a piecewise-smooth curve \(C\) from \(A\) to \(B\), then \(\displaystyle\int_C \nabla\varphi\cdot dr = \varphi(B) - \varphi(A)\).

So for a gradient field the integral is path independent and every closed-loop integral is zero. On a connected open set the converse holds: if \(\int_C F\cdot dr\) depends only on the endpoints, then \(\varphi(x) = \int_{x_0}^x F\cdot dr\) (along any path) is a potential with \(\nabla\varphi = F\). Fields with these equivalent properties are called conservative.

Theorem · cross-partial test

If \(F = (P, Q)\) is \(C^1\) and conservative, then \(P_y = Q_x\), since \(P_y = \varphi_{xy} = \varphi_{yx} = Q_x\) by Clairaut. Conversely, if \(P_y = Q_x\) on a simply connected open domain (no holes), then \(F\) is conservative. In \(\R^3\) the test is \(\operatorname{curl}F = 0\), three equations (L87).

The hole matters. \(F = \big(\tfrac{-y}{x^2+y^2}, \tfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\big)\) on \(\R^2\setminus\{0\}\) satisfies \(P_y = Q_x = \tfrac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\), yet on the unit circle \(F\cdot r' = \sin^2t + \cos^2t = 1\) and \(\oint = 2\pi \ne 0\). Locally \(F = \nabla\theta\), the polar angle, but \(\theta\) cannot be defined continuously all the way around the origin.

Worked example · test and recover a potential

\(F = (3 + 2xy,\ x^2 - 3y^2)\) on \(\R^2\). Test: \(P_y = 2x = Q_x\), and \(\R^2\) is simply connected, so \(F\) is conservative. Integrate \(P\) in \(x\): \(\varphi = 3x + x^2y + g(y)\), where the "constant" may depend on \(y\). Match \(Q\): \(\varphi_y = x^2 + g'(y) = x^2 - 3y^2\), so \(g = -y^3\). Hence \(\varphi = 3x + x^2y - y^3 + C\). Any path from \((0, 0)\) to \((1, 2)\) then gives \(\varphi(1, 2) - \varphi(0,0) = 3 + 2 - 8 = -3\), with no parametrization needed.

Physical reading. For a conservative force \(F = -\nabla U\), work equals the drop in potential energy and energy is conserved, hence the name. The same chain-rule identity explains gradient flow \(\dot x = -\nabla L\): along it, \(dL/dt = \nabla L\cdot\dot x = -\|\nabla L\|^2 \le 0\), so the loss can only go down along its flow lines.

Common errors
  • Mixing up \(ds\) and \(dr\). \(\int f\,ds\) needs \(\|r'\|\); \(\int F\cdot dr\) uses \(r'\) itself.
  • Ignoring orientation. Reversing the path negates \(\int F\cdot dr\) but not \(\int f\,ds\).
  • Using the cross-partial test on a domain with a hole. The vortex field passes the test and is not conservative.
  • A true constant of integration. After integrating \(P\) in \(x\), the leftover is a function \(g(y)\) (and \(g(y, z)\) in 3D).
  • Checking one pair of partials in \(\R^3\). You need \(P_y = Q_x\), \(P_z = R_x\) and \(Q_z = R_y\).

Divergence, Curl, the Big Theorems & Phase 5 Workshop

40m video55m practice

Divergence measures how much a field spreads out of a point, curl how much it swirls around it. Green, Stokes and Gauss are three instances of one statement: integrating a derivative over a region gives the original quantity summed over the region's boundary.

The del operator: divergence, curl, Laplacian

Definition

With \(\nabla = (\partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_z)\) and \(F = (P, Q, R)\): gradient \(\nabla f\); divergence \(\nabla\cdot F = P_x + Q_y + R_z\) (a scalar); curl \(\nabla\times F = (R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y)\) (a vector); Laplacian \(\nabla^2 f = \nabla\cdot\nabla f = f_{xx} + f_{yy} + f_{zz}\). In the plane the scalar curl is \(Q_x - P_y\).

Divergence is source density: \(\nabla\cdot F(p) = \lim \frac1{\text{vol}(B)}\iint_{\partial B}F\cdot n\,dS\) as the ball \(B\) shrinks to \(p\), the outflow per unit volume. \(F = (x, y, z)\) has divergence 3 everywhere: it is a uniform source. Curl is local rotation: a tiny paddle wheel at \(p\) with axis \(n\) spins at angular speed \(\tfrac12(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\). For a rigid rotation \(F = \omega\times r\) with \(\omega = (0, 0, w)\), \(F = (-wy, wx, 0)\) and \(\nabla\times F = (0, 0, 2w)\). Laplacian: \(\nabla^2 f\) is the trace of the Hessian (L80), the sum of the curvatures; functions with \(\nabla^2 f = 0\) are harmonic, and they have no strict interior maxima or minima.

Theorem · two identities

For \(C^2\) \(f\) and \(F\): \(\nabla\times(\nabla f) = 0\) and \(\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times F) = 0\). Gradient fields are irrotational; curl fields are source-free.

The second: \(\partial_x(R_y - Q_z) + \partial_y(P_z - R_x) + \partial_z(Q_x - P_y)\); the six terms cancel in pairs by Clairaut. The first is the Prove it below.

Worked example · Laplacians and harmonicity

\(e^x\sin y\): \(f_{xx} = e^x\sin y\), \(f_{yy} = -e^x\sin y\), sum 0, harmonic. \(\ln(x^2 + y^2)\) on \(\R^2\setminus\{0\}\): \(f_{xx} = \frac{2(y^2 - x^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\), \(f_{yy} = \frac{2(x^2 - y^2)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}\), sum 0, harmonic. \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2\): Laplacian \(6 \ne 0\), not harmonic (its Hessian is \(2I\)).

Green's theorem

Theorem · Green

If \(D \subset \R^2\) is a bounded region whose boundary \(C\) is piecewise smooth and positively oriented (counterclockwise, \(D\) on the left), and \(P, Q\) are \(C^1\) on an open set containing \(D\), then

$$\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D (Q_x - P_y)\,dA .$$
Proof for a rectangle

On \(D = [a,b]\times[c,d]\), Fubini and the FTC give \(-\iint_D P_y\,dA = -\int_a^b[P(x,d) - P(x,c)]\,dx\). That is \(\int P\,dx\) along the bottom edge (left to right) plus the top edge (right to left); the vertical edges contribute nothing to \(\int P\,dx\) since \(dx = 0\) there. Likewise \(\iint_D Q_x\,dA = \oint_C Q\,dy\). General regions are glued from small cells; shared interior edges are traversed twice in opposite directions and cancel (figure).

Area corollary: choosing \(Q_x - P_y = 1\), \(\text{Area}(D) = \oint_C x\,dy = -\oint_C y\,dx = \tfrac12\oint_C(x\,dy - y\,dx)\). For the ellipse \(x = a\cos t\), \(y = b\sin t\): \(\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}ab\,dt = \pi ab\).

Worked example

Verify Green for \(F = (y^2, 3xy)\) on the unit square. Double integral: \(Q_x - P_y = 3y - 2y = y\), and \(\iint_{[0,1]^2} y\,dA = \tfrac12\). Line integral, counterclockwise: bottom (\(y = 0\)): \(P = 0\), contributes 0; right (\(x = 1\), \(y: 0 \to 1\)): \(\int_0^1 3y\,dy = \tfrac32\); top (\(y = 1\), \(x: 1 \to 0\)): \(\int_1^0 1\,dx = -1\); left (\(x = 0\)): \(Q = 0\), contributes 0. Total \(\tfrac32 - 1 = \tfrac12\). ✓

Surface integrals, Stokes and Gauss

A parametrized surface \(r(u, v)\), \((u,v) \in D\), has area element \(dS = \|r_u\times r_v\|\,du\,dv\) and oriented normal \(n = r_u\times r_v/\|r_u\times r_v\|\). The flux of \(F\) through \(S\) is \(\iint_S F\cdot n\,dS = \iint_D F(r(u,v))\cdot(r_u\times r_v)\,du\,dv\). For the sphere of radius \(a\), \(r(\varphi, \theta) = a(\sin\varphi\cos\theta, \sin\varphi\sin\theta, \cos\varphi)\) gives \(r_\varphi\times r_\theta = a^2\sin\varphi\,(\text{outward unit radial})\), so \(dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta\).

Theorems · Stokes and divergence (Gauss)

Stokes: for an oriented surface \(S\) with boundary curve \(\partial S\) oriented by the right-hand rule, \(\oint_{\partial S} F\cdot dr = \iint_S (\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS\). Green is the flat case \(n = (0,0,1)\).

Divergence: for a solid \(E\) with closed boundary surface \(\partial E\) and outward normal, \(\iint_{\partial E} F\cdot n\,dS = \iiint_E \nabla\cdot F\,dV\).

Worked example

Flux of \(F = (x^3, y^3, z^3)\) out of the sphere of radius \(a\). \(\nabla\cdot F = 3(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 3\rho^2\). In spherical coordinates (L85): \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^a 3\rho^2\cdot\rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 3\cdot2\pi\cdot2\cdot\tfrac{a^5}5 = \tfrac{12\pi a^5}{5}\). Sanity check with \(F = (x, y, z)\): divergence 3 gives \(4\pi a^3\), and directly \(F\cdot n = a\) on the sphere times area \(4\pi a^2\) gives the same.

Common errors
  • Orientation. Green needs counterclockwise (region on the left); Gauss needs the outward normal; Stokes needs the right-hand rule linking \(n\) and \(\partial S\).
  • Gauss on an open surface. The divergence theorem needs a closed surface; close it with a lid and subtract the lid's flux.
  • Singularities inside. The vortex field of L86 has zero scalar curl away from 0, yet circulation \(2\pi\): Green fails because the field is not \(C^1\) on the disc.
  • \(dS\) without the norm, or flux with the wrong sign of \(r_u\times r_v\).
