Matrix Calculus & Optimization Theory

Phase 6 turns multivariable calculus into optimization theory: differentiating vector and matrix expressions without leaving matrix notation, recognizing convexity, analysing gradient descent and Newton's method, and solving constrained problems through duality. It builds on the gradients, Hessians and KKT conditions of Phase 5, and its least-squares, ridge and convexity results return in Phases 7 and 8, where estimators are defined as solutions of optimization problems and Jensen's inequality drives the probability bounds.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Derive the standard matrix-calculus identities (quadratic forms, traces, log-determinants, inverses) with differentials, stating the layout convention and checking every shape.
  • Derive the OLS and ridge solutions by matrix calculus and explain ridge's shrinkage direction by direction with the SVD.
  • Prove that an objective is convex from the definition, the first- or second-order condition, or the composition rules, or show where convexity fails.
  • Prove the descent lemma and state gradient descent's convergence rates, with and without momentum, in terms of the condition number κ = L/μ.
  • Carry out Newton, Gauss–Newton and quasi-Newton steps by hand and say when each one fails.
  • Form the Lagrange dual of a linear program, a quadratic program and the SVM, and use weak duality, Slater's condition and complementary slackness.
  • Solve small linear programs graphically and with a simplex pivot, and equality-constrained quadratic programs through the KKT block system.

Matrix Calculus I: Layouts, Shapes & Basic Derivatives

36m video50m practice

Matrix calculus is ordinary partial differentiation, organized so you never have to write an index. Fix a layout convention, check the shape of every result, and compute with differentials: write \(df\) in the form \(g^\top dx\) and read off the gradient \(g\).

Why matrix calculus exists

The loss of a linear model, \(\|Ax - b\|^2\) with \(A \in \R^{m \times n}\), can be differentiated by expanding it as \(\sum_i(\sum_j A_{ij}x_j - b_i)^2\) and taking \(\partial/\partial x_k\). That is correct, but with three or four matrices in an expression the indices become unreadable, and you still have to recognize the result as a matrix product at the end. Matrix calculus does the bookkeeping once, as identities and rules, so derivations stay in the notation of linear algebra.

Columns: the output is a scalar \(y\), a vector \(y \in \R^m\) or a matrix \(Y \in \R^{p\times q}\). Rows: the input.

Numerator vs denominator layout

Definition · the two layouts

For \(y \in \R^m\), \(x \in \R^n\): numerator layout makes \(\partial y/\partial x\) an \(m \times n\) matrix (rows follow \(y\), the numerator), so a scalar's derivative \(\partial y/\partial x\) is a \(1 \times n\) row. Denominator layout is the transpose: \(n \times m\), and a scalar's gradient is an \(n \times 1\) column.

Books disagree. Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong write gradients as rows; the Matrix Cookbook and most optimization texts write \(\partial(a^\top x)/\partial x = a\) as a column. Every identity has a transposed twin in the other layout, and mixing them mid-derivation is the commonest source of a stray transpose.

Convention used in this course

Jacobians in numerator layout: \(J_F \in \R^{m \times n}\), \(J_{ij} = \partial F_i/\partial x_j\), as in L79, so the chain rule is \(J_{F\circ G} = J_FJ_G\). Gradients shaped like the variable: \(\nabla_x f \in \R^n\) is a column and \(\partial f/\partial X\) has the shape of \(X\). The two are linked by \(\nabla f = (Df)^\top\), where \(Df\) is the \(1\times n\) Jacobian. State the convention at the top of any derivation you hand to someone else.

The shape-checking discipline

The derivative of a scalar with respect to an \(n\)-vector must be an \(n\)-vector, and with respect to a \(p \times q\) matrix a \(p \times q\) matrix. Before simplifying anything, write the shape under every factor and check that the product is defined and lands on the right shape. This one check catches most wrong transposes.

First derivatives from the index definition

Derivation · three basic results

\(\partial(a^\top x)/\partial x = a\). \(a^\top x = \sum_i a_ix_i\), so \(\partial/\partial x_k = a_k\): the gradient is \(a\). Since \(x^\top a = a^\top x\) (a scalar equals its transpose), \(\partial(x^\top a)/\partial x = a\) too.

\(\partial(Ax)/\partial x = A\). \((Ax)_i = \sum_j A_{ij}x_j\), so \(\partial(Ax)_i/\partial x_j = A_{ij}\): the Jacobian (numerator layout) is \(A\). In denominator layout the same fact reads \(A^\top\).

The differential method

Algorithm · the differential method
  1. Rules: \(d(\alpha X + \beta Y) = \alpha\,dX + \beta\,dY\); \(d(XY) = (dX)Y + X(dY)\) (keep the order); \(d(X^\top) = (dX)^\top\); \(d\,\tr X = \tr(dX)\); \(dA = 0\) for constants.
  2. Expand \(df\) and collect it into a standard form.
  3. Read off: scalar \(f\) of a vector, \(df = g^\top dx\) ⇒ \(\nabla f = g\). Vector \(F\), \(dF = J\,dx\) ⇒ Jacobian \(J\). Scalar \(f\) of a matrix, \(df = \tr(G^\top dX)\) ⇒ \(\partial f/\partial X = G\).
Tool · the trace trick

For \(A \in \R^{m\times n}\), \(B \in \R^{n\times m}\): \(\tr(AB) = \tr(BA)\), since both equal \(\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}\). Hence traces are invariant under cyclic permutations, \(\tr(ABC) = \tr(BCA) = \tr(CAB)\); a scalar equals its own trace; \(\tr(M^\top) = \tr M\); and \(\tr(A^\top B) = \sum_{ij}A_{ij}B_{ij}\) is the matrix inner product. Use them to move \(dX\) to the far right.

Worked example

\(\partial(a^\top x)/\partial x\) both ways, then a matrix variable. Indices: done above, \(a\). Differentials: \(d(a^\top x) = a^\top dx\), already of the form \(g^\top dx\) with \(g = a\).

Matrix variable: \(f(X) = a^\top Xb\) with \(X \in \R^{p\times q}\), \(a \in \R^p\), \(b \in \R^q\). Then \(df = a^\top(dX)b = \tr(a^\top\,dX\,b) = \tr(ba^\top dX) = \tr\big((ab^\top)^\top dX\big)\), so \(\partial f/\partial X = ab^\top\), a \(p \times q\) matrix, the shape of \(X\). ✓

Common errors
  • Mixing layouts between steps, producing \(A\) where \(A^\top\) belongs. Fix one convention and write shapes down.
  • Commuting matrices in the product rule. \(d(X^2) = (dX)X + X(dX)\), not \(2X\,dX\).
  • Non-cyclic trace moves. \(\tr(ABC) \ne \tr(BAC)\) in general.
  • A gradient that does not have the shape of the variable. Something is transposed.
  • Testing only on square matrices, where a wrong transpose still type-checks.

  1. Prove it. \(\partial(x^\top x)/\partial x = 2x\), both by indices and by differentials. Hint: for differentials, \(dx^\top x\) is a scalar, so it equals its transpose.
    Proof sketch

    Indices: \(x^\top x = \sum_i x_i^2\), so \(\partial/\partial x_k = 2x_k\), i.e. \(2x\). Differentials: \(d(x^\top x) = (dx)^\top x + x^\top dx\). The first term is a scalar, so \((dx)^\top x = x^\top dx\), giving \(d(x^\top x) = 2x^\top dx = (2x)^\top dx\), hence \(\nabla = 2x\). Shape: \(n\), the shape of \(x\). ✓

  2. Gradient of \(f = b^\top Ax\) with respect to \(x\), and with respect to \(b\).
    Answer

    \(df = b^\top A\,dx = (A^\top b)^\top dx\), so \(\nabla_x = A^\top b \in \R^n\); \(df = (Ax)^\top db\), so \(\nabla_b = Ax \in \R^m\).

  3. Jacobian of \(F(x) = (a^\top x)\,x\).
    Answer

    \(dF = (a^\top dx)x + (a^\top x)dx = (xa^\top + (a^\top x)I)\,dx\): \(J = xa^\top + (a^\top x)I\).

  4. Show \(\partial\,\tr(AX)/\partial X = A^\top\) and \(\partial\,\tr X/\partial X = I\).
    Answer

    \(d\,\tr(AX) = \tr(A\,dX) = \tr\big((A^\top)^\top dX\big)\). With \(A = I\), \(\partial\tr X/\partial X = I\).

  5. Find \(2\times2\) matrices with \(\tr(ABC) \ne \tr(BAC)\).
    Answer

    \(A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(B = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(C = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\): \(\tr(ABC) = 1\), \(\tr(BAC) = 0\).

  6. Gradient of \(\|x\|\) for \(x \ne 0\).
    Answer

    \(d(x^\top x)^{1/2} = \tfrac12(x^\top x)^{-1/2}\,2x^\top dx\), so \(\nabla\|x\| = x/\|x\|\).

Matrix Calculus II: The Identity Toolbox

38m video55m practice

A dozen identities cover almost every gradient you will meet in machine learning, and each one is a three-line differential computation. Derive them once and the normal equations, the ridge solution and the famous softmax cross-entropy gradient \(\hat y - y\) all fall out in a few lines.

Vector identities

Convention from L88: gradients have the shape of the variable; read them off \(df = g^\top dx\) or \(df = \tr(G^\top dX)\).

Derivations

\(\nabla(a^\top x) = a\): \(d(a^\top x) = a^\top dx\).

\(\nabla(x^\top Ax) = (A + A^\top)x\): \(d(x^\top Ax) = (dx)^\top Ax + x^\top A\,dx\). The first term is a scalar, so it equals its transpose \(x^\top A^\top dx\). Total \(x^\top(A^\top + A)\,dx = \big((A + A^\top)x\big)^\top dx\). For symmetric \(A\) this is \(2Ax\).

\(\nabla\|x\|^2 = 2x\): the case \(A = I\).

\(\nabla\|Ax - b\|^2 = 2A^\top(Ax - b)\): with \(r = Ax - b\), \(d(r^\top r) = 2r^\top dr = 2r^\top A\,dx = \big(2A^\top r\big)^\top dx\).

Matrix identities

Derivations

\(\partial\,\tr(AX)/\partial X = A^\top\): \(d\,\tr(AX) = \tr(A\,dX) = \tr\big((A^\top)^\top dX\big)\).

\(\partial\,\tr(X^\top AX)/\partial X = (A + A^\top)X\): \(d\,\tr(X^\top AX) = \tr(dX^\top AX) + \tr(X^\top A\,dX)\). Transpose inside the first trace: \(\tr(dX^\top AX) = \tr(X^\top A^\top dX)\). So \(G^\top = X^\top A^\top + X^\top A\), i.e. \(G = (A + A^\top)X\).

\(d(X^{-1}) = -X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}\), so \(\partial X^{-1}/\partial t = -X^{-1}\frac{\partial X}{\partial t}X^{-1}\): differentiate \(XX^{-1} = I\) to get \((dX)X^{-1} + X\,d(X^{-1}) = 0\) and multiply by \(X^{-1}\) on the left.

Jacobi's formula \(d\det X = \det X\,\tr(X^{-1}dX)\): \(\det(X + dX) = \det X\,\det(I + X^{-1}dX)\), and \(\det(I + E) = \prod_i(1 + \varepsilon_i) = 1 + \tr E + O(\|E\|^2)\) with \(\varepsilon_i\) the eigenvalues of \(E\). Hence \(\partial\det X/\partial X = \det(X)\,X^{-\top}\) and, dividing by \(\det X\), \(\partial\log\det X/\partial X = X^{-\top}\) (for \(\det X > 0\)), which is \(X^{-1}\) when \(X\) is symmetric.

Chain rule and vec notation. If \(f = g(Y)\) with \(Y = h(X)\), write \(df = \tr(G_Y^\top dY)\), substitute \(dY\) in terms of \(dX\), and cycle the trace until \(dX\) is on the right. For completeness: stacking columns, \(\operatorname{vec}(AXB) = (B^\top\otimes A)\operatorname{vec}X\), so the Jacobian of \(X \mapsto AXB\) is the Kronecker product \(B^\top\otimes A\). You rarely need it by hand, but it is how a "matrix-by-matrix" derivative is made into an ordinary Jacobian.

The normal equations by matrix calculus

Derivation · least squares

Expand \(f(x) = \|Ax - b\|^2 = x^\top A^\top Ax - 2b^\top Ax + b^\top b\) (the two cross terms are equal scalars). Using the identities with the symmetric matrix \(A^\top A\): \(\nabla f = 2A^\top Ax - 2A^\top b\). Setting it to zero gives the normal equations \(A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b\). The Hessian is \(2A^\top A \succeq 0\), so \(f\) is convex and every solution is a global minimum; if \(A\) has independent columns, \(A^\top A\) is invertible and \(\hat x = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top b\) is unique.

Ridge regression in closed form

Derivation · ridge

\(f(x) = \|Ax - b\|^2 + \lambda\|x\|^2\) with \(\lambda > 0\). Then \(\nabla f = 2A^\top(Ax - b) + 2\lambda x = 0\), so \((A^\top A + \lambda I)\hat x_\lambda = A^\top b\) and \(\hat x_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top b\). The inverse always exists: for \(v \ne 0\), \(v^\top(A^\top A + \lambda I)v = \|Av\|^2 + \lambda\|v\|^2 \ge \lambda\|v\|^2 > 0\), so the matrix is positive definite (L56), even when \(A\) has dependent columns or more columns than rows.

Worked example · OLS and ridge for three points

Fit \(y = c_0 + c_1t\) to \((0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)\): \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), \(b = (1, 2, 2)\). Then \(A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}\), \(A^\top b = (5, 6)\), determinant 6, so \(\hat x = \tfrac16\begin{pmatrix}5&-3\\-3&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5\\6\end{pmatrix} = (\tfrac76, \tfrac12)\). Residual \((\tfrac16, -\tfrac13, \tfrac16)\): it sums to 0 and is orthogonal to \(t = (0,1,2)\). ✓

Ridge, \(\lambda = 1\) (penalizing both coefficients): \(A^\top A + I = \begin{pmatrix}4&3\\3&6\end{pmatrix}\), determinant 15, so \(\hat x_1 = \tfrac1{15}\begin{pmatrix}6&-3\\-3&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5\\6\end{pmatrix} = (0.8, 0.6)\). Its norm is 1.0 against 1.27 for OLS: shrunk toward the origin.

Gradients of common losses

Squared error \(\tfrac12\|\hat y - y\|^2\) with \(\hat y = Wx\): \(dL = (\hat y - y)^\top dW\,x = \tr\big(x(\hat y - y)^\top dW\big)\), so \(\partial L/\partial W = (\hat y - y)x^\top\), the outer product of error and input.

Worked example

Softmax cross-entropy gives \(\hat y - y\). Logits \(z \in \R^K\), \(\hat y = \operatorname{softmax}(z)\), target \(y\) a probability vector (\(\sum_k y_k = 1\), e.g. one-hot). Loss \(L = -\sum_k y_k\log\hat y_k\). Since \(\log\hat y_k = z_k - \log\sum_j e^{z_j}\),

$$L = -\sum_k y_kz_k + \Big(\sum_k y_k\Big)\log\sum_j e^{z_j} = -y^\top z + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}.$$

Differentiate: \(\partial L/\partial z_i = -y_i + e^{z_i}/\sum_je^{z_j} = \hat y_i - y_i\). So \(\nabla_zL = \hat y - y\). Check with the softmax Jacobian of L79: \(\nabla_z L = (\operatorname{diag}\hat y - \hat y\hat y^\top)(-y/\hat y) = -y + \hat y\,(\mathbf 1^\top y) = \hat y - y\). For a linear layer \(z = Wx + b\): \(\partial L/\partial W = (\hat y - y)x^\top\), \(\partial L/\partial b = \hat y - y\).

Common errors
  • \(2Ax\) for non-symmetric \(A\). The gradient of \(x^\top Ax\) is \((A + A^\top)x\).
  • \(\partial\log\det X/\partial X = X^{-1}\) in general. It is \(X^{-\top}\); the two agree only for symmetric \(X\).
  • \(d(X^{-1}) = -X^{-2}dX\). Matrices do not commute; the correct form is \(-X^{-1}(dX)X^{-1}\).
  • Forgetting \(\sum y_k = 1\) in the cross-entropy derivation; without it you get \(\hat y\sum y_k - y\).
  • Penalizing the intercept by accident in ridge. Usually the constant column is left unpenalized (or the data are centred first).

Convex Sets & Convex Functions

36m video50m practice

A convex function lies above all of its tangent planes, so local information is global: a zero gradient is a global minimum and there are no spurious local minima. Convexity is checked by a short list of rules rather than from scratch, and recognizing it is the most useful skill in optimization.

Convex sets

Definition

\(C \subseteq \R^n\) is convex if \(x, y \in C\) and \(\theta \in [0, 1]\) imply \(\theta x + (1 - \theta)y \in C\): the whole segment between any two points stays inside. The convex hull \(\operatorname{conv}S\) is the set of all convex combinations \(\sum\theta_ix_i\) (\(\theta_i \ge 0\), \(\sum\theta_i = 1\)) of points of \(S\), the smallest convex set containing \(S\).

The catalogue. Hyperplanes \(\{a^\top x = b\}\) and halfspaces \(\{a^\top x \le b\}\) (linearity). Norm balls \(\{\|x - c\| \le r\}\) in any norm: \(\|\theta x + (1-\theta)y - c\| \le \theta\|x - c\| + (1-\theta)\|y - c\| \le r\) by the triangle inequality and homogeneity. Ellipsoids \(\{(x - c)^\top P^{-1}(x - c) \le 1\}\), \(P \succ 0\), the affine image \(c + P^{1/2}(\text{unit ball})\). Polyhedra \(\{Ax \le b,\ Cx = d\}\), intersections of halfspaces and hyperplanes. The PSD cone \(\{X = X^\top : v^\top Xv \ge 0\ \forall v\}\): \(v^\top(\theta X + (1-\theta)Y)v = \theta v^\top Xv + (1-\theta)v^\top Yv \ge 0\).

Operations that preserve convexity: intersections of any number of convex sets; affine images and preimages \(\{Ax + b : x \in C\}\), \(\{x : Ax + b \in C\}\); Cartesian products.

Convex functions

Definition

\(f\) with convex domain is convex if \(f(\theta x + (1-\theta)y) \le \theta f(x) + (1-\theta)f(y)\) for all \(x, y\), \(\theta \in [0,1]\): the chord lies above the graph. Strictly convex: strict inequality for \(x \ne y\), \(\theta \in (0,1)\). \(\mu\)-strongly convex (\(\mu > 0\)): \(f - \tfrac\mu2\|x\|^2\) is convex. Concave: \(-f\) convex. The epigraph is \(\operatorname{epi}f = \{(x, t) : f(x) \le t\}\), and \(f\) is convex iff \(\operatorname{epi}f\) is a convex set (the chord inequality says exactly that convex combinations of points above the graph stay above it).

First- and second-order conditions

Theorem · first-order condition

A differentiable \(f\) on an open convex domain is convex iff \(f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x)\) for all \(x, y\): the graph lies above every tangent plane.

Proof

(⇒) Convexity gives \(f(x + \theta(y - x)) \le f(x) + \theta\big(f(y) - f(x)\big)\). Subtract \(f(x)\), divide by \(\theta > 0\), and let \(\theta \to 0^+\): the left side tends to \(\nabla f(x)^\top(y - x)\), so \(\nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) \le f(y) - f(x)\). (⇐) Put \(z = \theta x + (1-\theta)y\). The inequality at \(z\) gives \(f(x) \ge f(z) + \nabla f(z)^\top(x - z)\) and \(f(y) \ge f(z) + \nabla f(z)^\top(y - z)\). Take \(\theta\) times the first plus \(1 - \theta\) times the second: the gradient terms combine to \(\nabla f(z)^\top(\theta x + (1-\theta)y - z) = 0\), leaving \(\theta f(x) + (1-\theta)f(y) \ge f(z)\).

Theorem · second-order condition

A twice-differentiable \(f\) on an open convex domain is convex iff \(\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0\) for every \(x\). If \(\nabla^2 f \succ 0\) everywhere it is strictly convex (the converse fails: \(x^4\) is strictly convex with \(f''(0) = 0\)), and \(\nabla^2f \succeq \mu I\) everywhere means \(\mu\)-strongly convex.

Reason: \(f\) is convex iff each restriction \(g(t) = f(x + tv)\) to a line is convex, and \(g''(t) = v^\top\nabla^2f(x + tv)\,v\) (L80). So convexity is the definiteness test of L56 applied to the Hessian at every point.

Building convex functions

Rules · operations preserving convexity
  • Non-negative combinations: \(\sum w_if_i\), \(w_i \ge 0\).
  • Pointwise maximum (or supremum): \(\max_i f_i\). Each \(f_i(\theta x + (1-\theta)y) \le \theta f_i(x) + (1-\theta)f_i(y) \le \theta\max f(x) + (1-\theta)\max f(y)\).
  • Affine composition: \(f(Ax + b)\).
  • Scalar composition: \(h(g(x))\) is convex if \(h\) is convex and non-decreasing and \(g\) is convex, or \(h\) is convex and non-increasing and \(g\) is concave. In 1D: \((h\circ g)'' = h''(g)g'^2 + h'(g)g'' \ge 0\).
Theorem · Jensen's inequality

For convex \(f\): \(f\big(\sum\theta_ix_i\big) \le \sum\theta_if(x_i)\) for any convex combination, and \(f(\E X) \le \E f(X)\) for any integrable random vector \(X\).

Proof of the expectation form: let \(\mu = \E X\) and take a supporting hyperplane at \(\mu\), \(f(x) \ge f(\mu) + g^\top(x - \mu)\) (the tangent plane when \(f\) is differentiable). Take expectations: \(\E f(X) \ge f(\mu) + g^\top(\E X - \mu) = f(\mu)\). Jensen returns in L107 and in the non-negativity of KL divergence (L118). Also: every sublevel set \(\{x : f(x) \le \alpha\}\) of a convex \(f\) is convex; the converse fails (\(\sqrt{|x|}\) has interval sublevel sets but is not convex).

Worked example

\(x^2\), \(e^x\), \(-\log x\), \(\|x\|\). \((x^2)'' = 2 > 0\); \((e^x)'' = e^x > 0\); \((-\log x)'' = 1/x^2 > 0\) on \(x > 0\): all strictly convex. For any norm, \(\|\theta x + (1-\theta)y\| \le \theta\|x\| + (1-\theta)\|y\|\) by the triangle inequality and homogeneity, so norms are convex. They are not differentiable at 0, so the chord definition is the right tool there.

Worked example · logistic loss and hinge loss

Logistic \(\ell(t) = \log(1 + e^{-t})\): \(\ell'(t) = -\frac{e^{-t}}{1 + e^{-t}} = \sigma(t) - 1\), and \(\ell''(t) = \sigma(t)(1 - \sigma(t)) > 0\). As a function of weights, \(\ell(y\,w^\top x)\) is convex by affine composition, with Hessian \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\,xx^\top \succeq 0\).

Hinge \(h(t) = \max(0, 1 - t)\): a maximum of two affine functions, hence convex. At \(t = 1\) the left derivative is \(-1\) and the right derivative 0, so it is not differentiable there; any slope in \([-1, 0]\) gives a supporting line (a subgradient).

Standard ML objectives. Squared error \(\|Ax - b\|^2\) (Hessian \(2A^\top A \succeq 0\)), logistic and hinge losses of linear models, and the \(\ell_1\), \(\ell_2\) penalties are convex, and so are their sums. Neural network losses are not: for \(L(w_1, w_2) = (1 - w_1w_2)^2\), a one-unit "network", \((1,1)\) and \((-1,-1)\) both have loss 0 but their midpoint \((0,0)\) has loss 1. Symmetries like this (swapping hidden units) make non-convexity unavoidable.

Common errors
  • Checking \(f'' \ge 0\) at one point. The condition must hold on the whole (convex) domain.
  • Products and compositions of convex functions. \(x\cdot x^2 = x^3\) is not convex; \((x^2 - 1)^2\), convex \(u^2\) of convex \(x^2 - 1\), is not convex because \(u^2\) is not monotone.
  • Strictly convex ⇒ positive definite Hessian. False: \(x^4\) at 0.
  • Convex sublevel sets ⇒ convex function. False: \(\sqrt{|x|}\).
  • Jensen in the wrong direction for concave functions: \(\log\E X \ge \E\log X\).

Gradient Descent: The Mathematics

38m video50m practice

Gradient descent minimizes a linear model of \(f\) plus a penalty for moving far. Its guarantees rest on one inequality, the descent lemma, and its speed is governed by one number, the condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\); momentum improves the dependence from \(\kappa\) to \(\sqrt\kappa\).

Descent directions and the update

\(d\) is a descent direction at \(x\) if \(\nabla f(x)^\top d < 0\): then \(f(x + td) < f(x)\) for small \(t > 0\); the steepest is \(-\nabla f\) (L78). Trust the first-order Taylor model only nearby:

$$x_{k+1} = \argmin_y\Big\{f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top(y - x_k) + \frac1{2\alpha}\|y - x_k\|^2\Big\} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k),$$

since the bracket's gradient in \(y\) is \(\nabla f(x_k) + (y - x_k)/\alpha\). The step size (learning rate) \(\alpha\) sets how far the linear model is trusted. Too small: steady but slow progress. Too large: overshoot and oscillation. Beyond a threshold: divergence.

L-smoothness and the descent lemma

Definition · L-smooth

\(f\) is \(L\)-smooth if \(\|\nabla f(x) - \nabla f(y)\| \le L\|x - y\|\) for all \(x, y\). For \(C^2\) functions this means \(-LI \preceq \nabla^2 f \preceq LI\): curvature at most \(L\) in every direction. For a quadratic \(\tfrac12x^\top Ax\), \(L = \lambda_{\max}(A)\).

Lemma · descent lemma

If \(f\) is \(L\)-smooth, then \(f(y) \le f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y - x) + \frac L2\|y - x\|^2\) for all \(x, y\): \(f\) lies below a quadratic upper model at every point.

Consequence: for \(y = x - \alpha\nabla f(x)\), \(f(y) \le f(x) - \alpha\big(1 - \tfrac{L\alpha}{2}\big)\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\). Any \(0 < \alpha < 2/L\) decreases \(f\) unless \(\nabla f = 0\), and \(\alpha = 1/L\), the minimizer of the upper model, gives the largest guaranteed decrease (Prove it).

Convergence rates and the condition number

Theorem · rates for α = 1/L

Convex and \(L\)-smooth: \(f(x_k) - f^* \le \dfrac{L\|x_0 - x^*\|^2}{2k}\), a sublinear \(O(1/k)\) rate.
\(\mu\)-strongly convex and \(L\)-smooth: \(f(x_k) - f^* \le (1 - \mu/L)^k\,(f(x_0) - f^*)\), linear (geometric) convergence.

Proof sketches

Strongly convex. Minimizing the strong-convexity lower bound \(f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f^\top(y - x) + \tfrac\mu2\|y - x\|^2\) over \(y\) gives \(f^* \ge f(x) - \tfrac1{2\mu}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2\), i.e. \(\|\nabla f\|^2 \ge 2\mu(f - f^*)\). Combine with the per-step decrease \(\|\nabla f\|^2/(2L)\): \(f(x_{k+1}) - f^* \le (1 - \mu/L)(f(x_k) - f^*)\).

Convex. Convexity plus the same decrease give \(f(x_{k+1}) - f^* \le \tfrac L2\big(\|x_k - x^*\|^2 - \|x_{k+1} - x^*\|^2\big)\); summing telescopes, and monotonicity of \(f(x_k)\) yields the \(1/k\) bound.

The number that matters is \(\kappa = L/\mu\), the ratio of largest to smallest curvature: for a quadratic, exactly the condition number of its Hessian from L58 and L80. Reaching accuracy \(\varepsilon\) takes about \(\kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)\) iterations. Geometrically, large \(\kappa\) means long thin level ellipses; the gradient points nearly across the valley, and the iterates zig-zag.

Worked example · best fixed step, and divergence

For \(f = \tfrac12x^\top Ax\) each eigencomponent is multiplied by \(1 - \alpha\lambda_i\) per step. Stability needs \(|1 - \alpha\lambda_i| < 1\) for all \(i\), i.e. \(0 < \alpha < 2/L\). The worst factor \(\max(|1 - \alpha\mu|, |1 - \alpha L|)\) is minimized when the two are equal: \(\alpha^* = \frac{2}{L + \mu}\), giving rate \(\frac{L - \mu}{L + \mu} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}\). Line search: exact and backtracking

Exact line search picks \(\alpha_k = \argmin_{\alpha > 0}f(x_k - \alpha g_k)\); for \(\tfrac12x^\top Ax\) this is \(\alpha_k = g_k^\top g_k/g_k^\top Ag_k\) (from \((8,2)\) above: \(1856/32512 \approx 0.057\)). Backtracking (Armijo): start at \(\alpha_0\) and multiply by \(\rho \in (0,1)\) until \(f(x - \alpha g) \le f(x) - c\,\alpha\|g\|^2\) with \(c \in (0, 1)\) (often \(10^{-4}\)). By the descent lemma every \(\alpha \le 2(1 - c)/L\) passes, so the search stops after finitely many halvings without knowing \(L\).

Momentum and Nesterov acceleration

Heavy ball (Polyak): \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\nabla f(x_k) + \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})\). Nesterov: \(y_k = x_k + \beta(x_k - x_{k-1})\), \(x_{k+1} = y_k - \alpha\nabla f(y_k)\), the gradient taken at the look-ahead point.

Analysis · heavy ball on a quadratic

In the eigenbasis of \(A\), each error component obeys \(e_{k+1} = (1 + \beta - \alpha\lambda)e_k - \beta e_{k-1}\), with characteristic roots solving \(r^2 - (1 + \beta - \alpha\lambda)r + \beta = 0\); complex roots both have modulus \(\sqrt\beta\). With \(\alpha = \frac{4}{(\sqrt L + \sqrt\mu)^2}\), \(\beta = \big(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\big)^2\) one checks \(1 + \beta - \alpha\lambda = \pm2\sqrt\beta\) at \(\lambda = \mu, L\), so the discriminant is \(\le 0\) on all of \([\mu, L]\) and every mode contracts at \(\sqrt\beta = \frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1} \approx 1 - \frac2{\sqrt\kappa}\) per step (up to a factor linear in \(k\) at the endpoints), against \(\frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} \approx 1 - \frac2\kappa\) without momentum: order \(\sqrt\kappa\) iterations instead of \(\kappa\).

For general smooth convex functions, Nesterov's method with a suitable momentum schedule achieves \(f(x_k) - f^* = O(1/k^2)\) and, for strongly convex ones, roughly \((1 - 1/\sqrt\kappa)^k\); these rates are optimal for first-order methods (stated here without proof).

Schedules and stochastic gradients

Replace \(\nabla f\) by an unbiased estimate \(g = \nabla f + \xi\), \(\E\xi = 0\), \(\E\|\xi\|^2 \le \sigma^2\) (a minibatch gradient). Taking expectations in the descent lemma, using \(\E\|g\|^2 = \|\nabla f\|^2 + \E\|\xi\|^2\):

$$\E f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \alpha\big(1 - \tfrac{L\alpha}{2}\big)\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2 + \tfrac{L\alpha^2}{2}\sigma^2 .$$

The last term is the price of noise. For \(\mu\)-strongly convex \(f\) and \(\alpha \le 1/L\): \(\E[f_{k+1} - f^*] \le (1 - \alpha\mu)\E[f_k - f^*] + \tfrac{L\alpha^2\sigma^2}{2}\), whose fixed point is a floor \(\frac{L\alpha\sigma^2}{2\mu}\) proportional to \(\alpha\). Decaying the step removes the floor: schedules with \(\sum\alpha_k = \infty\), \(\sum\alpha_k^2 < \infty\) (e.g. \(\alpha_k \propto 1/k\)) give \(\E[f_k - f^*] = O(1/k)\) here. That is the mathematics behind "decay the learning rate".

Common errors
  • \(\alpha \ge 2/L\). The steepest direction diverges, however flat the others are.
  • Confusing \(L\) with \(\|\nabla f\|\). \(L\) bounds curvature (the largest Hessian eigenvalue), not slope.
  • Expecting \(-\nabla f\) to aim at the minimum. It does only when \(\kappa = 1\).
  • Misreading a linear rate. \((1 - 1/\kappa)^k\) needs about \(\kappa\ln(1/\varepsilon)\) steps, not \(\ln(1/\varepsilon)\).

Newton & Quasi-Newton Methods

34m video45m practice

Newton's method replaces \(f\) by its second-order Taylor model and jumps to the model's minimizer. Near a good minimum this doubles the number of correct digits every step and ignores conditioning entirely; the price is the Hessian, which quasi-Newton and Gauss–Newton methods approximate instead of computing.

Newton's method for roots

To solve \(g(x) = 0\), replace \(g\) by its tangent line at \(x_k\), \(g(x_k) + g'(x_k)(x - x_k)\), and solve that instead: \(x_{k+1} = x_k - g(x_k)/g'(x_k)\).

Theorem · quadratic convergence

If \(g \in C^2\), \(g(r) = 0\) and \(g'(r) \ne 0\), then for \(x_0\) close enough to \(r\), \(|x_{k+1} - r| \le M|x_k - r|^2\) for a constant \(M\) (about \(|g''(r)/2g'(r)|\)).

Proof

Taylor at \(x_k\): \(0 = g(r) = g(x_k) + g'(x_k)(r - x_k) + \tfrac12g''(\xi)(r - x_k)^2\). Divide by \(g'(x_k)\) and use \(x_{k+1} = x_k - g(x_k)/g'(x_k)\): \(r - x_{k+1} = -\frac{g''(\xi)}{2g'(x_k)}(r - x_k)^2\). Near \(r\), \(|g''(\xi)/2g'(x_k)|\) is bounded because \(g'(r) \ne 0\).

Worked example

Three iterations on \(x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0\). \(g' = 3x^2 - 2\). From \(x_0 = 2\): \(g = -1\), \(g' = 10\), so \(x_1 = 2.1\). Then \(g(2.1) = 0.061\), \(g'(2.1) = 11.23\), \(x_2 = 2.1 - 0.061/11.23 = 2.0945681\). Then \(g(x_2) \approx 1.857\times10^{-4}\), \(g'(x_2) \approx 11.1616\), \(x_3 = 2.0945515\). The root is \(2.09455148\ldots\); the errors are about \(9\times10^{-2}\), \(5\times10^{-3}\), \(2\times10^{-5}\), \(2\times10^{-10}\): the exponent doubles each step. (This is the equation Newton himself used to illustrate the method.)

Newton's method for optimization

A minimizer satisfies \(\nabla f = 0\). Root-finding on the gradient, whose Jacobian is the Hessian, gives the Newton step \(x_{k+1} = x_k - H(x_k)^{-1}\nabla f(x_k)\) (computed by solving \(H\,p = -\nabla f\), not by inverting). The same step comes from the local quadratic model of L80: \(m(p) = f + g^\top p + \tfrac12p^\top Hp\) has \(\nabla m = g + Hp = 0\) at \(p = -H^{-1}g\), its minimizer when \(H \succ 0\). Newton trusts the quadratic model completely; gradient descent trusts only the linear part.

Property · affine invariance

Substitute \(x = Ty\) with \(T\) invertible and let \(\tilde f(y) = f(Ty)\). Then \(\nabla\tilde f = T^\top g\), \(\nabla^2\tilde f = T^\top HT\), and the Newton step in \(y\) is \(-(T^\top HT)^{-1}T^\top g = -T^{-1}H^{-1}g\), which maps back to exactly \(-H^{-1}g\) in \(x\). Rescaling or rotating coordinates changes nothing, so conditioning that cripples gradient descent (L91) does not affect Newton. Gradient descent is not invariant: its step \(-T^\top g\) maps to \(-TT^\top g\).

Worked example · one Newton step on a 2D quadratic

\(f = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3x\) from \((0, 0)\): \(\nabla f = (2x + y - 3,\ x + 2y) = (-3, 0)\), \(H = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Solve \(Hp = (3, 0)\): \(p = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\end{pmatrix} = (2, -1)\). So \(x_1 = (2, -1)\), where \(\nabla f = (4 - 1 - 3,\ 2 - 2) = 0\): the exact minimizer in one step.

Failure modes and safeguards

  • Indefinite or singular \(H\). The model has no minimizer; \(-H^{-1}g\) may point uphill or toward a saddle, since Newton is attracted to any stationary point.
  • Overshooting far from the optimum. For \(f = \sqrt{1 + x^2}\), \(f' = x/\sqrt{1 + x^2}\) and \(f'' = (1 + x^2)^{-3/2}\), so the Newton map is \(x \mapsto -x^3\): it converges for \(|x_0| < 1\) and diverges for \(|x_0| > 1\), on a convex function with a unique minimum.

Fixes. Damped Newton keeps the direction but chooses the step length by backtracking line search, giving global convergence on strongly convex problems and the pure quadratic rate near the optimum. Trust-region methods minimize the model only within \(\|p\| \le \Delta\) and adapt \(\Delta\) to how well the model predicted the actual decrease. Levenberg–Marquardt damping solves \((H + \lambda I)p = -g\): \(\lambda \to 0\) recovers Newton, \(\lambda \to \infty\) gives \(p \approx -g/\lambda\), a short gradient step, and any \(\lambda > -\lambda_{\min}(H)\) makes the system positive definite.

The cost problem and quasi-Newton methods

Storing \(H\) costs \(O(n^2)\) memory and solving with it \(O(n^3)\) time per step. For a model with \(n = 10^9\) parameters, \(n^2 = 10^{18}\) entries: impossible. Quasi-Newton methods build a cheap approximation from gradients alone. With \(s_k = x_{k+1} - x_k\) and \(y_k = \nabla f_{k+1} - \nabla f_k\), Taylor gives \(y_k \approx H s_k\), so the new approximation is required to satisfy the secant condition \(B_{k+1}s_k = y_k\) (in 1D this is the secant method).

Algorithm · BFGS (inverse-Hessian form)
  1. Step \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha_k M_k\nabla f_k\), with \(M_k \approx H^{-1}\) and a line search that ensures the curvature condition \(y_k^\top s_k > 0\).
  2. Update with \(\rho = 1/(y_k^\top s_k)\): \(M_{k+1} = (I - \rho s_ky_k^\top)\,M_k\,(I - \rho y_ks_k^\top) + \rho s_ks_k^\top\), a rank-two correction that satisfies \(M_{k+1}y_k = s_k\) and keeps \(M\) positive definite.

The simpler SR1 update is rank one, \(B_{k+1} = B_k + \frac{(y - Bs)(y - Bs)^\top}{(y - Bs)^\top s}\), but need not stay positive definite. L-BFGS never forms \(M\): it stores only the last \(m\) pairs \((s_i, y_i)\), often \(m \approx 5\)–\(20\), and applies the update implicitly with a two-loop recursion in \(O(mn)\) time and memory.

Gauss–Newton and the natural gradient

For nonlinear least squares \(f(x) = \tfrac12\|r(x)\|^2\) with residual Jacobian \(J\): \(\nabla f = J^\top r\) and \(\nabla^2 f = J^\top J + \sum_i r_i\nabla^2r_i\). Gauss–Newton drops the second term (small when residuals are small or nearly linear) and solves \((J^\top J)p = -J^\top r\): Newton without second derivatives, always positive semidefinite. Levenberg–Marquardt is \((J^\top J + \lambda I)p = -J^\top r\).

Worked example · a Gauss–Newton step

Fit \(y = e^{at}\) to the points \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 4)\). Residuals \(r_i = e^{at_i} - y_i\), \(J_i = t_ie^{at_i}\). From \(a_0 = 0.5\): \(r = (-0.351, -1.282)\), \(J = (1.649, 5.437)\), \(J^\top J = 32.27\), \(J^\top r = -7.547\), so \(p = 7.547/32.27 = 0.234\) and \(a_1 = 0.734\). Next steps give \(0.6947\), then \(0.69315\), approaching the exact fit \(a = \ln 2 = 0.693147\). Zero-residual problems like this one converge about as fast as full Newton.

For probabilistic models, the analogue of \(J^\top J\) is the Fisher information matrix \(F = \E[\nabla\log p\,\nabla\log p^\top]\), and the natural gradient step \(-F^{-1}\nabla L\) (Amari 1998) is steepest descent measured by KL divergence rather than Euclidean distance; L111 meets \(F\) as the curvature of the log-likelihood.

Common errors
  • Inverting \(H\). Solve \(Hp = -g\); by hand, by elimination.
  • Order of factors: the step is \(H^{-1}g\), a matrix times a vector; \(g^\top H^{-1}\) is its transpose and \(g/H\) is meaningless.
  • Pure Newton with an indefinite Hessian, which happily converges to saddles and maxima.
  • Expecting quadratic convergence from anywhere. It is a local property; far away Newton can diverge.
  • Gauss–Newton on large-residual problems, where the dropped term matters and steps can be poor.

Lagrangian Duality

36m video50m practiceheavy lesson

Price every constraint, minimize the priced objective freely, and you get a lower bound on the true optimum for any non-negative prices. The best such bound is the dual problem: always concave, often easier, equal to the primal for convex problems under Slater's condition, and the reason the SVM depends on data only through inner products.

The Lagrangian and the dual function

Primal problem (standard form): minimize \(f_0(x)\) subject to \(f_i(x) \le 0\) (\(i = 1..m\)) and \(h_j(x) = 0\) (\(j = 1..p\)), with optimal value \(p^*\). Following L83, the Lagrangian is

$$\mathcal L(x, \lambda, \mu) = f_0(x) + \sum_i\mu_if_i(x) + \sum_j\lambda_jh_j(x), \qquad \mu \ge 0 .$$

Read \(\mu_i\) as a price per unit of violation: a point with \(f_i(x) > 0\) pays \(\mu_if_i(x)\), a strictly feasible one earns a rebate. The dual function is the best priced cost, \(g(\lambda, \mu) = \inf_x\mathcal L(x, \lambda, \mu)\), possibly \(-\infty\).

Theorem · weak duality and concavity

(a) \(g\) is concave, whether or not the primal is convex. (b) For every \(\mu \ge 0\) and \(\lambda\), \(g(\lambda, \mu) \le p^*\). Hence the dual problem \(d^* = \max_{\mu \ge 0,\,\lambda}g(\lambda, \mu)\) satisfies \(d^* \le p^*\); the difference \(p^* - d^*\) is the duality gap.

The proofs are the Prove it below; each takes two lines. A gap can be positive for non-convex problems: minimize \(x\) subject to \(x^2 \ge 1\), \(x \ge 0\) has \(p^* = 1\), but \(\mathcal L = x + \mu(1 - x^2) - \nu x\) is unbounded below in \(x\) unless \(\mu = 0\) and \(\nu = 1\), so \(d^* = 0\).

Strong duality and Slater's condition

Theorem · Slater

If the problem is convex (\(f_0\) and all \(f_i\) convex, all \(h_j\) affine) and some feasible \(x\) has \(f_i(x) < 0\) for every non-affine \(f_i\), then strong duality holds, \(d^* = p^*\), and the dual optimum is attained when \(p^*\) is finite.

Strong duality gives optimality certificates (a dual point with \(g = f_0(x)\) proves \(x\) optimal), an alternative problem that may be simpler, and the KKT conditions:

Derivation · complementary slackness and KKT from a zero gap

Let \(x^*\) be primal optimal and \((\lambda^*, \mu^*)\) dual optimal with \(f_0(x^*) = g(\lambda^*, \mu^*)\). Then

$$f_0(x^*) = \inf_x\mathcal L(x, \lambda^*, \mu^*) \le \mathcal L(x^*, \lambda^*, \mu^*) = f_0(x^*) + \sum_i\mu_i^*f_i(x^*) + 0 \le f_0(x^*).$$

Both inequalities are equalities. The second gives \(\sum_i\mu_i^*f_i(x^*) = 0\); every term is \(\le 0\), so each \(\mu_i^*f_i(x^*) = 0\): only active constraints carry nonzero prices. The first says \(x^*\) minimizes \(\mathcal L(\cdot, \lambda^*, \mu^*)\), so (for differentiable functions) \(\nabla_x\mathcal L = 0\): stationarity. With feasibility and \(\mu^* \ge 0\), these are exactly the KKT conditions of L83.

Duals of linear and quadratic programs

Worked example

Dual of \(\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \ge b\), \(x \ge 0\). Write the constraints as \(b - Ax \le 0\) and \(-x \le 0\), with prices \(\mu \ge 0\), \(\nu \ge 0\): \(\mathcal L = c^\top x + \mu^\top(b - Ax) - \nu^\top x = (c - A^\top\mu - \nu)^\top x + b^\top\mu\). A linear function of \(x\) is bounded below only if its coefficient vanishes, so \(g = b^\top\mu\) if \(A^\top\mu + \nu = c\), else \(-\infty\). Eliminating \(\nu \ge 0\):

$$\text{dual:}\quad \max_\mu\ b^\top\mu \quad\text{s.t.}\quad A^\top\mu \le c,\ \mu \ge 0 .$$

Numbers: minimize \(3x_1 + 2x_2\) s.t. \(x_1 + x_2 \ge 2\), \(x_1 + 3x_2 \ge 3\), \(x \ge 0\). Primal vertices: \((0, 2)\) costs 4, \((\tfrac32, \tfrac12)\) costs 5.5, \((3, 0)\) costs 9, so \(p^* = 4\). Dual: maximize \(2\mu_1 + 3\mu_2\) s.t. \(\mu_1 + \mu_2 \le 3\), \(\mu_1 + 3\mu_2 \le 2\), \(\mu \ge 0\); its best vertex is \(\mu = (2, 0)\) with value 4. The gap is zero, and complementary slackness checks out: the second primal constraint is slack (\(6 > 3\)) and \(\mu_2 = 0\); \(x_2 > 0\) and the second dual constraint is tight.

QP dual. For \(\min\tfrac12x^\top Qx + c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \le b\) with \(Q \succ 0\): \(\nabla_x\mathcal L = Qx + c + A^\top\mu = 0\) gives \(x = -Q^{-1}(c + A^\top\mu)\), and substituting, \(g(\mu) = -\tfrac12(c + A^\top\mu)^\top Q^{-1}(c + A^\top\mu) - b^\top\mu\). The dual is to maximize this concave quadratic over \(\mu \ge 0\): only sign constraints, which is why many QP solvers work on the dual.

Sensitivity. Perturb to \(f_i \le u_i\), \(h_j = v_j\). Under strong duality, any \(x\) feasible for the perturbed problem has \(p^* = g(\lambda^*, \mu^*) \le f_0(x) + \sum\mu_i^*f_i(x) + \sum\lambda_j^*h_j(x) \le f_0(x) + \mu^{*\top}u + \lambda^{*\top}v\). So \(p^*(u, v) \ge p^* - \mu^{*\top}u - \lambda^{*\top}v\) and, where differentiable, \(\partial p^*/\partial u_i = -\mu_i^*\): multipliers are shadow prices. In the LP, raising \(x_1 + x_2 \ge 2\) to \(\ge 2.1\) should cost \(\mu_1 \times 0.1 = 0.2\); the new optimum \((0, 2.1)\) costs 4.2.

The SVM dual

Hard-margin SVM: labels \(y_i \in \{\pm1\}\), separate the data with the widest margin. The margin is \(2/\|w\|\), so the primal is \(\min\tfrac12\|w\|^2\) s.t. \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1\).

Derivation · from margin maximization to inner products

\(\mathcal L = \tfrac12\|w\|^2 - \sum_i\alpha_i\big[y_i(w^\top x_i + b) - 1\big]\), \(\alpha \ge 0\). Minimize over the free variables: \(\nabla_w\mathcal L = 0\) gives \(w = \sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i\); \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial b = -\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\) must vanish, or the infimum over \(b\) is \(-\infty\). Substituting \(w\): \(\tfrac12\|w\|^2 - w^\top w - b\cdot 0 + \sum\alpha_i\), so

$$\max_{\alpha}\ \sum_i\alpha_i - \tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jy_iy_j\,x_i^\top x_j \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \alpha_i \ge 0,\ \ \sum_i\alpha_iy_i = 0 .$$

Complementary slackness, \(\alpha_i\big[y_i(w^\top x_i + b) - 1\big] = 0\), says \(\alpha_i > 0\) only for points exactly on the margin: the support vectors. The solution \(w = \sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\) is built from them alone, and \(b = y_s - w^\top x_s\) from any support vector \(x_s\).

The data enter the dual only through the inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\), and the classifier \(\operatorname{sign}\big(\sum_i\alpha_iy_i\,x_i^\top x + b\big)\) only through \(x_i^\top x\). Replace every inner product by a kernel \(k(x_i, x_j) = \phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)\) and the same algorithm works in a feature space you never construct. This kernel substitution is possible only because of the dual.

Worked example · support vectors by complementary slackness

1D data: \(x = 1\) and \(x = 3\) labelled \(+1\), \(x = -1\) labelled \(-1\). The widest gap is between \(-1\) and \(1\), so guess \(w = 1\), \(b = 0\): constraints \(1 \ge 1\) (tight), \(3 \ge 1\) (slack), \(1 \ge 1\) (tight). CS forces \(\alpha_3 = 0\) for \(x = 3\). Then \(\sum\alpha_iy_i = 0\) gives \(\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \alpha\), and \(w = \alpha\cdot1 + \alpha\cdot(-1)(-1) = 2\alpha = 1\), so \(\alpha = \tfrac12\). Dual value \(\sum\alpha - \tfrac12w^2 = 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12\) equals the primal \(\tfrac12w^2\): zero gap, so the guess is optimal.

Common errors
  • Forgetting that the infimum can be \(-\infty\). Those parameter values are excluded, and that exclusion is where dual constraints such as \(A^\top\mu \le c\) and \(\sum\alpha_iy_i = 0\) come from.
  • Sign slips for \(\ge\) constraints. Rewrite as \(\le 0\) first, then attach \(\mu \ge 0\).
  • Expecting strong duality without convexity (see the gap example), or without a Slater point for nonlinear constraints.
  • Assuming every margin point has \(\alpha_i > 0\). CS gives "\(\alpha_i > 0\) ⇒ on the margin", not the converse.

Linear & Quadratic Programming by Hand

30m video40m practiceheavy lesson

A linear program optimizes a linear function over a polyhedron, so if an optimum exists one sits at a vertex: that single fact drives both the graphical method and the simplex method. An equality-constrained quadratic program is even simpler: its KKT conditions are one linear system.

Forms and conversions

Definition

Standard form: \(\min c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax = b\), \(x \ge 0\). Inequality (canonical) form: \(\max c^\top x\) s.t. \(Ax \le b\), \(x \ge 0\) (books vary on which they call canonical). The feasible set of any LP is a polyhedron, an intersection of finitely many halfspaces, and therefore convex (L90).

Conversions: \(a^\top x \le b\) becomes \(a^\top x + s = b\) with a slack \(s \ge 0\); \(a^\top x \ge b\) becomes \(a^\top x - s = b\) (surplus); a free variable is split \(x = x^+ - x^-\) with \(x^\pm \ge 0\); \(\max c^\top x = -\min(-c^\top x)\); multiplying a constraint by \(-1\) flips \(\le\) and \(\ge\); an equality is two inequalities.

Optima live at vertices

Definition · vertex

An extreme point (vertex) of a convex set \(P\) is a point that is not \(\theta y + (1-\theta)z\) for any \(y \ne z\) in \(P\) and \(\theta \in (0,1)\). In \(\R^n\), a vertex of \(\{Ax \le b\}\) is a feasible point where \(n\) linearly independent constraints are active.

Theorem · fundamental theorem of linear programming

If an LP is feasible and its objective is bounded on the feasible set, and the feasible polyhedron has at least one vertex (true in standard form), then the optimum is attained at a vertex.

Convexity argument for a bounded polyhedron. A polytope is the convex hull of its finitely many vertices \(v_1, \dots, v_N\). Any feasible \(x = \sum\theta_iv_i\) has \(c^\top x = \sum\theta_ic^\top v_i \ge \min_ic^\top v_i\): some vertex is at least as good as every point. (The Prove it handles unbounded polyhedra.) An LP can also be infeasible (empty polyhedron), unbounded (the objective improves forever along a feasible ray), or degenerate (more than \(n\) constraints active at a vertex, which can make simplex pivots stall without moving).

Worked example · a two-variable LP, graphically

Maximize \(3x + 5y\) s.t. \(x \le 4\), \(2y \le 12\), \(3x + 2y \le 18\), \(x, y \ge 0\) (the Wyndor Glass example of Hillier & Lieberman). Vertices and values: \((0,0)\): 0; \((4,0)\): 12; \((4,3)\): 27; \((2,6)\): 36; \((0,6)\): 30. The optimum is 36 at \((2, 6)\), where \(2y \le 12\) and \(3x + 2y \le 18\) are active. Graphically: slide the line \(3x + 5y = c\) in the direction of \((3,5)\) until it last touches the region.

The simplex method and one tableau pivot

Simplex walks from vertex to adjacent vertex along edges that improve the objective, and stops when no edge improves; by convexity, a vertex with no improving edge is optimal. Algebraically, a vertex is a basic feasible solution and a move is a pivot. Add slacks: \(x + s_1 = 4\), \(2y + s_2 = 12\), \(3x + 2y + s_3 = 18\), objective \(z = 3x + 5y\). Start at \(x = y = 0\), \(s = (4, 12, 18)\).

The ratio test (RHS over positive entries in the \(y\) column) picks \(s_2\) to leave, because increasing \(y\) beyond 6 would make \(s_2\) negative. Divide the \(s_2\) row by 2, giving \(y + \tfrac12s_2 = 6\), and eliminate \(y\) from the other rows: \(s_3\) row becomes \(3x - s_2 + s_3 = 6\), \(z\)-row becomes \(z - 3x + \tfrac52s_2 = 30\). Interior-point methods (Karmarkar 1984, and the barrier methods that followed) instead move through the interior along a smooth central path, solving a sequence of barrier problems with Newton steps (L92). They run in polynomial time; simplex is very fast in practice but has exponential worst cases.

Quadratic programs and the KKT block system

A QP is \(\min\tfrac12x^\top Qx + c^\top x\) subject to linear constraints. With equality constraints \(Ax = b\) only, the Lagrangian \(\tfrac12x^\top Qx + c^\top x + \lambda^\top(Ax - b)\) gives stationarity \(Qx + c + A^\top\lambda = 0\) plus feasibility \(Ax = b\):

$$\begin{pmatrix} Q & A^\top \\ A & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \lambda\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -c \\ b\end{pmatrix}.$$
Result · when the KKT system has a unique solution

If \(A\) has full row rank and \(Q\) is positive definite (on the null space of \(A\) suffices), the KKT matrix is nonsingular and \(x\) is the unique global minimizer. Proof: if \(Qu + A^\top v = 0\) and \(Au = 0\), then \(u^\top Qu = -u^\top A^\top v = -(Au)^\top v = 0\), so \(u = 0\); then \(A^\top v = 0\) forces \(v = 0\). The matrix itself is indefinite, a saddle-point system.

Worked example · equality-constrained QP via the KKT system

Minimize \(x_1^2 + x_1x_2 + x_2^2 - x_1\) s.t. \(x_1 + x_2 = 1\). So \(Q = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\), \(c = (-1, 0)\), \(A = (1\ \ 1)\), \(b = 1\). The system: \(2x_1 + x_2 + \lambda = 1\), \(x_1 + 2x_2 + \lambda = 0\), \(x_1 + x_2 = 1\). Subtracting the first two, \(x_1 - x_2 = 1\); with the constraint, \(x = (1, 0)\) and \(\lambda = -1\). Check by substitution: \(x_2 = 1 - x_1\) turns the objective into \((x_1 - 1)^2\), minimized at \(x_1 = 1\), value 0. ✓

Recognizing LPs and QPs. Least squares is an unconstrained QP (\(Q = 2A^\top A\), \(c = -2A^\top b\)); ridge adds \(2\lambda I\) to \(Q\). The lasso is a QP after splitting \(x = x^+ - x^-\): minimize \(\|A(x^+ - x^-) - b\|^2 + \lambda\mathbf 1^\top(x^+ + x^-)\) with \(x^\pm \ge 0\). The SVM is a QP in \((w, b)\) with one linear inequality per data point, and \(\ell_1\) (least absolute deviations) regression is an LP.

Common errors
  • Slacks on the wrong side (\(+s\) for \(\ge\)), or forgetting \(s \ge 0\).
  • Ratio test errors: using non-positive column entries, or choosing the largest ratio, which makes a variable negative.
  • Not splitting free variables before using a method that assumes \(x \ge 0\).
  • Missing a better vertex by reading the picture loosely; always tabulate all vertices in 2D.
  • Confusing unbounded and infeasible: one has no finite optimum, the other has no feasible point.

Phase 6 Optimization Workshop

34m video60m practice

One problem set that uses every tool of the phase: least squares derived three ways, ridge regression dissected with the SVD, convexity proofs, Hessian classification, constrained problems with KKT, a convergence-rate bound, and the SVM's optimality conditions. It ends with a checklist for attacking any optimization problem on paper.

1 · Least squares three ways

Minimize \(f(x) = \|Ax - b\|^2\), \(A \in \R^{m\times n}\), and write \(r = A\hat x - b\).

Worked example · geometry, one-variable calculus, matrix calculus

Geometry (L53): \(A\hat x\) is the projection of \(b\) onto the column space, so the residual is orthogonal to every column: \(A^\top r = 0\).

One-variable calculus. Restrict \(f\) to any line through \(\hat x\): \(\varphi(s) = \|r + sAv\|^2 = \|r\|^2 + 2s\,v^\top A^\top r + s^2\|Av\|^2\), an ordinary parabola in \(s\). If \(\hat x\) is optimal, \(\varphi'(0) = 2v^\top A^\top r = 0\) for every \(v\), so \(A^\top r = 0\). Conversely, if \(A^\top r = 0\) then \(\varphi(s) = \|r\|^2 + s^2\|Av\|^2 \ge \varphi(0)\) on every line, so \(\hat x\) is a global minimum. (For the one-parameter model \(y \approx \beta t\) this is just \(\frac{d}{d\beta}\sum(y_i - \beta t_i)^2 = 0\), giving \(\hat\beta = \sum t_iy_i/\sum t_i^2\).)

Matrix calculus (L89): \(\nabla f = 2A^\top(Ax - b) = 0\). All three give the normal equations \(A^\top A\hat x = A^\top b\).

2 · Ridge regression through the SVD

Write the thin SVD \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\) with orthonormal columns \(u_i\) (in \(\R^m\)), orthonormal \(v_i\) (a basis of \(\R^n\)) and \(\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_n \ge 0\) (L57). The ridge solution \(x_\lambda = (A^\top A + \lambda I)^{-1}A^\top b\) (Prove it) is

$$x_\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\sigma_i\,(u_i^\top b)}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}\,v_i = \sum_{i:\,\sigma_i > 0} \underbrace{\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\sigma_i^2 + \lambda}}_{\text{filter factor}}\cdot\frac{u_i^\top b}{\sigma_i}\,v_i .$$

The OLS coefficient along \(v_i\) is \(u_i^\top b/\sigma_i\); ridge multiplies it by \(\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)\). Directions with \(\sigma_i \gg \sqrt\lambda\) pass almost untouched; those with \(\sigma_i \ll \sqrt\lambda\) are nearly removed.

  • \(\lambda \to 0\): every factor with \(\sigma_i > 0\) tends to 1 and those with \(\sigma_i = 0\) are 0 for every \(\lambda\), so \(x_\lambda \to A^+b\), the minimum-norm least-squares solution of L58; it is the OLS solution when \(A\) has full column rank.
  • \(\lambda \to \infty\): \(x_\lambda \approx A^\top b/\lambda \to 0\): all coefficients shrink to zero.
  • Stability. Noise \(e\) in \(b\) enters OLS as \((u_i^\top e)/\sigma_i\) along \(v_i\), amplified without bound as \(\sigma_i \to 0\). Under ridge the gain is \(\sigma/(\sigma^2 + \lambda) \le 1/(2\sqrt\lambda)\), since \(\sigma^2 + \lambda \ge 2\sigma\sqrt\lambda\). The system matrix's condition number drops from \(\sigma_1^2/\sigma_n^2\) to \((\sigma_1^2 + \lambda)/(\sigma_n^2 + \lambda)\).

Numbers. \(\sigma = (3, 0.1)\), \(\lambda = 0.01\): filter factors \(9/9.01 \approx 0.999\) and \(0.01/0.02 = 0.5\). Noise of size 0.05 along \(u_2\) moves the OLS coefficient by \(0.05/0.1 = 0.5\) but the ridge coefficient by only \(0.05\cdot0.1/0.02 = 0.25\), at the cost of halving that coefficient's signal.

\(\sum_i\log(1 + e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}) + \tfrac\lambda2\|w\|^2\)yes, and \(\lambda\)-strongly convex: Hessian \(\succeq \lambda I\)
\(-\log\det X\) on positive definite \(X\)yes: on a line, \(-\log\det X - \sum_i\log(1 + t\lambda_i)\) with \(\lambda_i\) the eigenvalues of \(X^{-1/2}VX^{-1/2}\), a sum of convex functions of \(t\)
\((1 - w_1w_2)^2\)no: \((1,1)\), \((-1,-1)\) have value 0, their midpoint 1
Worked example

Classify the critical points of \(x^4 - 2x^2 + y^2\). \(\nabla f = (4x^3 - 4x, 2y) = 0\) gives \(x \in \{0, \pm1\}\), \(y = 0\). \(H = \operatorname{diag}(12x^2 - 4, 2)\). At \((0, 0)\): \(\operatorname{diag}(-4, 2)\), indefinite, a saddle. At \((\pm1, 0)\): \(\operatorname{diag}(8, 2)\), positive definite, minima with \(f = -1\), global since \(f\) is coercive. A double well: the simplest non-convex landscape.

4 · Three constrained problems, a rate bound, and the SVM's KKT conditions

Worked examples · equality, inequality, both

Equality. \(\min x^2 + y^2\) s.t. \(x + 2y = 5\): \(2x = \lambda\), \(2y = 2\lambda\) give \((1, 2)\), value 5, \(\lambda = 2\) (check: \(f^*(c) = c^2/5\) has slope \(2\) at \(c = 5\)).

Inequality. \(\min(x - 1)^2 + (y - 2)^2\) s.t. \(x + y \le 2\): the free minimizer \((1,2)\) is infeasible, so the constraint is active; stationarity \(2(x - 1) + \mu = 2(y - 2) + \mu = 0\) gives \(y = x + 1\), so \((\tfrac12, \tfrac32)\), \(\mu = 1 \ge 0\), value \(\tfrac12\).

Both. \(\min x^2 + y^2 + z^2\) s.t. \(x + y + z = 3\), \(x \ge 2\): the equality alone gives \((1,1,1)\), infeasible; with \(x = 2\) active, \(y = z = \tfrac12\), \(\lambda = -1\), \(\mu = 2x + \lambda = 3 \ge 0\), value \(\tfrac92\).

Rate bound. \(f = \tfrac12x^\top Ax\), \(A = \begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\): eigenvalues 2 and 4, so \(\mu = 2\), \(L = 4\), \(\kappa = 2\). With \(\alpha = 1/L\), \(f(x_k) \le (1 - \tfrac12)^kf(x_0)\) (L91): \(k = 20\) steps guarantee a \(10^{-6}\) reduction. The best fixed step \(\alpha = 2/(L + \mu) = \tfrac13\) contracts the error in \(x\) by \(\tfrac13\) per step.

KKT · soft-margin SVM

Primal: \(\min\tfrac12\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i\xi_i\) s.t. \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i\), \(\xi_i \ge 0\). With multipliers \(\alpha_i, \beta_i \ge 0\): stationarity \(w = \sum\alpha_iy_ix_i\), \(\sum\alpha_iy_i = 0\), \(\alpha_i + \beta_i = C\); complementary slackness \(\alpha_i[y_i(w^\top x_i + b) - 1 + \xi_i] = 0\), \(\beta_i\xi_i = 0\). Reading: \(\alpha_i = 0\) means the point is on the correct side with margin \(\ge 1\); \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) forces \(\xi_i = 0\) and margin exactly 1; \(\alpha_i = C\) means margin \(\le 1\) (inside the margin or misclassified). The dual is the hard-margin dual of L93 with the box \(0 \le \alpha_i \le C\).

5 · A checklist for any optimization problem on paper

Algorithm · before you compute anything
  1. Variables: what are they, what shape, are they free or bounded?
  2. Objective: write it explicitly; compute gradient and Hessian, checking shapes (L88).
  3. Convexity: prove it with the rules (or find the non-convexity); note strong convexity and smoothness constants \(\mu\), \(L\).
  4. Constraints: equalities, inequalities, which can be active; is there a Slater point?
  5. Method: closed form (normal equations, KKT system), first-order (gradient descent, rate set by \(\kappa\)) or second-order (Newton, cost \(O(n^3)\)).
  6. Guarantees: existence (compactness or coercivity), uniqueness (strict convexity), global vs local, convergence rate. Write down what you actually know.
Common errors
  • "Ridge with \(\lambda \to 0\) is OLS" for rank-deficient \(A\): the limit is the minimum-norm solution \(A^+b\); OLS is not unique.
  • Using the filter factor as the coefficient. It multiplies the OLS coefficient \(u_i^\top b/\sigma_i\).
  • Claiming KKT ⇒ global optimum without checking convexity.
  • Mixing rates in \(x\) and in \(f\). For quadratics the \(f\)-error factor is the square of the \(x\)-error factor.

Practice · 60 min

  1. Prove it. Using the SVD, show that ridge regression's solution has coefficients \(u_i^\top b\,\sigma_i/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)\) in the basis \(v_i\), and interpret. Hint: \(A^\top A + \lambda I = V(\Sigma^2 + \lambda I)V^\top\).
    Proof sketch

    With \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\) (\(U^\top U = I\), \(V\) orthogonal): \(A^\top A = V\Sigma^2V^\top\), so \(A^\top A + \lambda I = V(\Sigma^2 + \lambda I)V^\top\) and its inverse is \(V(\Sigma^2 + \lambda I)^{-1}V^\top\) (diagonal entries \(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda > 0\)). Also \(A^\top b = V\Sigma U^\top b\). Hence \(x_\lambda = V(\Sigma^2 + \lambda I)^{-1}\Sigma U^\top b\), and the \(i\)-th coordinate in the \(v\)-basis is \(\sigma_i(u_i^\top b)/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)\). Interpretation: the OLS coefficient \(u_i^\top b/\sigma_i\) times the filter \(\sigma_i^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \lambda)\); weak directions (small \(\sigma_i\)), where data say least and noise is amplified most, are shrunk most.

  2. Ridge path for \(A = \operatorname{diag}(2, 0.5)\), \(b = (2, 1)\): OLS, then \(x_\lambda\) at \(\lambda = 0.25\).
    Answer

    OLS \((1, 2)\). \(x_\lambda = \big(\frac{4}{4 + \lambda}, \frac{0.5}{0.25 + \lambda}\big)\); at \(\lambda = 0.25\): \((0.941, 1.0)\). The weak direction is halved, the strong one barely moves.

  3. KKT for \(\min\|x\|^2\) s.t. \(a^\top x \ge 1\).
    Answer

    Active: \(2x = \mu a\), \(a^\top x = 1\), so \(x = a/\|a\|^2\), \(\mu = 2/\|a\|^2 \ge 0\), value \(1/\|a\|^2\).

  4. For \(f = \tfrac12x^\top\operatorname{diag}(1, 10)x\): how many steps with \(\alpha = 1/L\) guarantee a \(10^{-6}\) reduction in \(f\)?
    Answer

    Factor \(1 - \mu/L = 0.9\): \(k \ge \ln10^6/\ln(1/0.9) \approx 131.1\), so 132 steps.

  5. Apply the checklist to \(\ell_2\)-regularized logistic regression \(\frac1n\sum_i\log(1 + e^{-y_iw^\top x_i}) + \frac\lambda2\|w\|^2\).
    Answer

    Unconstrained, convex, \(\lambda\)-strongly convex, smooth with \(L \le \sigma_{\max}(X)^2/(4n) + \lambda\) (the logistic curvature is at most \(\tfrac14\)). Unique global minimizer, no closed form; gradient descent with \(\alpha = 1/L\) converges linearly with factor \(1 - \lambda/L\), Newton converges quadratically near the optimum.
