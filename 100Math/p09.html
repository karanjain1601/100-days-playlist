Information Theory, Numerics & Capstone

Phase 9 adds the last two toolkits, information theory and numerical mathematics, previews the analysis and measure theory underneath everything, and closes the course with a full dependency map and a 40-problem comprehensive exam. Entropy and KL divergence reinterpret the likelihood of Phase 8, floating-point analysis explains why the linear algebra of Phase 3 is computed the way it is, and the capstone ties all ten phases into six threads.

By the end of this phase you can

  • Derive self-information from its axioms, compute entropies, cross-entropies and KL divergences by hand, and prove that KL is non-negative.
  • Show that minimising cross-entropy, minimising KL to the data and maximising likelihood are the same optimisation.
  • Compute mutual information three ways, apply the data processing inequality, build a Huffman code, and derive the Gaussian as the maximum-entropy law at fixed variance.
  • Explain machine epsilon, cancellation and conditioning versus stability, and choose the finite-difference step that balances truncation against round-off.
  • Run bisection, Newton and fixed-point iterations and the trapezoid and Simpson rules by hand, with their error orders.
  • Distinguish pointwise from uniform convergence, say what compactness and dominated convergence guarantee, and restate probability measure-theoretically.
  • Sit and self-mark a 3-hour, 40-problem exam spanning all ten phases, and choose a next path from the results.

L118 · DAY 98

Information Theory I: Entropy, Cross-Entropy & KL Divergence

36m video45m practiceheavy lesson

Information theory measures surprise. Three natural axioms force self-information to be \(-\log p\); entropy is its average; cross-entropy and KL divergence measure the cost of using the wrong distribution. Minimising cross-entropy, minimising KL and maximising likelihood are one and the same optimisation.

Self-information from three axioms

Ask for a measure \(I(p)\) of the surprise of an event of probability \(p \in (0, 1]\) with (i) \(I(1) = 0\): a certain event carries no information; (ii) \(I\) is decreasing: rarer means more surprising; (iii) \(I(pq) = I(p) + I(q)\): the surprises of independent events add.

Theorem · Uniqueness of the logarithm

Any \(I\) satisfying (i)–(iii) is \(I(p) = -c\log p\) for some constant \(c > 0\).

Proof. Let \(g(x) = I(e^{-x})\) for \(x \ge 0\). Axiom (iii) becomes Cauchy's equation \(g(x + y) = g(x) + g(y)\), and (ii) makes \(g\) increasing. Induction gives \(g(nx) = ng(x)\), hence \(g(m/n) = \tfrac mn g(1)\) for rationals. For irrational \(x\), squeeze it between rationals \(r < x < s\): monotonicity gives \(rg(1) \le g(x) \le sg(1)\), so \(g(x) = cx\) with \(c = g(1) > 0\). Then \(I(p) = g(-\ln p) = -c\ln p\).

The constant is the choice of unit: \(\log_2\) gives bits, \(\ln\) gives nats, \(\log_{10}\) gives bans (hartleys). One nat is \(1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443\) bits. A fair coin flip is 1 bit; an event of probability \(1/1024\) is 10 bits.

Shannon entropy

Definition · Entropy

\(H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log p(x) = \E[-\log p(X)]\), the expected surprise, with the convention \(0\log 0 = 0\).

Theorem · Basic properties

(a) \(H(X) \ge 0\), since every \(-\log p(x) \ge 0\). (b) \(H(X) = 0\) iff \(X\) is deterministic: each term \(-p\log p\) vanishes only at \(p \in \{0, 1\}\). (c) On a support of \(n\) points, \(H(X) \le \log n\), with equality iff \(X\) is uniform.

Derivation · (c) by Lagrange multipliers

Maximise \(-\sum_i p_i\ln p_i\) subject to \(\sum_i p_i = 1\) (L82). The Lagrangian's partial derivatives are \(-\ln p_i - 1 - \lambda = 0\), so every \(p_i = e^{-1-\lambda}\) is the same constant, \(p_i = 1/n\), and \(H = \log n\). The objective is strictly concave (\(-x\ln x\) has second derivative \(-1/x\)), so this stationary point is the unique global maximum. The Prove it gives a second proof with KL divergence.

Worked example · Entropies of small distributions (bits)
  • Uniform on 4 outcomes: \(\log_2 4 = 2\).
  • \((\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\): \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 1.75\). Asking “is it the first? the second? the third?” uses exactly 1.75 questions on average.
  • \((0.9, 0.1)\): \(-0.9\log_2 0.9 - 0.1\log_2 0.1 \approx 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469\).
  • \((1, 0, 0, 0)\): 0.

Joint entropy, conditional entropy and the chain rule

\(H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(x,y)\), and \(H(Y \mid X) = \sum_x p(x)\,H(Y \mid X = x) = -\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log p(y \mid x)\), the average remaining uncertainty about \(Y\) once \(X\) is known.

Theorem · Chain rule

\(H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)\), and in general \(H(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_i H(X_i \mid X_1, \dots, X_{i-1})\).

Proof. \(\log p(x,y) = \log p(x) + \log p(y \mid x)\); take \(-\E\) of both sides.

Cross-entropy and KL divergence

Definition · Cross-entropy and KL divergence

\(H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x)\log q(x)\) and \(D_{\KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\). Adding and subtracting \(\sum p\log p\) gives

$$H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\KL}(p \,\|\, q).$$

Read \(H(p, q)\) as the average code length when data from \(p\) are encoded with a code built for \(q\), and \(D_{\KL}\) as the extra cost of the wrong code. Today's Prove it shows \(D_{\KL} \ge 0\) with equality iff \(p = q\). It is a divergence, not a metric: it is not symmetric and fails the triangle inequality, and \(D_{\KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \infty\) as soon as \(q(x) = 0\) at some \(x\) with \(p(x) > 0\).

Worked example · KL both ways

\(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac34)\), in bits:

$$D(p \,\|\, q) = \tfrac12\log_2 2 + \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac23 \approx 0.5 - 0.292 = 0.208, \qquad D(q \,\|\, p) = \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac12 + \tfrac34\log_2\tfrac32 \approx -0.25 + 0.439 = 0.189.$$

The two directions differ.

Forward versus reverse KL. When fitting a model \(q\) to a target \(p\), \(D(p \,\|\, q) = \E_p[\log p/q]\) explodes wherever \(q \approx 0\) but \(p > 0\), so minimising it makes \(q\) spread out to cover all of \(p\)'s mass (mass-covering; this is what maximum likelihood does). \(D(q \,\|\, p) = \E_q[\log q/p]\) explodes where \(q > 0\) but \(p \approx 0\), so \(q\) retreats into a single high-probability region (mode-seeking; this is what variational inference does). Fitting one Gaussian to a two-humped \(p\): forward KL gives a wide Gaussian straddling both humps, reverse KL a narrow one sitting on one hump.

Why cross-entropy is the classification loss

A classifier outputs a distribution \(q_\theta(\cdot \mid x)\) over classes. With one-hot targets, the average loss over the training set is

$$-\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) = H(\hat p, q_\theta) = H(\hat p) + D_{\KL}(\hat p \,\|\, q_\theta),$$

where \(\hat p\) is the empirical distribution of the labels. Since \(H(\hat p)\) does not depend on \(\theta\), minimising cross-entropy, minimising KL to the data, and maximising the likelihood \(\prod_i q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)\) (L112) are the same problem under three names.

Worked example · Cross-entropy on a one-hot label

True class 1 of 3, prediction \(q = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)\). Then \(H(y, q) = -(1 \cdot \ln 0.7 + 0 \cdot \ln 0.2 + 0 \cdot \ln 0.1) = -\ln 0.7 \approx 0.357\) nats: only the probability given to the correct class matters. If that probability were 0.01 the loss would be 4.61 nats, and it grows without bound as the probability goes to 0: confident mistakes are punished hardest.

Common errors
  • Mixing log bases within one computation; state bits or nats.
  • Treating KL as a distance: it is asymmetric, and the order matters (true distribution first in the forward direction).
  • Forgetting the support condition. A model that assigns probability 0 to something that happens has infinite cross-entropy; that is why classifiers never output exact zeros.
  • Writing \(H(q, p)\) for the loss. The data's distribution goes first: \(H(\hat p, q_\theta)\).

Practice · 45 min

  1. Prove it. \(D_{\KL}(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0\) with equality iff \(p = q\); deduce that the uniform distribution maximises entropy on a finite support. Hint: Jensen for the concave \(\log\) (L107), applied to \(q/p\) under \(p\).
    Proof sketch

    Over \(S = \{x : p(x) > 0\}\): \(-D = \sum_S p\log\frac qp \le \log\sum_S p\cdot\frac qp = \log\sum_S q \le \log 1 = 0\). Equality in Jensen forces \(q/p\) to be constant on \(S\), and equality in the last step forces \(\sum_S q = 1\); together \(q = p\). For the uniform \(u\) on \(n\) points, \(D(p \,\|\, u) = \sum p\log(np) = \log n - H(p) \ge 0\), with equality iff \(p = u\).

  2. Entropy of a fair six-sided die, in bits.
    Answer

    \(\log_2 6 \approx 2.585\).

  3. \(X\) a fair coin. Find \(H(X, Y)\) when \(Y = X\) and when \(Y\) is an independent fair coin.
    Answer

    1 bit (\(H(Y \mid X) = 0\)); 2 bits.

  4. Compute \(H(p, q)\) in bits for \(p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac34)\) directly, and check it equals \(H(p) + D(p \,\|\, q)\).
    Answer

    \(-\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac14 - \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac34 \approx 1 + 0.208 = 1.208\).

  5. Show that \(H(X) \le H(X, Y)\).
    Answer

    Chain rule \(H(X, Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)\), and conditional entropy of a discrete variable is \(\ge 0\).

L119 · DAY 98

Information Theory II: Mutual Information & Coding Bounds

30m video40m practiceheavy lesson

Mutual information is the uncertainty about \(X\) that learning \(Y\) removes. It is zero exactly under independence, so it detects every kind of dependence, not only linear ones. The same inequalities say that no code compresses below entropy, and that among all distributions with a given variance the Gaussian assumes the least.

Mutual information

Definition · Mutual information

\(I(X; Y) = \sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log\dfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = D_{\KL}\big(p_{XY} \,\|\, p_Xp_Y\big)\): how far the joint distribution is from the product of its marginals.

Theorem · Four expressions and three properties

\(I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)\) (today's Prove it). It is symmetric, non-negative (it is a KL divergence, L118), and zero iff \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, since KL vanishes only when \(p_{XY} = p_Xp_Y\). Hence \(H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)\): conditioning cannot increase entropy on average.

Worked example · \(I(X;Y)\) from a joint table, three ways

\(p(0,0) = p(1,1) = 0.4\), \(p(0,1) = p(1,0) = 0.1\). Both marginals are \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), so \(H(X) = H(Y) = 1\) bit.

  • Joint entropy: \(H(X,Y) = -2(0.4)\log_2 0.4 - 2(0.1)\log_2 0.1 \approx 1.0575 + 0.6644 = 1.722\), so \(I = 1 + 1 - 1.722 = 0.278\) bits.
  • Conditional entropy: given \(Y\), \(X\) agrees with it with probability 0.8, so \(H(X \mid Y) = h(0.2) \approx 0.722\) and \(I = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278\).
  • KL form: \(2(0.4)\log_2\frac{0.4}{0.25} + 2(0.1)\log_2\frac{0.1}{0.25} \approx 0.542 - 0.264 = 0.278\).

Stronger than zero correlation. For \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) and \(Y = X^2\), \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\) (L104), yet \(H(Y \mid X) = 0\), so \(I(X;Y) = H(Y) = h(\tfrac13) \approx 0.918\) bits. Chain rule: \(I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z \mid Y)\), where the conditional mutual information \(I(X;Z \mid Y) = H(X \mid Y) - H(X \mid Y, Z) \ge 0\) is zero iff \(X \perp Z \mid Y\).

Data processing, Gibbs, and other divergences

Theorem · Data processing inequality

If \(X \to Y \to Z\) is a Markov chain (\(Z\) depends on \(X\) only through \(Y\)), then \(I(X; Z) \le I(X; Y)\).

Proof. Expand \(I(X; Y, Z)\) both ways: \(I(X;Z) + I(X;Y \mid Z) = I(X;Y) + I(X;Z \mid Y)\). The Markov property gives \(I(X;Z \mid Y) = 0\), so \(I(X;Z) = I(X;Y) - I(X;Y \mid Z) \le I(X;Y)\).

For a feature pipeline label → raw input → features, no transformation can create information about the label that the raw input lacked; processing can only lose it, although it can make the remaining information easier for a model to use. Gibbs' inequality, \(-\sum p\log p \le -\sum p\log q\), is \(H(p) \le H(p, q)\), the same statement as \(D_{\KL} \ge 0\). The Jensen–Shannon divergence \(\operatorname{JS}(p, q) = \tfrac12 D(p \,\|\, m) + \tfrac12 D(q \,\|\, m)\) with \(m = \tfrac12(p + q)\) is symmetric, always finite, at most \(\log 2\) (1 bit), and its square root is a metric. All of these belong to the f-divergences \(D_f(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x q(x)f\big(p(x)/q(x)\big)\) with \(f\) convex and \(f(1) = 0\): KL (\(f = t\log t\)), reverse KL (\(-\log t\)), total variation (\(\tfrac12|t - 1|\)), \(\chi^2\) (\((t - 1)^2\)). Jensen makes every one of them non-negative.

Source coding: entropy is the compression limit

A prefix code gives each symbol a binary codeword, none a prefix of another, so a stream decodes without separators. Kraft's inequality: lengths \(\ell_i\) of a binary prefix code satisfy \(\sum_i 2^{-\ell_i} \le 1\) (a codeword of length \(\ell\) uses up a \(2^{-\ell}\) share of the binary tree), and any lengths satisfying it can be realised.

Theorem · \(L \ge H\)

Every prefix code has expected length \(L = \sum_i p_i\ell_i \ge H(X)\) bits, with equality iff \(p_i = 2^{-\ell_i}\).

Proof. \(H - L = \sum_i p_i\log_2\frac{2^{-\ell_i}}{p_i} \le \log_2\sum_i 2^{-\ell_i} \le 0\), by Jensen and then Kraft.

The lengths \(\lceil -\log_2 p_i\rceil\) satisfy Kraft and give \(L < H + 1\). Shannon's source coding theorem (stated): coding blocks of \(n\) symbols, the best achievable bits per symbol tend to \(H\), and no lossless scheme does better. On the channel side, the capacity is \(C = \max_{p(x)}I(X;Y)\), equal to \(1 - h(\varepsilon)\) for a binary channel that flips each bit with probability \(\varepsilon\); the noisy-channel coding theorem (stated) says every rate below \(C\) is achievable with error probability tending to 0, and no rate above it is.

Worked example · Huffman code against the entropy

\(p = (0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1)\) for A, B, C, D. Huffman repeatedly merges the two least likely nodes: \(0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3\); then \(0.3 + 0.3 = 0.6\); then \(0.6 + 0.4\). Reading the tree: A = 0, B = 10, C = 110, D = 111. Expected length \(0.4(1) + 0.3(2) + 0.2(3) + 0.1(3) = 1.9\) bits, while \(H = 1.846\) bits. So \(H \le L < H + 1\), and no prefix code for these probabilities beats 1.9.

Maximum-entropy distributions

Maximise the differential entropy \(h(f) = -\int f\ln f\) subject to constraints, using Lagrange multipliers pointwise. With only a support constraint on an interval you get the uniform (as in L118). On \([0, \infty)\) with a fixed mean \(\mu\): \(-\ln f - 1 + \lambda_0 + \lambda_1x = 0\) gives \(f \propto e^{\lambda_1x}\), the exponential \(\frac1\mu e^{-x/\mu}\).

Worked example · Fixed variance gives the Gaussian

On \(\R\) with \(\int f = 1\), mean 0 and \(\int x^2f = \sigma^2\): stationarity gives \(-\ln f - 1 + \lambda_0 + \lambda_2x^2 = 0\), so \(f \propto e^{\lambda_2x^2}\), integrable only for \(\lambda_2 < 0\); matching the variance gives \(\lambda_2 = -1/2\sigma^2\), the density \(\varphi\) of \(\mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)\). To confirm it is the maximum, take any \(g\) with the same variance and use Gibbs:

$$h(g) \le -\int g\ln\varphi = \int g(x)\Big[\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]dx = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12 = \tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2) = h(\varphi).$$

The middle integral depends on \(g\) only through its variance. So among all distributions with a given variance the Gaussian assumes the least extra structure, the information-theoretic reason it is the default noise model.

Common errors
  • “Uncorrelated, so zero mutual information.” The \(Y = X^2\) example has zero covariance and 0.918 bits.
  • Reading the DPI as “processing is useless”. It bounds information, not how easily a model can extract it.
  • Treating differential entropy like discrete entropy. It can be negative (\(\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2) < 0\) for small \(\sigma\)) and changes under rescaling.
  • Forgetting the constraint behind a max-entropy claim. The Gaussian is maximal only at fixed variance; at fixed mean on \([0, \infty)\) it is the exponential.

Practice · 40 min

  1. Prove it. \(I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)\), starting from the KL definition. Hint: split \(\log\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}\) as \(\log\frac{p(x \mid y)}{p(x)}\).
    Proof sketch

    \(I = \sum p(x,y)\log p(x \mid y) - \sum p(x,y)\log p(x) = -H(X \mid Y) + H(X)\), summing out \(y\) in the second term. Then substitute the chain rule \(H(X \mid Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y)\) to get \(H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)\), which is symmetric in \(X, Y\).

  2. For \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) and \(Y = X^2\), compute \(H(X)\), \(H(Y)\), \(H(X \mid Y)\) and \(I(X;Y)\).
    Answer

    \(\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\); \(h(\tfrac13) \approx 0.918\); given \(Y = 1\), \(X = \pm 1\) equally (1 bit, probability \(\tfrac23\)), so \(H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac23\); \(I = 1.585 - 0.667 = 0.918\).

  3. Capacity of a binary symmetric channel with flip probability 0.11.
    Answer

    \(1 - h(0.11) \approx 1 - 0.500 = 0.50\) bits per use.

  4. Build a Huffman code for \((\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)\) and compare \(L\) with \(H\).
    Answer

    Lengths 1, 2, 3, 3; \(L = H = 1.75\) bits, since the probabilities are powers of 2.

  5. Show \(\operatorname{JS}(p, q) \le \log 2\).
    Answer

    \(D(p \,\|\, m) = \sum p\log\frac{2p}{p + q} \le \sum p\log 2 = \log 2\), since \(p/(p + q) \le 1\); likewise for \(q\); average the two.

L120 · DAY 99

Numerical Mathematics (By Hand, No Code)

34m video45m practiceheavy lesson

Computers do arithmetic on a finite set of numbers, so almost every operation rounds. The disasters come not from rounding itself but from amplification: subtracting nearly equal numbers, solving ill-conditioned problems, or choosing a step size without weighing truncation error against round-off.

Floating-point numbers and machine epsilon

Definition · Floating point, machine epsilon

\(x = (-1)^s \times (1.f)_2 \times 2^{e - \text{bias}}\). IEEE 754 double precision: 1 sign bit, 11 exponent bits (bias 1023), 52 fraction bits (53 significant bits with the hidden 1); single precision: 1, 8, 23. Machine epsilon \(\varepsilon = 2^{-52} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\) is the gap from 1 to the next double. With round-to-nearest, \(\operatorname{fl}(x \circ y) = (x \circ y)(1 + \delta)\), \(|\delta| \le u = \varepsilon/2\).

The representable numbers are finite and unevenly spaced (the gap doubles at each power of 2), so floating point controls relative error \(|\hat x - x|/|x|\), not absolute error. The default rounding is to nearest, ties to even; the alternatives round toward 0, \(+\infty\) or \(-\infty\). Errors accumulate: a naive sum of \(n\) terms can be off by about \((n - 1)u\sum|x_i|\), which careful ordering or compensated (Kahan) summation reduces.

Worked example · Machine epsilon of a toy format

Numbers \(\pm(1.f_1f_2f_3)_2 \times 2^e\), \(e \in \{-2, \dots, 3\}\). In \([1, 2)\) they are \(1, 1.125, \dots, 1.875\), so \(\varepsilon = 2^{-3} = 0.125\) and \(u = 0.0625\). There are \(6 \times 8 = 48\) positive numbers, from 0.25 to \((1.111)_2 \times 2^3 = 15\), spaced \(\tfrac1{32}\) near 0.25 and 1 near 15. And \(1 + 0.06\) rounds back to 1: adding less than \(u\) to 1 changes nothing.

Why 0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3, and log-sum-exp

Repeated doubling of 0.1 gives binary digits \(0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, \dots\): \(0.1 = (0.0\overline{0011})_2\) never terminates. The doubles nearest 0.1 and 0.2 are \(0.1000000000000000055\ldots\) and \(0.2000000000000000111\ldots\); their sum rounds to \(0.3000000000000000444\ldots\), while the double nearest 0.3 is \(0.2999999999999999888\ldots\). They differ in the last bit, so compare floats with a relative tolerance, never with \(=\).

Doubles overflow above about \(1.8 \times 10^{308}\) and underflow below about \(10^{-308}\). The log-sum-exp trick (L17), \(\log\sum_i e^{x_i} = m + \log\sum_i e^{x_i - m}\) with \(m = \max_i x_i\), keeps every exponent \(\le 0\) and one term equal to 1. For \(x = (1000, 1001)\), \(e^{1000}\) overflows but the trick gives \(1001 + \ln(1 + e^{-1}) \approx 1001.313\). Softmax is computed the same way.

Catastrophic cancellation

Subtracting two nearly equal numbers keeps their absolute errors but cancels their leading digits, so the relative error explodes.

Worked example · The quadratic formula in 4-digit arithmetic

Solve \(x^2 + 62.10x + 1 = 0\), rounding each result to 4 significant digits (true roots \(-0.01611\) and \(-62.08\)). \(b^2 = 3856.41 \to 3856\), \(b^2 - 4 = 3852\), \(\sqrt{3852} \to 62.06\). Then \(x_1 = (-62.10 + 62.06)/2 = -0.02000\): a 24% error, because two numbers agreeing in three of four digits leave one digit of information. Fix: rationalise, \(x_1 = \frac{-2c}{b + \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}} = \frac{-2}{124.2} = -0.01610\), which adds instead of subtracting.

Variance formulas. For 1000.1, 1000.2, 1000.3 in 6-digit arithmetic, the one-pass \(\sum x_i^2 - n\bar x^2\) gives \(3.00120 \times 10^6 - 3.00120 \times 10^6 = 0\) (other data can even give a negative variance), while the two-pass \(\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\) uses deviations \(-0.1, 0, 0.1\) and gets \(s^2 = 0.01\) exactly.

Conditioning of a problem, stability of an algorithm

Definition · Condition number, backward stability

The condition number of a problem bounds how much relative input error is amplified in the exact answer: \(\kappa_f(x) = |xf'(x)/f(x)|\) for a function, \(\kappa(A) = \|A\|\|A^{-1}\| = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\) for \(Ax = b\) (L58). An algorithm is backward stable if its output is the exact answer for an input perturbed by a relative \(O(u)\), the backward error. Then forward error \(\lesssim \kappa \times\) backward error.

Conditioning belongs to the problem, stability to the algorithm. Even a perfect algorithm loses about \(\log_{10}\kappa\) digits. The quadratic's small root is a well-conditioned problem attacked by an unstable algorithm; \(x - 1\) near \(x = 1\) is ill-conditioned (\(\kappa = |x/(x-1)|\)) and no algorithm rescues it. For linear systems, never form \(A^{-1}\): use LU with partial pivoting in general, Cholesky for symmetric positive definite matrices (half the work, no pivoting), and QR for least squares, which avoids the normal equations' \(\kappa(X^TX) = \kappa(X)^2\).

Numerical differentiation and the optimal step

By Taylor (L69), the forward difference \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f' + \frac h2 f''(\xi)\) is first order and the central difference \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h} = f' + \frac{h^2}6 f'''(\xi)\) is second order (today's Prove it). But each computed \(f\) value has relative error up to about \(\varepsilon\), and the difference quotient magnifies it to about \(\varepsilon|f|/h\). The best forward step is near \(\sqrt\varepsilon \approx 10^{-8}\) (error about \(10^{-8}\)); the best central step near \(\varepsilon^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}\) (error about \(10^{-11}\)). With one or two evaluations per coordinate and 5 to 10 correct digits, finite differences are for checking backpropagated gradients at a few points, never for training.

Root finding and fixed points

  • Bisection: for continuous \(f\) with \(f(a)f(b) < 0\), halve the bracket; after \(n\) steps the error is at most \((b - a)/2^{n+1}\). Guaranteed, linear: one bit per step.
  • Newton–Raphson: \(x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\), with \(e_{n+1} \approx \frac{f''}{2f'}e_n^2\) near a simple root. For \(\sqrt2\) from 1: \(1.5, 1.416667, 1.414216, 1.41421356237469\), the correct digits doubling. It fails when \(f' \approx 0\), can cycle (\(x^3 - 2x + 2\) from 0 goes \(0, 1, 0, \dots\)), diverges from bad starts, and is only linear at multiple roots.
  • Secant: \(f'\) replaced by the slope through the last two iterates; order \(\frac{1 + \sqrt5}2 \approx 1.618\), no derivative.
  • Fixed-point iteration \(x_{n+1} = g(x_n)\): if \(g\) maps an interval into itself with \(|g'| \le L < 1\), the fixed point is unique and \(|x_n - x^*| \le L^n|x_0 - x^*|\) (contraction mapping). \(x = \cos x\) converges to \(0.739085\). Newton is the case \(g'(x^*) = 0\), hence quadratic.

Numerical integration

The composite trapezoid rule \(T = h\big[\tfrac12 f_0 + f_1 + \cdots + f_{n-1} + \tfrac12 f_n\big]\) has error \(-\frac{(b-a)h^2}{12}f''(\xi)\). Simpson's rule (\(n\) even) \(S = \frac h3[f_0 + 4f_1 + 2f_2 + \cdots + 4f_{n-1} + f_n]\) has error \(-\frac{(b-a)h^4}{180}f^{(4)}(\xi)\) and is exact for cubics. Richardson extrapolation cancels the leading error term: \(T(h/2) + \frac{T(h/2) - T(h)}{3}\) is exactly Simpson, and iterating it is Romberg integration. Gaussian quadrature puts \(n\) nodes at Legendre roots and is exact up to degree \(2n - 1\).

Worked example · Trapezoid and Simpson against \(\int_0^1 e^x\,dx = e - 1 \approx 1.718282\)

\(h = 0.5\), \(e^{0.5} \approx 1.648721\). \(T = 0.25(1 + 2(1.648721) + 2.718282) \approx 1.753931\): error \(3.6 \times 10^{-2}\), inside the predicted \(\frac{h^2}{12}[\min f'', \max f''] = [0.021, 0.057]\). \(S = \frac{0.5}{3}(1 + 4(1.648721) + 2.718282) \approx 1.718861\): error \(5.8 \times 10^{-4}\), sixty times smaller from the same three values.

Common errors
  • Testing floats for equality.
  • Subtracting nearly equal quantities when a rearrangement exists (quadratic roots, one-pass variance, \(1 - \cos x\) for small \(x\)).
  • Forming \(A^{-1}\) or \(X^TX\) when a factorisation would do.
  • The smallest possible \(h\) in a finite difference; Newton without a bracket.

L121 · DAY 99

Analysis Touchstones & a Measure-Theoretic Preview

32m video40m practiceheavy lesson

The calculus and probability of earlier phases rest on a few facts about \(\R\) (completeness, compactness, uniform convergence) and on an integral that behaves well under limits. This lesson states them precisely, shows where the naive versions break, and previews the measure theory that makes discrete and continuous probability one subject. The goal is to make graduate texts readable, not to prove everything.

Completeness and sequences

Definition · Completeness, Cauchy sequences

Completeness axiom: every non-empty subset of \(\R\) that is bounded above has a least upper bound (supremum) in \(\R\). \(\Q\) fails it: \(\{q \in \Q : q^2 < 2\}\) has no rational supremum. A sequence is Cauchy if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N\) with \(|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon\) for all \(m, n \ge N\): its terms eventually crowd together, with no limit mentioned.

Theorem · Three consequences of completeness

Monotone convergence: a bounded monotone sequence converges. (If \(a_n \uparrow\) and \(s = \sup a_n\), some \(a_N > s - \varepsilon\), and then \(s - \varepsilon < a_N \le a_n \le s\) for all \(n \ge N\).) Bolzano–Weierstrass: every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence (every sequence has a monotone subsequence; apply monotone convergence). Cauchy criterion: in \(\R\) a sequence converges iff it is Cauchy. In \(\Q\) the decimal truncations \(1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, \dots\) of \(\sqrt2\) are Cauchy with no limit, which is exactly what completeness repairs.

Open, closed, compact

A set is open if each point has a ball around it inside the set, and closed if its complement is open, equivalently if it contains the limits of its convergent sequences. A set \(K \subseteq \R^n\) is compact if every sequence in \(K\) has a subsequence converging to a point of \(K\) (equivalently, every open cover has a finite subcover). Heine–Borel: in \(\R^n\), compact ⇔ closed and bounded.

Theorem · Extreme value theorem

A continuous \(f\) on a compact \(K\) attains its maximum and minimum. Proof idea: choose \(x_n \in K\) with \(f(x_n) \to \sup_K f\); compactness gives a subsequence converging to some \(x^* \in K\); continuity gives \(f(x^*) = \sup_K f\).

Each hypothesis is needed: \(f(x) = x\) on \((0, 1)\) (not closed) and on \(\R\) (not bounded) has no maximum, and \(1/x\) on \((0, 1]\) is unbounded. That is why existence theorems in optimisation (L81) ask for a compact feasible set or an objective that grows at infinity.

Pointwise versus uniform convergence

Definition · Two kinds of convergence for functions

\(f_n \to f\) pointwise on \(S\) if for every \(x\) and \(\varepsilon\) there is an \(N(x, \varepsilon)\) beyond which \(|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon\). It is uniform if one \(N(\varepsilon)\) works for every \(x\) at once, i.e. \(\sup_{x \in S}|f_n(x) - f(x)| \to 0\). The difference is the order of the quantifiers.

Worked example · \(f_n(x) = x^n\) on \([0, 1]\)

For \(0 \le x < 1\), \(x^n \to 0\); at \(x = 1\), \(x^n = 1\). So the pointwise limit is \(f = \mathbf 1_{\{1\}}\). But \(\sup_{[0,1]}|x^n - f(x)| = \sup_{[0,1)}x^n = 1\) for every \(n\): not uniform. Consequence: a pointwise limit of continuous functions can be discontinuous. (The integrals still behave here, \(\int_0^1 x^n = \frac1{n+1} \to 0 = \int_0^1 f\), because every \(x^n\) is bounded by 1; uniformity is sufficient for that, not necessary.) On \([0, a]\) with \(a < 1\) the convergence is uniform, since \(\sup = a^n \to 0\).

Theorem · What uniform convergence rescues

(i) A uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous (the \(\varepsilon/3\) argument). (ii) On \([a, b]\), \(\int f_n \to \int f\), since \(|\int(f_n - f)| \le (b - a)\sup|f_n - f|\). (iii) If \(f_n \to f\) pointwise and the derivatives \(f_n'\) converge uniformly to \(g\), then \(f' = g\). Weierstrass M-test: if \(|f_n(x)| \le M_n\) for all \(x\) and \(\sum M_n < \infty\), then \(\sum f_n\) converges uniformly. So \(\sum \sin(nx)/n^2\) is continuous on \(\R\), and power series may be differentiated term by term inside their interval of convergence (L69).

Without uniformity, integrals can fail: \(f_n = n\,\mathbf 1_{(0, 1/n)}\) tends to 0 at every point, yet \(\int_0^1 f_n = 1\) for all \(n\).

Why the Riemann integral is not good enough

The indicator of the rationals \(\mathbf 1_\Q\) on \([0,1]\) has every upper sum equal to 1 and every lower sum 0 (each subinterval contains both rationals and irrationals), so it is not Riemann integrable. Worse, limits escape the class: list the rationals as \(q_1, q_2, \dots\) and let \(f_n\) be 1 on \(\{q_1, \dots, q_n\}\) and 0 elsewhere. Each \(f_n\) is Riemann integrable with integral 0, and \(f_n \uparrow \mathbf 1_\Q\), which is not.

Measure theory in outline

A measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \mu)\) is a σ-algebra (L96) with a countably additive \(\mu \ge 0\). Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\) on the Borel sets of \(\R\) extends length: \(\lambda([a, b]) = b - a\). A function is measurable if every \(\{f \le t\}\) is in \(\mathcal F\). The Lebesgue integral starts from simple functions, \(\int\sum_i c_i\mathbf 1_{A_i}\,d\mu = \sum_i c_i\,\mu(A_i)\), extends to \(f \ge 0\) by \(\int f\,d\mu = \sup\{\int s : 0 \le s \le f,\ s \text{ simple}\}\), and to general \(f\) via \(f = f^+ - f^-\). Every Riemann-integrable function on \([a,b]\) is Lebesgue integrable with the same integral.

Worked example · The rational indicator is Lebesgue integrable

\(\Q \cap [0,1]\) is countable: cover \(q_k\) by an interval of length \(\varepsilon/2^k\), total length at most \(\varepsilon\), for any \(\varepsilon\). So \(\lambda(\Q \cap [0,1]) = 0\). Then \(\mathbf 1_\Q\) is itself a simple function, and \(\int_{[0,1]}\mathbf 1_\Q\,d\lambda = 1 \cdot \lambda(\Q \cap [0,1]) + 0 \cdot \lambda([0,1] \setminus \Q) = 0\).

Theorem · The payoff: limit theorems for integrals
  • Monotone convergence: if \(0 \le f_n \uparrow f\), then \(\int f_n \to \int f\).
  • Dominated convergence: if \(f_n \to f\) pointwise (almost everywhere) and \(|f_n| \le g\) with \(\int g < \infty\), then \(\int f_n \to \int f\). It covers \(x^n\) (dominated by 1) and correctly refuses \(n\,\mathbf 1_{(0,1/n)}\) (no integrable dominating \(g\) exists).
  • Fubini–Tonelli: for \(f \ge 0\), or for \(\iint|f| < \infty\), the double integral equals both iterated integrals.

Probability, restated

A probability space is a measure space with \(P(\Omega) = 1\). A random variable is a measurable function \(X : \Omega \to \R\), which is exactly the condition “\(\{X \le x\}\) is an event” of L98. Its law is \(P_X(B) = P(X \in B)\), and \(\E X = \int_\Omega X\,dP\): one definition for discrete, continuous and mixed variables. If \(P_X(B) = 0\) whenever \(\lambda(B) = 0\), the Radon–Nikodym theorem gives a density \(f = dP_X/d\lambda\) with \(P_X(B) = \int_B f\,d\lambda\). A pmf is the same object taken with respect to counting measure, so \(\sum_x g(x)p(x)\) and \(\int g(x)f(x)\,dx\) are both \(\int g\,dP_X\), and a mixed law (L100) has a density with respect to \(\lambda\) plus counting measure on its atoms. Dominated convergence is what licensed swapping derivative and integral for the score (L111) and for MGFs (L102).

Reading on: Abbott, Understanding Analysis, then Rudin, Principles of Mathematical Analysis; for probability, Rosenthal, A First Look at Rigorous Probability Theory, Williams, Probability with Martingales, and Billingsley, Probability and Measure.

Common errors
  • Swapping a limit and an integral (or a derivative and an integral) without a uniform or dominated bound.
  • Assuming pointwise limits of continuous functions are continuous.
  • “Closed, so compact.” \(\R\) is closed and not compact; Heine–Borel also needs bounded.
  • Measure zero taken for empty. The rationals are dense and have Lebesgue measure 0.

Practice · 40 min

  1. Prove it. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, and every Cauchy sequence is bounded. Hint: an \(\varepsilon/2\) argument for the first; take \(\varepsilon = 1\) for the second.
    Proof sketch

    If \(a_n \to L\), pick \(N\) with \(|a_n - L| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(n \ge N\); then \(|a_m - a_n| \le |a_m - L| + |L - a_n| < \varepsilon\). If \((a_n)\) is Cauchy, pick \(N\) for \(\varepsilon = 1\): for \(n \ge N\), \(|a_n| \le |a_N| + 1\). So \(|a_n| \le \max(|a_1|, \dots, |a_{N-1}|, |a_N| + 1)\) for all \(n\).

  2. Show \(f_n(x) = x/n\) converges uniformly on \([0, 1]\) but not on \(\R\).
    Answer

    On \([0,1]\), \(\sup|x/n| = 1/n \to 0\). On \(\R\), \(\sup|x/n| = \infty\) for every \(n\).

  3. Does \(\sum_n x^n/n^2\) converge uniformly on \([-1, 1]\)?
    Answer

    Yes, by the M-test with \(M_n = 1/n^2\); the sum is continuous there.

  4. Use dominated convergence to find \(\lim_n\int_0^1(1 + x/n)^n\,dx\).
    Answer

    \((1 + x/n)^n \to e^x\) and \((1 + x/n)^n \le e^x \le e\), so the limit is \(\int_0^1 e^x\,dx = e - 1\).

  5. Show every countable subset of \(\R\) has Lebesgue measure 0. Is the converse true?
    Answer

    Cover the \(k\)-th point by an interval of length \(\varepsilon/2^k\). No: the Cantor set is uncountable with measure 0.
